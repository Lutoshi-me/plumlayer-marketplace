# Waterproofing

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the waterproofing
package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets.
The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real waterproofers can bid it and a real estimator can
level it.

One warning before anything else. This trade does not have a single shape. It runs in two regimes
that share a division number and share almost nothing else, and it contains a supply sub-trade that
behaves like a third. Establish which regime you are in before you write a single scope line.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

### The three regimes

New-construction envelope work is the first regime. The package is below-grade membrane, elevator
pit waterproofing, plaza and amenity deck membrane, balcony membrane, traffic coating, the air and
vapor barrier, and joint sealants. It is bid by envelope specialists, and its grain is the building
detail.

Restoration and remedial work is the second. On an existing building the same division number buys
repointing, brick and stone replacement, crack repair, cleaning, through-wall flashing replacement,
crystalline treatment of existing vaults and pits, and sealant replacement. It is bid by restoration
contractors, most of whom submit one proposal covering both the masonry division and the
waterproofing division at once, and its grain is an action applied to a surface, with a quantity and
often a minimum billable size per repair. When that happens the general contractor splits the single
proposal across two evaluation tabs, and where the split falls is a judgment call that moves between
rounds. The same bidder can appear three times in one ranking at three different numbers off one
document for exactly this reason.

Expansion joint systems are the third. They are a manufactured product sold by model designation, and
the market bids them either as material furnished only through a manufacturer's representative or as
furnish and install by a specialty installer. Those two are not comparable numbers and are routinely
ranked against each other anyway.

### Sections

The anchor sections a proposal will name are 071100 for bituminous damproofing, 071300 for
post-applied sheet waterproofing with a companion pre-applied section, 071326 where the specification
writes self-adhering sheet as one section, 071400 or 071413 for hot fluid-applied rubberized asphalt,
071416 for cold fluid-applied, 071610 or 071616 for crystalline, 071810 for penetrating traffic
sealer, 072700 or 072726 for air barriers, 079200 and 079213 for interior and exterior joint sealants,
and 079500 with its interior, exterior and manufactured-roof subsections for expansion control.
Restoration adds 040120 for masonry restoration and cleaning.

A bidder that cites sections tends to cite several and to price them as separate numbers, which is the
single most useful proposal habit in this trade. Section citation is far more common here than in the
finish trades, because the section boundary is also the product boundary and the product boundary is
also the crew boundary.

### How the package gets drawn

A general contractor commonly names the package "dampproofing and waterproofing", and on projects with
significant framed wall area it names it "dampproofing, waterproofing and air vapor barrier". That
naming decision is the whole game, because the barrier is usually the largest single number in the
package and it is the one most likely to be bought from somebody who is not a waterproofer at all.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another section:

- Joint sealants, interior and exterior. Almost always inside the package when a full-package
  waterproofer bids it. The broadest bidders reach all the way to plumbing fixture caulking and
  countertop-to-wall joints.
- The air and vapor barrier, when a specialist carries it. See section 5, because it splits by wall
  type more often than it stays whole.
- Traffic coatings at garage entries, speed ramps and loading docks, and penetrating sealers at
  parking decks. Commonly carried by whoever carries the deck membrane.
- Pedestal pavers and the sloped rigid insulation above a plaza or terrace membrane. Some bidders
  carry the whole assembly up through the walking surface, and when they do the paver line can rival
  the membrane line. Others stop at the membrane.
- Expansion joints, when a whole-envelope bidder carries them as a work family rather than letting
  them go to a manufacturer.
- Firestopping. Occasionally carried by a waterproofing bidder, at a figure that can equal the whole
  below-grade scope, while the general contractor keeps it in its own package. This one inflates a
  comparison badly if you do not catch it.
- Mold-prevention or wood-preservative coating on framing lumber. It arrives inside barrier
  proposals because the same companies sell both, and it competes against insulation contractors
  rather than against waterproofers.
- Interior air sealing by aerosolized sealant. A separate specialty entirely, but it lands on this
  scope sheet because nobody else claims it.
- Concrete repair on renovation work, priced as partial-depth and full-depth areas.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like waterproofing:

- Membrane systems requiring installer certification by the manufacturer. This is the sharpest carve
  in the trade and it has nothing to do with capability or price. See section 6.
- Sheet metal flashing, through-wall flashing, counter flashing, coping and back dams. Excluded by
  essentially every barrier and membrane bidder seen, in near-identical language, and it is a real
  gap if no sheet metal package carries it.
- The roof membrane proper, and the flashing where the roof meets the parapet.
- Expansion joints at a firewall and at the roof, which route to the drywall contractor and the
  roofer respectively on the same project the expansion joint package exists.
- Air barrier over wood and gypsum sheathing, where a panelized framer carries it.
- Air barrier commissioning: third-party testing, air leakage testing and infrared imaging are
  excluded by every barrier bidder seen, in language that is close to identical.
- Winter conditions, tenting and heating. Excluded by every bidder in the corpus without exception.

## 2. How the market bids

### Proposal structure

Waterproofing is the opposite of a lump-sum trade. The default output is a short list of work-family
numbers, usually between three and six, sometimes as many as eleven. A single undifferentiated number
is a minority behavior and comes almost entirely from restoration contractors and from the broadest
whole-envelope bidders.

- Segmented by work family, which usually means by product system: the majority, on every project.
  The families are stable enough to be predictable, and they are below grade, elevator pits, deck or
  plaza membrane, balconies, traffic coating, air barrier, and sealants.
- Segmented by specification section, which is the same segmentation with the section numbers
  written down: common, and the mark of the more capable bidders.
- One lump sum over a bullet list: a minority, and the bullets often carry sheet references even
  when the price does not decompose.
- A true unit-price schedule with quantity, unit, unit price and extension: seen almost only in
  restoration, where it is the norm rather than the exception.

Quantities are stated more often here than in the finish trades but far from universally. When a
bidder states quantities it usually states area for the below-grade and deck families and length for
the sealant family, and it usually states them for some families and not others in the same document.
Treat a stated quantity as a genuine finding, not as a routine field.

There is no total on many of these documents. A proposal that presents four independently priced
sections and stops is common, and the estimator sums them. When there is a total, treat it with
suspicion: totals have been seen rolling in an entire window supply package, an unrelated framing
package, and a wood-treatment product, so a printed total in this trade is frequently not a
waterproofing number. Figures and written-out words on the same total have been seen disagreeing, and
where they do, the words reconcile against the priced lines and the figure does not.

### What a proposal typically will state

- The product system by manufacturer and model, per work family. Nearly always. This is the most
  reliably present fact in the trade, and it matters because thickness, permeability and fire
  classification are properties of the named product and are what the specification actually
  regulates.
- The basis documents by date, and often by named sheet and detail. Common, and much more common than
  in the finish trades.
- Which layers of the assembly are included: membrane, protection board, drainage mat or board,
  rigid insulation at a stated thickness. Nearly always, because the layers are commercially
  detachable and the bidder knows it.
- Labor type, union or open shop. Stated by some, and recorded by an estimator for every bidder
  regardless.
- Temperature and weather conditions required for application. Commonly, in the assumptions block.
- Whether prevailing wage applies, usually as a negative.

### What a proposal typically will not state

- The side of the wall the membrane goes on. Positive-side and blindside are wholly different systems
  at wholly different prices, and one proposal in the corpus names the side explicitly. Everyone else
  leaves it to the detail reference, or to nothing.
- Warranty duration and warranty type. Rarely stated by a bidder, though a general contractor may
  carry a warranty term as its own leveled line with a shorter term as a deduct alternate.
- Who owns flood testing or water testing. One bidder in the corpus states it, and states it as the
  general contractor's obligation and a precondition to starting.
- Bond and insurance cost, which are near-universally excluded and quoted only on request.
- Any markup or overhead structure. One proposal in the corpus prints a profit line, along with
  separate mobilization and lift lines, which makes it the only document that shows how the number
  was built.

### Revision habits

Revisions in this trade are frequent, and their mechanisms are more varied than in any finish trade.
The ones to expect:

- The negotiation ladder. Every line falls, by different amounts, with no scope text change and no
  stated reason. Movement of a tenth to a third per line is normal. A leveler working from an earlier
  round overstates by up to a third.
- The uniform de-escalation re-issue. Every line falls by very nearly the same small fraction, and
  the scope text is word for word identical. Where one line moves the other way it is usually the
  mock-up, which suggests the concession was taken out of production work and the pre-construction
  obligation repriced honestly.
- The commercial-basis restatement. A full re-issue one day after the prior round, with every base
  figure identical, existing solely to add an owner-controlled insurance adder per work family. No
  scope, no money, pure basis.
- The breakout restructure. The base bid, the inclusions, the exclusions and the qualifications are
  all identical and two cost breakouts appear inside them, because the general contractor asked for
  them so it could procure those blocks on a different schedule. Zero money moves and it is the
  highest-information revision in the corpus.
- The scope-sheet answer. An item excluded in an earlier round as pending the general contractor's
  scope sheet comes back priced. The stale exclusion frequently survives in the same document, so
  the proposal contradicts itself. The priced breakout is authoritative.
- Scope departure. Add alternates that existed in an earlier round vanish entirely rather than being
  folded in or deducted, and the headline number falls by more than half. That is not a saving.
- Scope entry. A bidder answers a question about square footage by adding an entire adjacent trade
  to its proposal, tripling the total.
- The unit-of-measure restatement. The rarest and the most dangerous: a row keeps its quantity number
  and changes its unit, so an area becomes a count. The total moves a few percent and looks like a
  small concession. Always diff the unit column, not just the extension.
- The drawing-round retake. Genuine quantity revision driven by a new drawing issue, common in the
  expansion joint sub-trade because the supplier takes off from the drawings, and rare everywhere
  else.

Two consequences. First, a revision here is usually a negotiation or restructuring artifact, not a
document-change artifact, and the exception is the supply sub-trade. Second, order rounds by the
basis document the proposal cites, never by the date on the letterhead. Letterhead dates have been
seen a full year off, carried over from a template, so a proposal that says it prices a set can appear
to predate that set.

### Files lie

Open the file. In this trade specifically: the same PDF is filed under two different trade tokens
because it carries two product scopes, so counting folder entries double-counts bidders. A file named
for one company contains a proposal from a different company entirely, so what looks like two bidders
is one bidder's two rounds. A file whose name contains both barrier and flashing is a metal panel
material quote with no membrane in it, because flashing means sheet metal trim to one half of the
market and membrane to the other. A proposal in a project folder is addressed to all bidders rather
than to the general contractor, because it is a supplier's circulated quote. A file labeled as a
later revision is byte-identical to a much earlier one sitting in a different set folder. Hash the
files, then read them.

Render before reading. Image-only scans, tables whose row-to-amount binding vanishes in text
extraction, and character-shifted fonts that extract as gibberish are all normal here. Two of the most
information-dense documents in the corpus extract as fewer than a hundred characters across four
pages.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets in this trade have run about thirty-five to ninety rows. Target roughly forty to seventy
lines for a full new-construction package, more if the project has balconies or is a renovation.

The organizing axis is not area type, the way it is in the finish trades. It is the referenced detail
or the referenced wall, floor and roof assembly. Estimator sheets key their rows to sheet-and-detail
numbers or to assembly codes, and the strongest bidder proposals do exactly the same thing, decomposing
each priced section into lettered conditions that each name a detail and often a dimension. When the
grain of the scope list matches the grain of the details, the leveling works. When it does not, every
comparison becomes a negotiation.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every work family, always: below grade, elevator pits, deck and plaza membrane, balconies, traffic
  coating, air and vapor barrier, interior sealants, exterior sealants, expansion joints. These are
  the crew boundaries, the product boundaries and the buyout boundaries all at once.
- Within below grade, the application method: pre-applied against a support of excavation or an
  existing structure, pre-applied under slabs and mat slabs, post-applied on formed walls, and
  damproofing on frost walls. These are different products, different sequences and different rates,
  and the same wall gets different ones depending on whether it can be reached from outside.
- Every layer of every horizontal and below-grade assembly, separately: membrane, protection board,
  drainage mat or drainage board, rigid insulation at a stated thickness and rated compressive
  strength, and the wearing surface above it. Bidders offer these layers as detachable alternates, so
  a line that says "waterproofing system" invites two bidders to price two different assemblies. This
  is the single most important enumeration rule in the trade.
- The transition from below grade to above grade, as its own dimensioned line. One bidder in the
  corpus prices it as a named strip of stated height; everyone else either assumes it or is silent
  about it, and the barrier specialists mark it in their own documents as assumed. A seam that the
  market itself flags as assumed on both sides is a scope gap by definition.
- The air and vapor barrier split by wall type, not as one line. See section 5.
- Every barrier transition condition separately: inside and outside corners, floor to exterior wall,
  wall to low roof, slab on grade to wall, wall to parapet top, base of wall to waterproofing
  membrane, courtyard or setback to wall, and head, jamb and sill of openings. The better barrier
  proposals enumerate exactly these, and the exclusion lists enumerate the transitions over
  through-wall flashing, metal flashing, counter flashing, rain-screen track, window strap anchors,
  brick shelf angle and penetrations set in sealant. Those exclusions are a scope list somebody has
  to carry.
- Elevator pits by pit, with the car configuration stated. Pit counts and single-versus-double
  configuration change between drawing rounds and bidders track the change, so a line reading
  "elevator pit waterproofing" without a count is uncomparable. State also whether the pit gets a
  sheet system from outside or a crystalline treatment from inside; those differ by several times.
- Sump pits, sand interceptors, silicone pits and utility vaults, each on their own line. They are
  routinely missing from the drawings' waterproofing callouts and routinely asked about by estimators.
- Balconies, with a count. On a wood-framed residential building the balcony membrane can be more
  than half the whole waterproofing number, which inverts the intuition that below-grade work
  dominates. If the set does not make the balcony count unambiguous, the package is unbiddable.
- Deck and plaza membrane by deck, with area, and with leak-detection testing as its own line.
- Traffic coating by location: garage entry, speed ramp, loading dock, parking deck.
- Sealants by joint condition, not as one line: exterior window and door perimeters, storefront and
  curtainwall perimeters, louver perimeters, interior window and door perimeters, masonry control
  joints, brick-to-panel and brick-to-siding control joints, dissimilar material joints, site concrete
  to foundation, and the interior wet-area joints at plumbing fixtures and countertops. Bidders split
  cleanly on which of these they carry and no two draw the line in the same place.
- Interior sealants separately from exterior sealants, always. They are separate specification
  sections, they are frequently sequenced by floor rather than by trade, and a proposal that carries
  one and not the other is common.
- Expansion joints by location family and by joint size: interior floor, interior wall, interior
  ceiling, exterior wall, roof, and vehicular. Each size and each location is a different model at a
  different rate, and the fire barrier behind the cover runs at several times the length of the cover
  it serves because the barrier is continuous where the cover is not.
- Mock-up, as its own line. Bidders price it and it is small enough to disappear.
- Testing: electronic leak detection or vector mapping on decks, flood or water testing, and
  third-party air barrier testing. Three separate lines with three different owners.
- Access, as its own line or block. See section 6. On restoration work it can be a quarter of the
  base bid.
- On renovation, the restoration block enumerates by action against surface: repoint, remove and
  replace, rebuild, rout and seal, patch with repair mortar, coat, clean, reanchor. Action and surface
  are orthogonal, and a line naming only one is unbiddable. The same joint appears twice under two
  actions with two different quantities in a well-written proposal.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Primer, adhesive, mastic, termination bar and seam treatment collapse into the membrane line. They
  are attributes of the named system.
- Individual foundation wall segments collapse into pre-applied and post-applied groups. Bidders
  locate scope by column-line range within one priced line rather than pricing walls separately.
- Coat count within a crystalline application collapses to the crystalline line, with one exception:
  where the specification calls for a concentrate coat and a modified coat, both areas are stated,
  because the same area is treated twice.
- Repeated identical balconies collapse into a balcony count times a system. Do not price balconies
  individually.
- Individual penetrations, boots and pipe seals collapse into the assembly line they sit in, except
  where a bidder excludes comebacks for below-grade penetrations, which several do, and then the
  comeback becomes its own line.
- Sheet metal accessories never belong on these lines at all. They are excluded so uniformly that
  putting them here just guarantees a plug.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In this trade
the test fires hardest on assembly layers, on the barrier's wall-type boundary, and on access, which
is why those get lines even when the drawings show them as one hatch pattern in one detail.

There is a fourth outcome here that the finish trades do not have, and it breaks the test unless you
plan for it. A bidder can list a scope item, price nothing against it, and exclude nothing, because it
is not certified to install the specified product. That reads as included to anyone skimming, it
appears in the numbered scope list, and it is a hole. Any scope line whose specification names a
certified-installer requirement needs an explicit carrier, not an assumption.

The related pattern is the item that is priced inside one line and excluded in the printed exclusion
list of the same document. The priced breakout wins. Exclusion lists in this trade are pasted
templates and repeat bidders reproduce them verbatim across unrelated projects, so never level off an
exclusion list alone.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs an estimator adds to every bidder before comparing, because the market
systematically will not carry them:

- Winter conditions, tenting and heating. Excluded by every bidder without exception.
- Access: staging, swing staging, mast climbers, boom and scissor lifts, and hoisting. Carried by
  some, excluded by others, and priced as duration rather than as area when it is carried.
- Sheet metal flashing, through-wall flashing, counter flashing and metal back dams at sills.
- Interior air sealing.
- Third-party testing of any kind, including air leakage testing, infrared imaging and fire-rating
  inspection of expansion joints.
- Substrate preparation: form-tie removal, snap-tie removal, concrete cleaning, patching, parging and
  grinding, and repair of honeycombing. Excluded near-universally, and it is real work on every
  cast-in-place foundation.
- Dewatering, and water management generally.
- Damproofing at inner frost walls, which is small, easy to miss on the drawings, and routinely
  plugged.
- Line striping at the garage, where a traffic coating exists.
- Bonds, insurance riders and additional-insured endorsements.
- Mock-up.

A scope line set that names all of these explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging for it.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the trade that must pick the work up, and if
no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| Excluded by the waterproofer | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Winter conditions, tenting, temporary heat (every bidder seen) | General contractor |
| Sheet metal flashing, through-wall flashing, counter flashing, coping (nearly every membrane and barrier bidder) | Sheet metal or roofing, frequently unassigned |
| Metal back dams at window sill openings (barrier bidders) | Frequently unassigned |
| Preformed silicone extrusions and sheets, silicone transition sheet (commonly) | Frequently unassigned |
| Third-party testing, air leakage testing, infrared imaging (every barrier bidder seen) | Owner or general contractor, via a commissioning agent |
| Concrete repair, crack repair, parging, patching, grinding, honeycomb repair (commonly) | Concrete contractor |
| Form-tie and snap-tie removal, concrete cleaning at foundations (commonly) | Concrete contractor |

### The exclusion list as a documents-defect report

This is the highest-value paragraph in the trade. In detail-driven waterproofing work a large share
of exclusions carry a stated reason, and the reason is that the documents are broken. Exclusions have
been seen reading that a specification section is not mentioned in the wall assembly sheet, that a
scope item has no specification section at all, that a bidder was unable to locate the conditions
shown on a named detail, that the extent of work in three named structural notes is unknown, that no
detail or gap is shown for a cover the specification requires, that a bidder found none of a scope
item that the drawings call for removing, and that a joint movement class is nowhere indicated so a
standard was assumed.

Each of those is an RFI written for you by the market, with the sheet number attached. Capture the
reason, route it upstream, and do not absorb it as a leveling adjustment. The pattern is strong
enough that when a set produces no reasoned exclusions in this trade, that itself is a signal that
nobody read the details.

The same list runs in the other direction, and both directions occur on one project. A bidder can
price an item inside a line and exclude it three pages later on the ground that no specification
section exists for it, while the estimator's sheet routes the same item to a different trade
entirely, while a second bidder answers no to it. Three positions, two documents, one item.

### Contract terms that are really scope

Waterproofing proposals are unusually often executable contracts. Several in the corpus carry
signature blocks, initial-here option checkboxes, and language stating that signing makes the
document binding. The terms attached materially change what the number is worth:

- Deposits and mobilization money. A deposit of a tenth of the contract price before mobilization,
  or a fixed six-figure sum due at project start, appears in this trade and appears nowhere in the
  finish trades in the corpus.
- Remobilization charges. A stated flat fee per additional mobilization, chargeable for incomplete
  work by other trades, failed inspections, or obstructed access. That converts a sequencing problem
  into a priced change order automatically.
- Escalation triggers. A right to reprice if raw material costs move beyond a stated percentage, and
  a right to adjust for inflation, duties and tariffs at any time.
- Tariff carve-outs. Excluded from the base and treated as an additional expense if they occur,
  sometimes with a stated notification and mitigation obligation.
- Pay-when-paid. One bidder states expressly that its contract is not subject to pay-when-paid or
  paid-if-paid, which is a term the general contractor's own subcontract will contradict.
- Validity windows. Thirty, forty-five or sixty days, and material prices held for as little as seven
  days.
- Quantity caps. An exclusion of footage beyond the quantities stated in the inclusions converts a
  lump sum into a capped quantity instrument. Two bidders can state the same quantities and only one
  of them be capped, in which case they are not the same kind of number.
- Takeoff-validation disclaimers. A supplier stating that the customer is ultimately responsible for
  validating scope and that price adjusts proportionally if quantities deviate. That moves the
  takeoff risk onto the buyer, which is the opposite of how a lump sum behaves.
- Minimum billable units on restoration work. A unit price with a stated minimum area per repair is a
  different instrument from a unit price, and stated areas systematically understate scattered work.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

- Substrate condition. Footings broom-swept clean, foundation surfaces sound and free of voids,
  spalled areas, loose aggregate and sharp protrusions. Stated commonly, and the same sentence often
  appears under two separate specification sections in one proposal.
- No active water infiltration during installation. A pricing assumption on pit and below-grade work,
  and one that fails often in the field.
- Temperature and weather windows. A stated minimum substrate and ambient temperature, rising, and
  for crystalline products a requirement that conditions hold for a full day after application. See
  section 6.
- Award conditions. Pricing contingent on awarding all referenced scopes, with a reserved right to
  reprice if only some are selected. Stated by more than one full-package bidder, and it invalidates
  per-section ranking, which a qualifications field cannot express.
- Access assumptions. All work reachable by aerial lift, lifts stored on site, boom lift placeable at
  every exterior joint location, a crane available to place a lift in an enclosed courtyard.
- Interior caulking sequenced by floor rather than by trade.
- Mobilization counts, sometimes stated as one per level.
- Butt joints assumed, and blockout repair dimensions assumed at stated widths and depths, on
  expansion joint work.
- Labor basis: union, open shop, prevailing wage or not, and on one restoration proposal a stated
  market-recovery credit for journeyman labor, which is neither scope nor alternate.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This trade is almost entirely seams. It is the layer between every other layer, so nearly every line
it prices touches somebody else's work.

### The air and vapor barrier: the seam that matters most

The barrier is usually the largest number in the package and it is the least stable in ownership.
On a single project it can split three ways, and it has:

1. A barrier specialist carrying masonry and concrete walls only, explicitly excluding the barrier
   over wood and gypsum sheathing.
2. A panelized framer carrying the barrier within exterior wood walls above the podium only, as a
   priced line inside its framing package.
3. A sealant applicator carrying all exterior wall assemblies with no wall-type qualifier at all.

Three numbers on one project, none of them comparable, and none of them saying so on the cover. Two
framers bidding the same wood-wall-only boundary are comparable to each other and to nothing else.
Before you compare any barrier number, establish the wall-type denominator. Before you write a
barrier scope line, split it by wall type and say which detail governs each.

The barrier specialist's vertical boundary is remarkably consistent across companies and projects: it
installs nothing below the top of the foundation and nothing up onto a roof deck, and it wraps into
openings but does not seal, flash or caulk windows and doors after they are set. That leaves three
seams:

- The base of the exterior wall to the below-grade waterproofing membrane. Barrier specialists mark
  this transition in their own documents as assumed. The below-grade waterproofer normally stops at
  the top of the foundation wall. One waterproofer in the corpus prices the strip explicitly, with a
  height. Everyone else leaves it in the gap between two packages.
- The top of the wall to the roof. The roofer's membrane and the barrier meet somewhere at the
  parapet, and both sides exclude the sheet metal, coping and through-wall flashing that make the
  joint work.
- The window and door perimeter after installation. The barrier wraps in, the window installer sets
  the unit, and the perimeter sealant belongs to whoever the general contractor says it belongs to.
  Framers exclude exterior caulking and sealants explicitly; barrier specialists exclude joint
  sealants not within the barrier system; the sealant applicator prices the perimeter as length.

Sill pan flashing deserves its own mention because it is disclaimed by name from both sides. Framers
exclude it and state that it travels with the door rather than with the barrier; barrier specialists
exclude metal back dams at sill openings. If the door and window package does not carry it, nobody
does.

### Below grade and the support of excavation

Blindside work is a different scope from positive-side work, and the drawings distinguish them by
detail rather than by note. Pre-applied membrane goes against a support of excavation, against an
existing adjacent building, under thickened slabs at cantilever walls, and under mat slabs. Post-applied
goes on formed walls that can be reached from outside. Damproofing goes on frost walls. A scope line
that says "foundation waterproofing" without naming the method has priced nothing.

The support of excavation is a separate package with its own contractor, and the interface is a real
gap. A full-package waterproofer will run its pre-applied membrane up against the support of
excavation per a named detail and in the same document exclude detailing at the support of
excavation's form-tie penetrations. Those penetrations are the leak path.

Two more below-grade seams:

- Mud slab and blinding. Pre-applied membrane under a mat slab needs a surface to be laid on, and
  whether that is a mud slab, and whose it is, does not appear in any proposal in the corpus.
- Early backfill. A general contractor commonly needs the footing waterproofed before the wall is
  ready so the site contractor can backfill to footing level. Waterproofers exclude that separate
  mobilization by name. It is a sequencing cost that is nobody's until it is somebody's.

### Decks, plazas and the roofing boundary

The amenity deck or plaza over occupied space is contested territory. It is bought as a waterproofing
scope, not a roofing scope, in this corpus, and the estimator's sheet carries it on the waterproofing
tab while the roofing package's own knowledge records it as a waterproofer buy. Expect both trades to
price it and expect the numbers to diverge sharply. Two waterproofers on one deck have disagreed
modestly on the area and enormously on the rate per unit area, which means the divergence is in the
assembly they each assumed, not in the takeoff.

The assembly above the membrane is where the money hides: protection mat, drainage mat, rigid
insulation at a stated compressive strength, sloped insulation, and then either pedestal pavers, a
green roof buildup, or a topping slab. Some bidders carry the whole stack through the walking surface
and some stop at the membrane. On one project the paver-on-pedestal line was larger than the membrane
line beneath it. Enumerate the layers.

At the parapet the deck membrane meets the roof membrane and both trades exclude the metal. On a
firewall at the roof, the expansion joint cover and the fire barrier below it are excluded by the
expansion joint supplier and routed to the roofer.

### Balconies

On wood-framed residential work, balconies are the largest block in the package and they are usually
a proprietary system. That system has a component list, and the waterproofer takes some components
and not others: it will carry the membrane, primer, detail membrane, seam sealant, corner treatment,
spacer shims, drainage mat and flashing tape, and exclude the railing embed supports, the flashings
and door pans, and the proprietary transition pieces. Those excluded components are metal and they go
to somebody who has not been told. Enumerate the system components, not the system.

Balcony membrane can also be the item nobody can install, because the specification names a product
that requires manufacturer certification. See section 6.

### Interior wet areas

Shower pans and wet-area membranes sit on a genuine three-way split with the tile setter and the
waterproofer, and this corpus is thin on it. What the corpus does show is that interior sealants
reach further into the building than you would expect: the broadest bidders carry caulking at plumbing
fixtures, at countertop-to-wall joints, at interior door frames and at stair-to-wall joints. Those are
items the painting package also claims, and items the tile package also claims, and the boundary is
drawn differently by every bidder. Beware of the same joint being claimed by three trades and carried
by none.

### Expansion joints

Expansion joints route three ways on a single project: to the expansion joint package, to the drywall
contractor at a typical firewall, and to the roofer at the garage and at the firewall roof condition.
An expansion joint scope line that does not name its routing is unbiddable.

Within the package itself, the supply and install split is the trap. A material-furnished-only quote
from a manufacturer through a representative and a furnish-and-install quote from a specialty
installer have been ranked adjacent on a leveling sheet, with the material-only number roughly twice
the furnish-and-install number per unit, because the two takeoffs were different. The leveling sheet
recorded "supplier" in its labor-type column, which is the sheet acknowledging that one of the bidders
sells no labor at all, but no adjustment followed.

Manufacturer quotes also disclose deviations that the drawings will never surface: standard product
in lieu of the specified and detailed types, a blanket fire barrier in lieu of fire-rated foam on the
ground that the foam is not listed above a stated joint width, standard movement class because the
documents indicate none, standard finishes and gasket colors, and field-fabricated transitions in place
of factory-fabricated ones. Read the qualifying notes; they are the real scope.

### Other seams

- Crystalline and negative-side work at elevator pits. State whether the pit is treated from outside
  with a sheet system before the slab or from inside with a crystalline treatment after. The two
  differ by several times and both appear as "elevator pit waterproofing". Elevator pit work in an
  active shaft requires a licensed elevator technician present by law in some jurisdictions, and
  removal of any mechanicals is the client's cost, both of which appear in bidder assumptions.
  Hydraulic jack waterproofing is excluded by name.
- Firestopping. Sometimes inside a waterproofing proposal, usually its own package. When it is inside,
  it can equal the entire below-grade scope. Its own seams are putty pads at electrical boxes, which
  go to electrical, and coring and sleeving, which go to the trade making the hole.
- Insulation. Rigid board at foundations and decks sits inside the waterproofing assembly and is
  offered as a detachable alternate. Spray foam at a garage ceiling is sold by barrier specialists in
  lieu of sprayed fibrous insulation, which puts a waterproofing bidder in competition with the
  insulation package.
- Masonry. On restoration work the same contractor does both, and the boundary is where the veneer
  stops and the backup wall begins. One restoration bidder states explicitly that there is no barrier
  and no waterproofing on the backup wall and that the rebuilt veneer will be tied back with helical
  ties. A rebuilt veneer over an untreated backup wall is a decision somebody made; make sure it was
  made deliberately.
- Concrete. Substrate preparation, honeycomb repair, form-tie removal and snap-tie removal are
  excluded by nearly every waterproofer and are prerequisites to every membrane.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

### Certification is a scope gate

This is the finding that has no analogue in the finish trades. A specification can require that a
membrane be installed by a contractor certified by its manufacturer. When it does, an otherwise
capable bidder will list the scope item in its numbered scope list, price nothing against it, and
exclude nothing, stating only that the specification calls for a certified installer and that it is
not certified. The item reads as covered to anyone skimming the proposal and is a hole.

Certification is invisible on the drawings, it is invisible in the price, and it eliminates bidders
rather than repricing them. Any scope line whose specification names a proprietary system needs its
certified-installer requirement carried on the line, and needs a named carrier.

The related fact is that barrier installers are commonly certified by their industry association, say
so on their letterhead, and then exclude the association's own testing. Certification of the installer
and verification of the installation are separate purchases.

### What waterproofers actually price from, in descending order of what they cite

1. The wall sections and the foundation, floor and roof details. This is the primary document and it
   is what distinguishes this trade from the finish trades. Bidders cite specific details by number
   and derive both the extent and the assembly from them, and estimator sheets do the same. Where a
   set uses assembly codes on an assemblies sheet, both sides key to the codes instead.
2. The specification, for product, thickness, permeability, compressive strength, coat count and
   testing requirements. Bidders quote it, and quote it against the details when the two disagree.
3. The structural drawings, for foundation wall heights, cantilever conditions, mat slabs, elevator
   pit configuration and the support of excavation. More load-bearing here than in almost any other
   finish-adjacent trade.
4. Elevations, on restoration work, where the repair extents and their notes live.
5. Unit count, as the leveling denominator. Most estimator sheets normalize this package per
   residential unit, though one normalized lump-sum with no denominator at all. Whichever it is, if
   the unit count is ambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable.
6. External non-drawing documents on renovation: a structural engineer's facade report, which
   defines rebuild and displaced-masonry extents that the architectural elevations do not. Bidders
   qualify against the report and exclude parts of it by name.

### Documents conflicts to expect

This trade generates more genuine documents conflicts than any other in the corpus, and bidders
disclose which side they priced:

- A detail calling out one insulation compressive strength and the specification calling out a higher
  one, with a bidder disclosing that it carried the higher value. On the same item the general
  contractor's own sheet notes that the previous set specified a different R-value, so three documents
  and two rounds disagree about one board.
- A typical wall-section detail showing one wall height and a specific structural detail showing a
  shorter one at a named location.
- A specification section that exists and is not referenced by the wall assembly sheet, and a scope
  item shown on the drawings for which no specification section exists. Both occur on one project.
- Two dates for the same basis sheet inside a single proposal, one on the cover and one in a note.
- A window perimeter sealant length differing by nearly an order of magnitude between the general
  contractor's takeoff and a bidder's stated length.

Route all of these upstream as RFIs. None of them is a leveling problem.

### Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number

- Whether the specified system requires a certified installer, and who holds the certification.
- Union, open shop or prevailing wage, per work group rather than per proposal.
- Whether staging, swing staging, mast climbers, boom lifts or hoisting are in the package. On
  restoration this can be a quarter of the base bid, and it is priced as duration, not as area. A
  negative alternate deducting all staging is the cleanest way to see it.
- The weather and temperature window. Every bidder excludes winter conditions, tenting and heat, and
  several state a minimum substrate temperature that must be rising. Crystalline products need dry
  conditions above freezing for application and a full day after. On a schedule that puts foundation
  waterproofing in winter, that exclusion is a real and large cost, and it belongs to the general
  contractor.
- Mobilization structure. Bidders state minimum productive areas per crew day, and below those areas
  they expect to be paid for the mobilization rather than the area. Small scattered scopes and
  phased releases therefore cost far more per unit than the rate implies, and additional mobilizations
  are separately chargeable at a stated fee.
- Sequence dependencies. Whether the footing needs waterproofing before the wall so the site can
  backfill; whether interior caulking runs by floor; whether the barrier will be exposed to weather
  long enough to need UV protection nobody has priced; whether the deck membrane goes on before the
  trades above it are done.
- Who owns flood testing and water testing. One bidder makes a passing water test a precondition to
  starting its own work. That is a schedule gate and a cost, and no drawing shows it.
- Who owns air barrier verification testing. Excluded by every barrier bidder.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies. It shows up as a small consistent percentage
  adder per work family, or as a deduct in a framer's package, and it is enough to reorder a close
  ranking.
- Warranty term and warranty type. A general contractor may carry a multi-year warranty in the base
  and put the shorter term out as a deduct alternate. Manufacturer system warranties on proprietary
  balcony and mold-prevention products run far longer than the contractor warranty and are released
  only on full payment.
- Tax basis. Some bidders include tax and some exclude it, so round totals across a ladder can measure
  different things.
- Whether the pit is treated from inside or outside, and the pit count and configuration.
- Whether the deck assembly runs through the walking surface or stops at the membrane.
- Whether the general contractor intends to buy this package from one contractor at all. See below.

### The composite-award problem

This is the most important structural fact about the trade and it has no analogue in painting. On a
project with a full envelope scope, no single bidder covers the package. The estimator's winning
column is assembled row by row from several different bidders plus its own plugs: below grade and deck
and pits from one, the barrier from another, expansion joints from a third, interior air sealing from
a fourth, and frost wall damproofing from nobody at all.

That has three consequences for how you write scope. First, the scope lines have to be independently
awardable, because they will be independently awarded. Second, an award-all-or-reprice qualification
from a full-package bidder directly conflicts with how the package actually gets bought, and it needs
surfacing rather than filing. Third, the leveling sheet's ascending ranking is close to meaningless:
it mixes barrier-only bids, mold-coating bids, below-grade-only bids, composite rows and the general
contractor's own budget in one column. The printed spread on such a ranking has run to several times
low-to-high and up to more than an order of magnitude per unit, and almost none of that is price.
Compare only within a scope-breadth class, and establish the class before comparing anything.

The corollary is that this package is frequently under-budgeted. On one project the general
contractor's carried budget was about two fifths of the only complete envelope bid it received.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet for below-grade wall area, split by pre-applied, post-applied and damproofing. The
  primary unit.
- Square feet for deck, plaza, balcony and slab membrane, and for the barrier.
- Square feet for crystalline treatment, counted per coat where the specification calls for two.
- Linear feet for sealants and backer rod, by joint condition. Note that one restoration bidder
  priced sealant replacement by area rather than length, which is unusual enough to be worth
  catching.
- Linear feet for expansion joint covers and, separately and at several times the length, for the
  fire barrier behind them.
- Each, for elevator pits, sump pits, vaults, and balconies. Balcony count is the leveling
  denominator for the largest block on a wood-framed residential job.
- Locations, on expansion joint work: joint size at a stated number of locations totaling a stated
  length is the standard construction of a line.
- Mils, for membrane and barrier thickness, and pounds per square inch for insulation compressive
  strength. These are specification compliance values, not quantities, and they are where a
  specification ladder hides.
- Square feet per crew day, as a mobilization minimum. Real stated figures from one full-package
  bidder, useful as a planning band: damproofing and self-adhered below-grade membrane at several
  hundred square feet per crew day, the air barrier at roughly half that, cold fluid-applied at about
  a thousand, hot fluid-applied at about two thousand, and traffic coatings at about five thousand.
  The ranking matters more than the numbers: the barrier is the slowest work in the package per unit
  area and traffic coating is the fastest by an order of magnitude.
- Residential units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package on most projects.
- Percent of contract value, for bond and general liability, quoted only on request.

Rules of thumb an estimator actually uses, worth carrying because no proposal will supply them:

- The exterior sealant quantity and the air barrier quantity are both driven off the same exterior
  wall area. The barrier runs at several times the sealant rate per square foot of that wall. This is
  the cleanest transferable relationship in the trade.
- Interior caulking is plugged per residential unit, at a few hundred per unit.
- Below-grade waterproofing and damproofing are plugged per square foot of foundation wall at a
  single blended rate.
- Elevator pits are plugged as a lump sum per pit.
- Deck and green-roof horizontal waterproofing is plugged per square foot at a rate several times the
  below-grade rate, and real bids on hot fluid-applied assemblies have landed both below and well
  above that plug.
- Crystalline treatment is plugged per square foot at a rate a few times the below-grade rate.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Scope breadth, meaning which work families the number covers. This dominates everything else and
   it is not a price variable at all. Rankings in this trade are mostly a scope-breadth artifact.
2. Whether the air barrier is in, and over which wall types. It is usually the largest single family
   and its boundary is the least stable.
3. Balcony count and balcony system, on wood-framed residential work.
4. Access: staging type, duration and ownership. On restoration it rivals the production work.
5. The assembly layer stack. Protection board, drainage mat, rigid insulation and the wearing surface
   are each separately large and each detachable.
6. Blindside versus positive-side below-grade work, and pre-applied versus post-applied within it.
7. Union versus open shop, per work group, with prevailing wage as a third basis that leveling sheets
   have no column for.
8. Specification compliance level: membrane thickness, insulation compressive strength and R-value,
   warranty term. These degrade quietly across rounds and buying compliance back can cost more than
   the apparent saving.
9. Sequencing and mobilization count, because minimum crew-day areas make scattered and phased work
   disproportionately expensive.
10. Weather window and winter conditions, which are excluded by everyone and land on the schedule.
11. Testing scope: leak detection on decks, water testing, air barrier verification.
12. Commercial terms: tax basis, tariff exposure, owner-controlled insurance, bond, deposits and
    escalation rights.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within a comparable scope-breadth class the
spread between low and high has been narrow, around a third low-to-high on whole-package bids, and
around a third again between the two bidders who genuinely covered below-grade foundation and pits on
another project. Across a printed ranking as the sheet presents it, the spread has run to several
times and, on one project, to more than fourteen times per unit. The difference between those two
figures is the entire lesson: this trade's apparent price variance is almost all scope variance.

Bidder counts per package run from about four to about eight distinct companies, which is a much
thinner list than the finish trades, and the ranking rows overstate it because one company can appear
several times as different scope carve-outs of one document.

One more convention worth knowing. An estimator here does not compare base bids and it frequently
does not select a single sub. It compares a base-bid-plus-plugs figure for each bidder against a
composite it assembles itself, and where the composite wins, no bidder wins. A scope line set built
for independent award, with every structural gap in section 3 named as its own line, is doing that
assembly work in advance.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly three dozen waterproofing, air barrier and expansion joint
proposals plus five estimator leveling tabs, drawn from four projects in one regional market, all bid
by one general contractor. Three of the projects are multifamily residential new construction with
podium parking, amenity decks and balconies; one is a renovation and facade restoration of an existing
mid-rise. The proposals span design development, permit, construction document and conformed rounds,
and several bidders appear on more than one project, which is what makes the distinction in section 4
between boilerplate and project signal testable. Several bidders submitted three or four rounds, which is
what makes the revision taxonomy in section 2 more than a guess.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from one regional market through one general contractor, and mostly from
  multifamily residential work. Nothing is tested against commercial, institutional, healthcare,
  industrial, below-grade transit or public prevailing-wage work.
- Only one project involved restoration, so the entire restoration regime described in section 1
  rests on that project's five proposals.
- Interior shower pan and wet-area waterproofing barely appears. The corpus shows interior sealants
  reaching plumbing fixtures and countertops, but it does not show a single shower pan membrane
  priced, so the three-way split with the tile setter is asserted from the tiling side and not
  corroborated here.
- Blindside waterproofing appears only as pre-applied membrane against a support of excavation and
  against an adjacent existing building. No deep-excavation or tieback-heavy project is in the
  corpus, so the support-of-excavation interface rests on one project's details and one bidder's
  exclusion.
- Mud slab and blinding dependencies under pre-applied mat slab membrane are named nowhere in any
  proposal. The gap is real; its size is unknown.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, whether the composite awards were actually made as leveled, or what the
  certified-installer scope was ultimately bought from.
- No change order history and no leak or warranty claim history is in the corpus. The claim that the
  transition seams and the sequencing qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the
  proposals' own language rather than observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- Injection and remedial waterproofing on an occupied or leaking building does not appear as a priced
  scope. One bidder lists epoxy injection among its capabilities on its letterhead and prices none.
- Warranty structure is thin. Manufacturer system warranty terms appear only on two proprietary
  products, watertightness warranty language appears nowhere, and the only warranty leveling seen is
  a general contractor carrying a term in the base with a shorter term as a deduct.
- Flood testing ownership rests on a single sentence in a single proposal.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the general contractor successfully bought this entire
  package from one contractor, so the composite-award pattern in section 6 may be a property of this
  general contractor's bid lists rather than of the trade.
