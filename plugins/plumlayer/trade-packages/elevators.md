# Elevators and Vertical Transportation

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the elevator package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The entry
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real elevator contractors can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

One warning belongs at the top, because it reorders everything else. In most trades the drawings
describe the work and the proposal prices it. Here the proposal describes the building. An elevator
proposal is mostly a list of things the general contractor must build, provide, power, frame, drill,
rate, vent and protect before the elevator contractor will set foot on site, and those things change
depending on which bidder wins. Scope for this package is only half about the elevator. The other
half is the hoistway, and the hoistway is not settled until the bidder is chosen.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The anchor section is 142100, electric traction elevators. It is the section a proposal names when it
names one, and the section the estimator uses to key the package. Two sibling numbers appear on the
same projects and mean real distinctions rather than filing preference:

- 142200 for traction elevator modernization on existing equipment, sometimes split further by the
  general contractor into control equipment and car components.
- 142600 for limited use and limited application elevators, the small low-rise accessibility car
  usually called a LULA.

A fourth number, in the 149100 range, is where lifts get leveled when the estimator keeps them off the
elevator sheet. Trash and linen chutes also sit in division 14 and are never part of this package;
they go to the specialties bidder and share nothing with elevators but a division number.

### Two grain regimes under one division

This trade splits cleanly into two kinds of work that share almost no scope vocabulary, and an entry
that treats them as one will produce a scope list that fits neither. Decide which regime the project
is in before anything else.

New construction is an equipment purchase. The scope question is which car goes in which shaft, at
what capacity and speed, with what finishes, and what the building must provide around it. The
proposal is a configuration document.

Modernization is a surgical replacement inside a machine that already exists and often still runs. The
scope question is which of about thirty-five components is replaced and which is retained, what
happens to the cars while the work proceeds, and who owns the demolition and disposal of what comes
out. The proposal is a retain-or-replace matrix. Pricing comes from a physical survey of the existing
equipment, not from the drawings, and existing capacity and speed are read off the state's own rating
record rather than off a schedule. A project can carry both regimes at once, plus a new lift, and each
needs its own scope list.

### Product class is a scope decision, not a preference

The single most consequential fact in this trade: the class of machine the bidder proposes rewrites the
general contractor's own work. Bidders on the same package routinely propose different classes, and
the drawn hoistway usually suits only one of them.

- Machine-room-less traction is the default offering. It wants a deep pit, tall overhead, a rated
  hoist beam and a separate lifeline beam, and a small adjacent control space rather than a machine
  room.
- In-ground conventional hydraulic wants a shallower pit, less overhead, a lower-rated hoist beam, a
  real machine room adjacent at the lowest landing, and a drilled jack hole with spoils to remove.
- Geared and machine-room traction wants an actual machine room, with the reactions on walls for a
  basement or adjacent machine roughly double those of a machine directly overhead.
- LULA and residential lifts want a pit well under three feet, a dedicated machine space, and a
  hoistway that a lift manufacturer will typically hand over as a design guide rather than describe
  in clauses.

On a package where the drawings showed a four foot pit, the hydraulic bidder quoted that pit and every
traction bidder demanded five feet, one of them flagging the overhead as short by inches as well.
Nobody was wrong. The drawings had committed to a machine class the market did not want to sell. That
concrete and that shaft height are not in anybody's number, and the class question is worth resolving
before the package goes out. It is also a value-engineering lever: an estimator's own note on one
project put the saving from converting a short passenger car from traction to holeless hydraulic at
roughly fifteen to twenty percent of that car.

### Collapse attractors

Scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits elsewhere:

- Pit ladders. Genuinely contested, and answered three different ways: some bidders supply and
  install them, some supply and let others install, some leave them entirely to the general
  contractor. Watch for a single proposal answering both ways, because it happens.
- Hoist beams and lifeline beams. Usually entirely by others, occasionally furnished by the elevator
  contractor and cut and installed by others.
- Rail bracket inserts. Commonly furnished by the elevator contractor and installed by others, with
  the supports themselves always by others.
- Machine beams. Furnished and installed by the elevator contractor while the supports under them are
  the general contractor's.
- Hoistway screening and barricades. Always the general contractor's on new construction. On
  modernization one bidder carried screening in its base, so the regimes differ here.
- Cab flooring installation labour, in small capped amounts. See section 5.
- The crane. Sometimes included by the bidder, sometimes required from the general contractor,
  sometimes offered as an alternate against a building modification.

### Specialty carve-outs

Scope that leaves the package even when it looks like elevator work:

- Custom cab interiors from a third-party cab manufacturer. Specifications name a separate tier of cab
  and entrance manufacturers, and no bidder in the corpus answered those rows. Priced through the
  elevator contractor a premium factory finish package runs a few thousand per car; a third-party
  custom interior can run several times that.
- Smoke curtains at elevator doors. Named and excluded, never carried.
- Firefighters' two-way telephone and the fire command center panel. Excluded by every bidder seen,
  with one arguing at length that the requirement is obsolete.
- Cab flooring material. Always by others, without exception.
- The structural or architectural stamp on the elevator shop drawings that the state permit requires.
  Excluded by every bidder that mentions it, and mentioned by several. Nobody else prices it either.
- Decommissioning of existing cars, which behaves as its own trade. See section 7 for why.

## 2. How the market bids

This is the opposite of a degenerate proposal trade. Painting gives you a number and a bullet list.
Elevators give you twenty to sixty pages, and the number is the smallest part of it.

The standard-form proposal has a stable anatomy, and a reader should expect all of it:

1. A cover and a sales letter carrying the lump sum, usually written out in words as well as figures,
   with the tax and bond basis stated in the same sentence.
2. A block of numbered project-specific qualifications and clarifications. Thirty to fifty-seven of
   them is normal. This block is where the project-specific content lives and it is the highest-value
   reading in the document.
3. Sometimes a yes-or-no matrix of additional scope items with quantities, and a rate card of fees for
   the things not included.
4. One equipment specification sheet per car mark, giving capacity, speed, travel, stops, front and
   rear openings, hoistway dimensions, pit depth, overhead, power supply, and every finish.
5. Contract terms and conditions, which are scope. See section 4.
6. A preparatory work by others attachment, running four to eight pages, organised by building system:
   job conditions, electrical, hoistway, hoistway front, machine or control room, pit, and turnover.

The base bid itself is nearly always one lump sum. Segmentation, when it happens, is by car: a genuine
per-car schedule of values with a group subtotal and a building total. Roughly a third of proposals do
this and it is enormously more useful than the lump sum, because it exposes that price tracks stops
and capacity rather than car count. Segmentation by scope family appears on projects that carry
several kinds of work, where modernization, decommissioning and a lift each get their own number.

Genuine line-item pricing is rare and appears at the small end of the market. A lift dealer's quote
priced thirteen separate lines including equipment, installation labour, freight, delivery, permit fee,
inspection fee and an administration fee, and it is the only document in the corpus that separates
equipment cost from installation labour, disclosing roughly a two-to-one split. The large
manufacturers never disclose that split.

What a proposal will state:

- The cited basis document, with dates, nearly always, and usually naming the drawing set and the
  specification separately with different dates.
- The equipment configuration per car in exhaustive detail.
- Lead times broken into phases, and what gates each phase.
- Labour type. Every traction bidder seen is signatory to the elevator constructors' union, and the
  estimator records labour type for every bidder anyway. The lift market is genuinely mixed, and one
  company appeared as union on the traction package and open shop on the lift package of the same
  project.
- A long, specific list of what the general contractor must provide.
- Rates for the things excluded: operator hours, storage, remobilisation, temporary use, reinspection.

What a proposal will not state:

- Installation labour hours, unless the general contractor asks. When asked, the answers spread nearly
  two to one between bidders quoting identical equipment, which is worth knowing before treating a low
  number as efficiency.
- The material and labour split.
- Any quantity of the building work it is requiring, which is what makes the by-others list a scope
  problem rather than a reading exercise.
- Whether the control system is proprietary. On modernization, one independent led with a
  non-proprietary controller as its headline feature; the manufacturers' own terms make the opposite
  commitment. See section 4.

Revision habits are distinctive and mostly not about price:

- The general contractor issues the for-construction set with a narrative of changes and requires a
  price response from every competitive bidder even from those whose price is unaffected, explicitly
  so that receipt and review are confirmed. This generates a large volume of no-change
  reconfirmations that are real records but not pricing events.
- A revision may move only the schedule gate. One pair of rounds carried the same date, the same
  price and byte-nearly-identical text, differing only in the ship-by date, the
  installation-complete date and the price validity date, all pushed out about six months. The entire
  substance was three dates.
- A revision may move only the maintenance terms, arriving as an explanation of what the included
  warranty year actually covers.
- The tax basis can change between rounds. One ladder went from a figure quoted plus applicable taxes
  to a figure quoted including applicable taxes, which makes the apparent rise between round totals
  a comparison of two different things and hides a real decrease.
- Filename revision labels are unreliable to the point of being misleading. One identical document
  appeared three times across two set-stage folders under three different labels including an R1
  suffix, none of them a distinct pricing moment. Another folder's base file was the superseded
  earlier proposal with a marketing brochure moved to the front, byte-different from the original and
  character-for-character identical in text. Order rounds by the cited basis document, never by the
  label and never by the letterhead date.
- Prices fall across rounds more often than they rise, sometimes by a tenth or more from design
  development to construction documents, and the reason is essentially never documented.

Two process facts shape the whole package. First, both sides expect a descope call before award; the
bidders ask for it in writing, naming scope of work, installation process, site readiness expectations
and turnover dates as the agenda. Second, the project owner may attend subcontractor interviews
alongside the general contractor before award. Neither is optional in practice, and a scope list that
does not survive a descope call has not done its job.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Elevator scope sheets seen have run 8, 26, 27, 38 and 58 rows. Those numbers are misleading, because
almost every one of those rows describes the equipment rather than the work. The honest target for a
new-construction traction package is roughly fifty to eighty rows, of which no more than half should be
equipment configuration and the rest should be the boundary items that the market sheets have no rows
for at all.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every car, by its mark. Cars differ in capacity, speed, stops, opening configuration, cab height and
  finish package, and the price differences between them are large. Every proposal enumerates by car
  mark and every estimator sheet does too.
- Per car: capacity, speed, travel, number of stops, and front and rear openings counted separately.
  Openings are the row most often wrong, and getting it wrong is expensive. On one package a bidder
  disputed the architectural sheet's front and rear count outright, arguing the front opening must be
  at the main landing. On another the estimator's own quantity cell read four or five stops with a
  note asking whether one street frontage had a roof landing. Stop count is both the leveling
  denominator and the primary price driver, so an ambiguous stop count corrupts the entire comparison.
- Product class per car: traction with no machine room, traction with a machine room, in-ground
  hydraulic, holeless hydraulic, LULA. This is a row, not a note, because it determines the pit, the
  overhead, the beam ratings and whether a machine room exists.
- The hoistway envelope per car: clear width and depth, pit depth, clear overhead, and hoist beam and
  lifeline beam ratings. Enumerate these because bidders each demand different ones. On one package
  four bidders each stated a different required hoistway width or footprint and at least one said the
  drawn width would not accept its equipment.
- Machine room or control space: whether one exists, where it is, its clear headroom, its access
  route, its ventilation and temperature range, and whether the door and slab are the elevator
  contractor's or the general contractor's.
- Cab interior finishes as a group per car: walls, front return, ceiling, handrails, skirting, sill,
  and the flooring recess depth. Not one row per element; one row per car for the finish package,
  with the recess depth and weight limit called out because they bind another trade.
- Hoistway entrances as a group per car: frame type, frame and door finish, sill material, door type
  and speed, and opening size. Separate the entrance finish from the cab finish, because the
  main-landing-only stainless upgrade is a standard value-engineering move and needs somewhere to land.
- Hall and car fixtures as a group per car.
- Every item on the by-others list that costs money, as its own row. This is the block the market
  sheets omit entirely and it is the reason to write a scope list at all. At minimum: hoist beam,
  lifeline beam, temporary working platform or workdeck, rail bracket supports and intermediate
  supports, insert installation and welding, pit ladder, pit waterproofing, sump and sump pump with a
  stated capacity, pit lighting and receptacle, hoistway venting, counterweight screening, hoistway
  barricades and entrance screening, cutting and patching and core drilling and firestopping, sill and
  entrance grouting, machine beam supports, machine room slab and its blockouts, disconnects and
  feeders, shunt trip, smoke detectors and their recall zones, the dedicated phone or data line, and
  the structural stamp on the elevator shop drawings.
- Operator time, called ride time or car running, with an explicit hours basis. See below.
- Temporary use of the permanent car, as its own block. See section 5.
- Maintenance term, as its own row, separate from warranty term.
- Tariff treatment, as its own row.
- On modernization: each component, marked new or retained. Controller, machine and motor, governor,
  machine room wiring, door operator, door protection device, car door panels, car door tracks and
  hangers, hoistway door panels, hoistway door equipment, interlocks, hoistway entrances, hoistway
  wiring, terminal stopping devices, car guides, counterweight guides, car safety device, car frame,
  car platform, ropes, cab shell and interior, cab flooring, load weighing device, buffers, pit
  switch, guide rails and their supports, counterweight assembly, car operating panel, position
  indicator, emergency car lighting, intercom provision, audible signal, top-of-car station, hall
  stations, hall buttons, hall lanterns, combination lantern and indicator, and the lobby emergency
  power panel. That is the modernization scope list. Each retained item is a risk the owner inherits,
  and the enumerated list is what makes two modernization bids comparable.
- On modernization: decommissioning per car, with the operations named. See section 7.
- On modernization: the rigging route, as an explicit either-or between an access hatch with its
  ceiling demolition and reinstatement and its MEP relocation, and crane picks.
- On modernization: out-of-service duration per car, because it is a building-operations constraint,
  not just a schedule number.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual fixtures, buttons, lanterns and indicators collapse into a fixtures group per car. Every
  proposal and every estimator sheet does this.
- Individual cab finish materials collapse into the cab finish package per car, with the exceptions
  noted above.
- Landing-by-landing enumeration collapses into a stop count plus a front and rear split, unless a
  landing genuinely differs. It sometimes does: a car may change opening sides partway up the rise,
  and that is worth its own line.
- Coat counts, gauges and material grades are attributes of a line, not lines. Bidders state that all
  gauges and materials are their standard and that no deviation is permitted.
- Individual pieces of hoistway steel collapse into the beam and bracket rows above.
- Individual code citations collapse. The by-others lists cite code paragraph by paragraph and the
  scope line only needs the obligation.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied here,
the test almost never fires on the equipment and almost always fires on the boundary, which is exactly
backwards from how the market's own sheets are built.

The test also tells you when the specification is not the answer. Manufacturers write, in their
general clarifications, that differences may exist between the proposed product and the specified
product and that in the case of any such difference the proposal supersedes and takes precedence over
the specification. Every large manufacturer in the corpus carries a version of that clause. So the
specification does not settle what is being bought; the proposal does. A scope line that says "per
specification" has not scoped anything in this trade.

### The trade's structural gap list

An estimator does not compare base bids here either. It compares base bid plus plugs, and in this
trade the plug list is short, large and remarkably consistent.

Operator time, called ride time or car running, is the plug. It appeared on three of the four projects
as an explicit estimator row with an hours quantity and an hourly rate, and the rate was identical
across projects, which means it is a standing internal rule rather than a per-job judgement. Every
bidder but one excludes it. The one bidder that did carry it carried half the hours the estimator
assumed. On a mid-size package the plug runs roughly six to thirteen percent of base bid. It exists
because the elevator constructors' union rules make a licensed elevator mechanic the only person
permitted to operate a car so other trades can access the hoistway, so every trade that needs the shaft
is buying elevator labour whether anyone priced it or not. Any elevator scope line set should carry a
ride time line with an explicit hours basis, because no proposal will supply it.

The other recurring plugs:

- Decommissioning, where existing cars are involved and a bidder did not carry it. The estimator
  routes another bidder's price in, which is a defensible move only because the spread makes any other
  choice arbitrary.
- Cab finishes allowance, carried per car where the finish selection is not settled.
- Tariff, now appearing as its own leveling row.
- Bond, where the contract value crosses the threshold at which the general contractor asks for it.
- Everything on the by-others list, which no estimator sheet in the corpus plugged and every one of
  them should have. On one project the adjustments column was zero for every bidder while every
  proposal excluded operator time, temporary use, cutting and patching, hoist beams and barricades.
  That is the largest single gap this entry can point at.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate in the sense that every bidder carries some version. Their value is that
they name the party who must pick the work up, and if no other package carries it, the exclusion is a
gap.

| Excluded by the elevator contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Cutting, patching, core drilling, firestopping and fire safing (every bidder seen) | General contractor, and it recurs at every hoistway penetration |
| Hoist beam and separate lifeline beam, both rated (most bidders; a minority furnish and let others install) | Structural steel, coordinated to the elevator layout |
| Rail bracket supports, intermediate supports and separator beams (every bidder seen) | Structural steel or masonry; inserts usually furnished by the elevator contractor |
| Welding the elevator contractor's inserts into support steel (some bidders) | Steel erector, and easy to miss |
| Temporary working platform or workdeck at the top of the hoistway (most bidders) | General contractor, who also erects, maintains and removes it, and whose electrician uses it |
| Hoistway barricades and full entrance screening to fall-protection standards (every bidder seen on new construction) | General contractor, erect, maintain and remove |
| Counterweight screening where a counterweight sits inside a shared hoistway (some bidders) | General contractor, and it must be done before the elevator crew mobilises |
| Pit waterproofing, sump, sump pump at a stated gallons-per-hour capacity, cover and grating (every bidder seen) | Concrete and plumbing |
| Pit lighting, guarded fixture, switch by the ladder, and a ground-fault receptacle (every bidder seen) | Electrical |
| All disconnects, feeders, branch wiring and shunt trip (most bidders; one manufacturer furnishes and installs the top-of-shaft disconnect and says so as a selling point) | Electrical |
| Smoke and heat detectors, their wiring, and the recall zone contacts (every bidder seen) | Fire alarm, and the recall zoning is specific enough to need its own coordination |
| A dedicated phone line or data connection per car, continuously monitored (every bidder seen) | Electrical or technology, and digital or fibre lines must be converted by others |
| Remote conduit outside the shaft, to lobby panels, fire command, security desk and monitoring stations (most bidders) | Electrical, with pull strings left in |
| Card readers, cameras, speakers and their wiring and conduit in the lobbies (every bidder seen; the elevator contractor supplies only the interface board and the in-car wiring) | Security and technology |
| Hoistway venting to code, with a power shut vent where required (most bidders) | Mechanical, and it is a permitting item on lifts |
| Machine room ventilation, heating and cooling to hold a stated temperature and humidity band (every bidder seen) | Mechanical |
| Grouting of sills and entrance frames (most bidders; one manufacturer's entrance arrangement removes the need for a support angle and it says so) | General contractor, after frames are set |
| Machine beam supports, machine room slab, blockouts and pockets (every bidder seen) | Structural and concrete, to reactions the elevator contractor supplies |
| Certification that the building will accommodate the elevator loads and reactions, and the structural stamp on the elevator shop drawings for the state permit (several bidders, explicitly) | Structural engineer of record, and frequently unassigned |

| Temporary use of the permanent car (every bidder seen) | General contractor, and see section 5 |
| Offsite storage, double handling and remobilisation (most bidders, all with rate cards) | General contractor, and the rates differ by a factor of forty between market segments |
| Overtime, off hours and police details (most bidders) | General contractor |

| Flood-zone equipment and provisions (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned |
| Attic stock and spare parts (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned |
| Buy America, domestic sourcing and filed sub-bid qualifications (most bidders, explicitly refused) | Disqualifying on public work; ask early |
| Local residency and hiring goal compliance (one manufacturer refuses outright, and no bidder on the sheet answered the question) | Unresolved, and see below |
| On modernization: asbestos and hazardous material survey, abatement and containment (every bidder seen) | Abatement contractor, before the elevator crew works |
| On modernization: removal or encapsulation of non-elevator pipes and wiring found in the existing shaft (every bidder seen) | General contractor, and it is a discovery item |
| On modernization: disposal of the removed machines, controllers, ropes, hydraulic fluid and oils (every bidder seen) | Owner or general contractor, which surprises people |
| On modernization: permit-required confined space monitoring, permitting, attendants and rescue planning if the pit is classified that way (several bidders) | Owner, and one bidder simply declares it will not work in a permit-required confined space |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

The exclusion lists travel verbatim. One independent's terms and conditions were near word-for-word a
large manufacturer's, down to an unfilled placeholder where an insurance rate should have been. Two
competitors quoted an identical bond rate per thousand. Leveling on exclusion lists alone yields close
to zero differentiation in this trade. Read the boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

The project signal lives in the numbered project-specific qualifications, and it is unusually good:

- A stated dimension the bidder's equipment requires that the drawings do not show. Pit depth,
  overhead height, hoistway width, and front-versus-rear opening assignments all appeared this way,
  each flagged as important by the bidder. These are the trade's best request-for-information source.
- A statement that a specific drawing sheet is incorrect, with the sheet the bidder priced instead
  named. Two competitors independently wrote the same objection in the same words on one project, and
  the estimator recorded it as a note rather than converting it into a request for information.
- A refusal to install a named product line, which on the small-lift side determines who can bid at
  all.
- A required building modification stated as an assumption. One bidder assumed the general contractor
  would provide new machine room platform stairs with railings and an exterior machine room entrance;
  nobody else mentioned it and nobody priced it, which makes that number incomparable in a direction
  the leveling sheet cannot see.
- An explicit note that the bidder has not visited the site.

### Contract terms are scope

More than in any other trade, the commercial terms change what the number is worth, and they are not
negotiable at the margins.

- Payment is heavily front-loaded and nearly uniform: fifty percent on award or before release to the
  factory, thirty to thirty-five percent on material at the warehouse or before shipment, fifteen to
  twenty percent for installation labour, five percent retention. One bidder requires ninety-five
  percent before turnover. This is a working-capital fact, not a footnote, and it is a condition of
  the price.
- Liquidated damages are refused universally.
- Insurance is constrained. Manufacturers will not name additional insureds and will not waive
  subrogation, and will participate in an owner-controlled programme only at no cost to themselves.
  Asked for a credit against an owner-controlled programme, one bidder offered none.
- Escalation clauses are live and varied. Price protection windows of sixty days, four months, six
  months and twelve months all appear, after which the price adjusts. One manufacturer states the
  price is subject to escalation even after acceptance. Another ties a labour rate increase to a
  calendar year boundary if site work slips.
- Tariff is handled four different ways on the same bid list: disclaimed with a recommended
  contingency, reserved as a future adjustment, indemnified onto the purchaser outright, and priced
  as separate per-car pass-through alternates at about two percent of each car. Only the last is
  comparable to anything.
- Validity windows are short and enforced: twenty-one, thirty, forty-five and sixty days all appear,
  and one proposal automatically revokes itself if not accepted in time.
- Proprietary lock-in is a term, and it is the most consequential one on modernization. The
  manufacturers retain ownership of diagnostic tools, software, manuals and monitoring hardware, will
  not release them, will deactivate connected features if the maintenance contract ends, and license
  the diagnostic tools for a fee for the life of the building. An independent led its modernization
  proposal with the opposite commitment: a non-proprietary controller requiring no manufacturer
  tools, devices, passwords or software to maintain for the equipment's life. That is a
  decades-long service-cost decision being made inside a bid comparison, and it appears nowhere on a
  drawing.
- Maintenance is sold during the bid, not after. A twelve-month term is the near-universal base.
  Extending it is priced, and the same extension was priced very differently by different bidders on
  the same package. Multi-year agreements are quoted as alternates with monthly rates, and an owner's
  own maintenance specification ran about forty percent above the manufacturer's standard agreement,
  buying hourly preventive maintenance per unit per month, monthly fire service testing, premium
  callbacks and an annual clean down. Warranty term and maintenance term are different things and
  bidders conflate them; keep them on separate rows.

### Scope gates

Distinct from qualifications, because they void scope rather than qualify it:

- Critical dimensions field-verified before material release, on some bidders. On others the opposite:
  the bidder explicitly does not field verify and builds to approved shop drawings, putting the risk
  of a wrongly built shaft on the general contractor. Both postures appear on the same bid list, and
  which one you have determines who eats a hoistway that is out of tolerance.
- Site readiness fully complete before manpower is scheduled, verified by a pre-start checklist or a
  site check inspection. Material will not be delivered until it passes.
- Permanent power, of permanent characteristics, available before or during installation. Traction
  installations typically require it for the entire installation, which is a real sequencing gate and
  a question general contractors ask by email because proposals do not volunteer it.
- All electrical work by others complete before mobilisation, including work in the overhead.
- Ship-by and installation-complete dates, past which the price rises regardless of fault.
- One bidder will not proceed with extra work once unexecuted change orders exceed ten percent of the
  subcontract price.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The hoistway readiness seam, which is the trade

The preparatory work by others attachment is not boilerplate to skim. It is the trade's real scope
knowledge, it runs four to eight pages, and its contents vary by manufacturer in ways that cost money.
Treat differences between two bidders' by-others lists as price differences, because they are.

Concrete examples of the same seam answered differently on one bid list:

- The top-of-shaft disconnect for a machine-room-less car. One manufacturer furnishes and installs it
  and advertises that it saves the electrician's cost. Another states that all disconnects related to
  the elevator work, including in the machine room, are furnished and installed by others.
- The hoistway lobby sill support angle. One manufacturer's entrance arrangement eliminates it, and it
  says so explicitly as a cost the general contractor avoids. Others require it.
- Sill grouting. One manufacturer requires no grout under the sill, only between the sill face and the
  finished floor. Others require grouting under and in front of all sills and around the machine
  beams.
- The hoist beam. Entirely by others at one rating, entirely by others at a different rating for a
  hydraulic car, or furnished by the elevator contractor and cut to size and installed by others. On
  a renovation one bidder offered to use the existing roof beam in lieu of a hoist beam to avoid roof
  modifications, while explicitly disclaiming responsibility for verifying that the beam could hold
  the load, and requiring others to fire rate it before testing.
- The pit ladder, three ways, as noted in section 1.
- Rail bracket support. Intermediate supports are required above a stated floor-to-floor height, and
  the height differs by product line, so the same building can need intermediate steel for one bidder
  and not for another.
- Masonry hoistways specifically: hollow block will not take bracket fastenings, so either the
  manufacturer's inserts go in as the wall is built or a concrete belt is required at every floor.
  That decision has to be made before the shaft is laid up.

The by-others list also carries requirements that are easy to read past and expensive to discover:

- The front entrance wall at the main landing must not be built until all elevator equipment is in the
  hoistway, and the remaining front walls not until frames and sills are set. This sequences the
  masonry or drywall contractor around the elevator, not the other way round.
- The machine room slab or grating over the hoistway must not be poured until the machinery is set.
- The machine room and hoistway must sit on the same side of a building expansion joint.
- The hoistway must be dried in and stay dried in for the entire project, and the roof over the
  hoistway or machine room may need to be left open for a crane pick and then closed.
- A minimum temperature must be maintained in the work areas during installation.
- Refuse containers must be provided for elevator packaging or the disposal becomes the owner's.
- Storage requirements are specific and large: a dry enclosed secure area of a stated footprint per
  elevator, within a stated distance of the hoistway, with rollable access at grade. Footprints and
  distances differ by bidder. Delivery is by long flatbed and needs a turning radius.
- Ledges and projections over a stated depth need bevel guards at a stated angle.
- No non-elevator pipe or conduit may run in the hoistway, and power feeders may not run up it.

### Cab flooring, the seam that reaches another trade

Cab flooring material is by others without exception, and it is bounded by physical limits that
constrain the flooring contractor's product selection: a recess depth of three quarters of an inch to
an inch and a quarter, a maximum thickness, and on one bidder an explicit maximum weight per square
foot. Installation labour is answered four ways on four bids: entirely by others; up to four team
hours per car; up to eight hours per car; up to one day per car with anything beyond billed by change
order. One bidder adds that grouted tile specifically incurs additional labour, which is the same fact
stated from the weight side.

Three consequences. The material must be on site when the cab is installed or remobilisation is
charged. The flooring selection must be checked against the weight and thickness limits before it is
bought. And a heavy floor finish quietly converts a small included labour allowance into a change
order. Elevator cab flooring is a row on the flooring scope sheet and a row on the elevator scope
sheet, and the two must agree.

### Painting

Where entrances and frames are supplied prime finished rather than factory finished, the painting
contractor paints them, and elevator contractors say so directly in their by-others lists. Three
separate bidders across the new-construction and modernization regimes name the painter for this.
Painters, for their part, commonly exclude factory-quality metal finishing at elevator doors and
sometimes exclude metallic and specialty coatings at elevator surrounds. So the seam closes only if
the entrances are specified factory finished, most often stainless. If the specification allows prime
finished entrances and the painting package does not carry them, this is a real gap on both sides.

### Structural

The elevator contractor supplies loads and reactions and explicitly does not certify that the building
can carry them. Someone must: the structural engineer of record stamps the elevator shop drawings for
the state permit, and several bidders name this as by others. Nobody prices it. The reactions
themselves depend on the machine location, and a basement or adjacent machine roughly doubles the wall
reactions of an overhead machine, so a late change of machine location is a structural change.

### Temporary use of the permanent car

This is the seam every other trade cares about, because a working elevator changes the hoisting
economics of the whole project. No bidder includes it. All of them price it, and none price it the
same way: a one-time startup and inspection fee plus a weekly rate plus a clean-down fee; a flat
monthly fee per car plus inspection costs; or a lump sum per car plus a monthly maintenance adder and
an overtime adder. Those three structures are not comparable to each other without deciding a duration
first.

The general contractor's own obligations during temporary use are a scope list in themselves, and one
bidder enumerates six: protection of the car, protection of the hoistway openings, electrical service,
a temporary car enclosure, an operator, and two-way voice communication boxes at each landing. Add to
that a state inspection before the temporary period begins and further inspections during it, and two
weeks at the end to refurbish the car to a like-new state before final turnover. Refurbishment beyond
that is assessed by change order based on condition, and replacement of components due to misuse is
excluded. If the schedule depends on using the permanent elevators, all of that belongs on the scope
sheet and in the schedule, not in a note.

### Electrical, in more detail than the exclusion row suggests

The elevator by-others lists are effectively an electrical scope package, and the electrician's
estimate will be wrong if it is written before this list is read. Beyond the disconnects and feeders:
a separate car light circuit with its own disconnect; ground-fault receptacles in pit, machine room
and car top, with a non-ground-fault receptacle for the sump pump; a specific short-circuit limit at
the load side of the main disconnect; a ground wire to the primary building ground; hoistway and pit
lighting at stated foot-candle levels with switches at stated positions; shunt trip where sprinklers
are present, with heat detectors at a stated distance from the sprinkler heads; the fire alarm recall
zones wired as normally closed contacts in named groupings; conduit and pull string to every remote
panel; a dedicated circuit and network connection for the emergency communication system with battery
backup and stated minimum bandwidth per car; and temporary three-phase power of permanent
characteristics during installation.

### Regenerative drives

Most current products are regenerative, which means they push power back into the building. If the
building or the standby generator cannot absorb it, that is a problem, and the elevator contractor
puts it back on the general contractor: absorption of regenerated power on standby power is by others,
and one bidder prices chopper and resistor units to dissipate it as a per-car adder. Confirming the
generator can accept it is a question that must be answered before award.

### Modernization-specific seams

- Demolition splits. On a shared machine room serving several cars, one bidder disposed of the
  equipment for two of three cars in its base and left the rest. The estimator's own note records the
  reason the boundary sits there: equipment left behind in the machine room is not considered elevator
  equipment by the state, so it is cheaper for the general contractor to remove it than the elevator
  contractor.
- The rigging route is an either-or with real building cost on one side. An access hatch requires
  ceiling removal and reinstatement, and relocation and reinstatement of any wiring, sprinklers or
  duct beneath it, plus a rated rigging beam, all by others. The alternative is premium-time crane
  mobilisations priced as an alternate. Both belong on the sheet so the comparison is visible.
- Existing hoistway doors removed by the elevator contractor while the new pit door is by others is
  the kind of split that gets missed.
- Out-of-service duration runs roughly fourteen to eighteen weeks per car, which in an occupied
  building is an operations problem before it is a schedule problem.
- Keeping the remaining cars running matters. One bidder priced connecting existing controllers to
  temporary generators and re-inspecting them with the state as an alternate.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What elevator contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The dedicated elevator plan and section sheets, usually one or two per car group. These give the
   shaft, the pit, the overhead and the openings, and they are what the bidder measures against its own
   product requirements.
2. The specification section, cited by number, though as section 4 explains the manufacturers' own
   terms make the specification advisory rather than binding.
3. The finishes sheet or elevator finish schedule.
4. On modernization: a physical survey of the existing equipment, and the state's rating record for
   capacity and speed. Neither is a project document.
5. On modernization: an independent elevator consultant's scope document, where one exists. This is
   neither the drawings nor the architect's specification, and on one project it was the basis two
   bidders cited. Those two landed within one percent of each other while every bidder pricing from
   something else ran twenty to sixty percent higher. If a consultant scope exists, find it; it is
   the most price-determining document in the package.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number or the building:

- Which product class the winning bidder proposes, and therefore the pit depth, overhead height, beam
  ratings, machine room existence and location, and whether a jack hole gets drilled.
- Whether the drawn hoistway dimensions actually accept anybody's equipment.
- The seismic design category, which bidders assume and ask to have confirmed, and which changes
  hoistway dimensions as well as cost.
- Whether local residency or hiring goals apply. Asked on one project, the row came back blank, not
  applicable, to be determined, or a literal question mark from every bidder, and one manufacturer
  states plainly it will not meet them.
- Whether public-work sourcing or filed sub-bid rules apply, which most bidders refuse outright.
- How many ride time hours the general contractor expects. See section 3.
- Whether the permanent cars will be used temporarily, for how long, and who supplies the six
  general-contractor items that go with it.
- Whether permanent power will be available for the whole installation.
- Whether the standby generator can absorb regenerated power.
- Whether the control system is proprietary, and what that costs the owner over the building's life.
- The maintenance term the owner actually wants and whose maintenance specification governs.
- Tariff exposure and how it is allocated.
- Factory shutdown weeks, which one manufacturer discloses add two weeks if the manufacturing window
  falls in them.
- Whether the pit will be classified a permit-required confined space.
- On existing buildings: asbestos, non-elevator services in the shaft, whether the shaft can be
  extended, and how the machine gets in and out.

### Lead times are a leveling axis, not a footnote

This is a long-lead, fabricate-to-order trade and the schedule is a comparison field. The phases and
their typical durations:

- Submittal preparation: one to six weeks.
- Contract review, where the manufacturer's legal review is a stated gate: up to six weeks.
- Submittal approval: undefined, and owned by the design team.
- Manufacturing: twelve to twenty-two weeks is normal, and one bidder quotes a range up to forty.
- Manpower scheduling: ten to twelve weeks out, and independent of manufacturing.
- Installation: four to eight weeks per car for traction on the manufacturers, and up to seventeen
  weeks total from one independent quoting a different product class.
- Adjusting and commissioning: one to two weeks per car.
- State inspection: dependent on inspector availability, which nobody controls.

Two things about these phases matter more than the durations. First, they are explicitly not
contiguous, and manpower lead time runs in parallel with manufacturing rather than after it. Second,
manufacturing does not start until a fully executed contract, approved submittals, the first payment,
and in some cases field verification are all in hand. The buyout date for this package is driven by
that gate, not by the installation date, and it is usually the earliest hard buyout on the job.

### Set-stage behaviour

At design development, bidders quote budgets and say so, sometimes as a single sentence in an email
with one number covering three scope families. Budget quotes carry the phrase "for budgetary purposes
only" and a stated dollar-year. By permit and construction documents the same bidders produce the full
standard-form document with the numbered qualification block, and that block is where the project
appears. Treat a design-development elevator number as a per-stop budget carry, not as a bid.

Prices generally fall from design development to construction documents, then move a little either way
through the negotiation and interview rounds. The exception is the reconfirmation round, which
frequently moves nothing at all and exists because the general contractor requires a response.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Stops, which is the leveling currency of this trade. Every new-construction leveling sheet in the
  corpus normalises to cost per stop, counting total stops across all cars, and ranks on it. If the
  set does not make the stop count unambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable, which is why the
  ambiguous-stop-count row in section 3 matters so much.
- Cars, for the equipment itself and for most rate cards.
- Dwelling units, on a residential modernization project, where the estimator normalised to cost per
  unit instead of per stop. The two bases are not comparable to each other, and a rate carried across
  from one project type to another on the wrong basis will be wrong by a large factor. Always record
  which denominator a rate came from.
- Landings and openings, counted separately front and rear, for entrances and fixtures.
- Pounds of capacity and feet per minute of speed, which together with stops set the product selection.
- Hours, for operator time, ride time, standby and change-order labour, always with a rate.
- Weeks, for lead time, installation duration and out-of-service duration.
- Months, for storage and maintenance.
- Dollars per thousand of contract value, for bonds and for owner-protective insurance, from the
  manufacturers. The small-lift end of the market quotes bond as a percentage of contract value
  instead, at roughly six to nine times the manufacturers' effective rate.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Product class and manufacturer. The largest single lever in the corpus was a manufacturer
   substitution recorded on a leveling sheet at about nineteen percent of the base bid. A product
   class conversion on a single car was valued by an estimator at roughly fifteen to twenty percent of
   that car. Both are choices made after the drawings are done.
2. Stops, and therefore rise. Cost per stop is not linear. It falls as stop count rises, because the
   machine, controller and cab amortise over more stops. On one project the estimator's own derived
   per-stop rates had the four-stop car about fifteen percent more expensive per stop than the
   six-stop car in the same building.
3. Capacity, speed and cab size, which move together and set the machine.
4. Scope breadth on the by-others boundary, which is invisible in the base bid and is where the
   leveling sheets are weakest.
5. Ride time and operator hours.
6. Finish package. Stainless entrances at every landing versus stainless at the main landing only is a
   standard value-engineering move that bidders offer unprompted. Custom third-party cab interiors are
   several times a premium factory package.
7. Maintenance and warranty term, and whose maintenance specification governs.
8. Temporary use, if it happens.
9. Tariff, freight and escalation exposure.
10. Bond and insurance basis.
11. Local hiring, residency and public-sourcing compliance, which can disqualify rather than price.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within a single new-construction package the
spread between the lowest and highest cost per stop ran 1.09 times on the tightest project and 1.56,
1.68 and 1.72 times on the others. That is dramatically tighter than a painting bid list, and it is
worth understanding why: the product is engineered and configured rather than estimated, so bidders
are pricing nearly the same thing. Bidder counts per project ran from three to eight companies, with
the leveling sheets often listing ten or twelve rows because each revision round is ranked separately.
A bidder count taken off a ranking table will overstate the list, sometimes by double.

Three dispersion facts that are not about price competitiveness:

- The tightest spread in the corpus came from the project where the estimator's own sheet carried the
  fewest scope rows and applied no plugs at all. Tight spreads can mean the sheet is not asking hard
  enough questions.
- Where an independent consultant wrote the modernization scope, the two bidders who priced it landed
  within one percent of each other. Scope definition compresses spread far more than competition does.
- Decommissioning of existing cars is the widest-dispersion scope family in the trade, running roughly
  seven times between the lowest and highest bidder for nominally the same cars, and it does not track
  scope breadth in the obvious direction: the most expensive bidder explicitly excluded the rip-out.
  Nobody agrees what decommissioning includes. One bidder spelled out its operations, and that list is
  the closest thing to a definition available: apply for the state decommission permit, pin all
  hoistway doors, land the counterweight, chain fall the car down onto the buffers, remove the hoist
  ropes, and get the state inspection. Any scope line for decommissioning should name those
  operations, or the numbers will not mean the same thing.

Two figures worth carrying. An estimator budgeting a new elevator package before bids will use a flat
dollars-per-stop rate applied to the stop count, split by building area. And ride time is budgeted at
a fixed hourly rate that did not change between projects, with only the hours estimated per job, at
two hundred to three hundred and twenty hours for a two to three car project.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about thirty-eight elevator and lift proposals from thirteen companies,
across four multifamily and mixed-use projects in one regional market, all bid by one general
contractor, plus five estimator leveling tabs covering traction elevators, traction modernization and
lifts. The proposals span design development, permit, construction documents and conformed sets, and
the corpus includes several full revision ladders, one running four rounds. Three of the four projects
are new construction and one is a modernization of an existing building that also carries a new lift
and the decommissioning of three existing cars.

The bidder pool covers five distinct kinds of company: manufacturers bidding direct, independent
full-service elevator contractors, independent installers reselling a manufacturer's product line,
dealer-installers of a lift manufacturer, and a lift manufacturer selling direct. Establishing which
kind a bidder is before comparing anything is necessary here, because an installer reselling a
competitor's product means the bid list contains fewer distinct manufacturers than it contains
companies.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from mid-rise residential and mixed-use work in one market through one general
  contractor. Nothing is tested against high-rise, where gearless traction, dispatch systems, sky
  lobbies and destination dispatch would change the equipment and probably the leveling basis.
  Nothing is tested against hospital, institutional, industrial or public prevailing-wage work.
- No escalators, moving walks, dumbwaiters, freight elevators or material lifts appear at all. A
  freight elevator would bring vertical bi-parting entrances and their separate power feed, which is
  mentioned in the by-others lists but never priced.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which of the
  by-others items actually got bought back and by whom, or which exclusions survived the descope call.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the site-readiness and dimension-verification
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed, so treat it as untested here.
- No completed drawing set was read alongside these proposals, so the conflicts recorded in section 3
  are the bidders' own accounts of what the drawings show, not verified against the sheets.
- The corpus has not shown a project where a general contractor plugged the by-others items before
  comparing bids. The recommendation in section 3 that those items become scope rows is therefore a
  reading of the gap rather than an observation of practice.
- The corpus has not shown seismic-category work, flood-zone work, or a project where filed sub-bid or
  domestic-sourcing rules actually applied, so what those requirements cost is unknown here; only that
  the market refuses them.
- Only one document in the corpus separated equipment cost from installation labour, and it was a
  small lift, so the material and labour split for traction elevators rests on nothing.
- The proprietary versus non-proprietary control system distinction rests on one modernization
  proposal making the commitment and the manufacturers' standard terms making the opposite one. What
  the difference is worth over a service life is not observable in this corpus.
