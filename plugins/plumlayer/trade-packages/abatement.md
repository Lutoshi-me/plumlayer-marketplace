# Abatement

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the asbestos, lead and
regulated-materials abatement package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals
and estimator leveling sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior
is, is carried by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says
so.

Read this before reading the drawings, and then accept the first hard fact about this trade: the
drawings are not the scope document. A hazardous materials survey is. The set will tell you a
building is being gutted and will not tell you that a fifth of the abatement money sits in roof
sealants, that a bathroom ceiling row means one floor or ten depending on which survey you read, or
that the abatement contractor is forbidden by regulation to certify its own work. Everything that
decides this package sits in documents the architect did not draw.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI 028000 is the anchor, with 028200 used interchangeably for the asbestos portion. Both live in
Division 02 alongside demolition, and the two are commonly bid by the same company. That shared
bidder list is the most misleading fact about the trade and the reason a leveling sheet built off
folder contents will overstate the market.

### Establish the kind of company before comparing anything

Bidder kind drives packaging here more than scope does, and five kinds recur:

- The licensed environmental remediation specialist. Abatement is its whole business. It prices the
  survey's material table row by row, cites its state licence number on the face of the proposal,
  names the statutory notification period, and does not bid demolition.
- The demolition contractor holding an abatement licence. It prices both as two numbers on one
  document. Its abatement scope is usually a single sentence citing a survey, and its qualification
  block is its standing demolition template, which is why its abatement bids exclude lintels,
  excavation and site fencing.
- The environmental consultant that also contracts. It bids the abatement and attaches five pages of
  professional-services terms that cap its liability at its own fee and run a hazardous-materials
  indemnity back to the client.
- The owner-operator specialist. One or two people, an email-based proposal, real licensing, and the
  most direct questions of anyone on the list.
- The third-party monitor. Not a competitor at all. Section 5 covers it, and it is bought by the
  owner rather than by the general contractor.

Scope breadth and price rank track kind more than they track efficiency. On one gut renovation the
abatement line ranged better than five to one across seventeen companies pricing the same survey, and
the top of that range was a bidder carrying the smallest stated quantities on the sheet.

### The packaging decision is the general contractor's, not the market's

Most demolition contractors in this market carry an abatement licence and will price both. They price
them as two numbers on one document and let the general contractor decide whether to buy them
together or apart. A general contractor was seen asking a bidder outright whether it wanted a
combined number or separate proposals, and answering its own question by asking for both, stand-alone
and combinable. Combining is not free: a combined figure offered late in a negotiation collapses
three rounds of breakout and makes the abatement line unrecoverable from that round alone.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package:

- Universal waste, meaning fluorescent bulbs, ballasts, mercury switches and thermostats. Answered
  four different ways across one bid list: priced inside the abatement lump, priced as its own
  allowance, excluded by name, or silent. All four read identically at the total.
- PCB-containing caulking and sealants. Commonly carried by the specialists and commonly excluded by
  the demolition-side bidders on the same list.
- Refrigerant recovery. Occasionally carried, usually as one stated mobilization.
- Demolition to reach the hazardous material. Genuinely contested, and section 5 treats it as the
  defining seam.
- Regulated waste generally, sometimes carried as a round allowance rather than a price, which is a
  third state between carried and excluded.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like abatement:

- Third-party air monitoring, the post-abatement visual inspection and the final clearance. Excluded
  by every bidder seen, without exception, because regulation forbids an abatement contractor from
  clearing its own work.
- The hazardous materials survey itself. Excluded by everyone, and several state on the face of the
  proposal that a survey is required for all demolitions.
- Lead paint, where it is treated as licensed abatement. See section 5 for the two treatments.
- Contaminated soil, storage tanks, free liquids and licensed site professional services. Excluded by
  everyone.
- The abatement work plan, in one case. A bidder excluded the regulatory work plan itself, which is a
  deliverable the project cannot proceed without.

## 2. How the market bids

There is a rule that survives everything else in this section: the survey supplies the quantities, so
this is the one part of the demolition family where bidders enumerate. Whether that enumeration makes
the bids comparable is a separate question, and the answer is usually no.

Proposal shapes, in rough order of how common they are:

- The survey material row list. The bidder reproduces the survey's material table, quantity by
  quantity, and prices the whole list as one lump sum. The most common shape among specialists and
  the most useful one to receive.
- One number citing a survey by name and date, with no quantities at all. Common among the
  demolition-side bidders. Nothing about such a number is checkable.
- Priced by regulated material class: asbestos, then PCB, then mercury, as three lines. Uncommon and
  valuable, because those three classes get bought and excluded independently.
- Priced by survey page range. One bidder split its number at a page boundary of the consultant's
  report. Precise against that one issue of that one report, and meaningless the moment a second
  consultant's report enters the project, which is what happened.
- Unit priced per material row. Almost never in a proposal. Where it appears it is on the general
  contractor's own answered leveling tab, and section 7 explains why those rates are the most useful
  numbers in the trade.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The basis survey, by consultant name and date. Nearly always, and it is the single most important
  field on the document. Section 6 explains why it is also the least reliable.
- The labor basis, union or open shop. Nearly always, and it is the largest single price variable.
  Prevailing wage appears as a third basis with no column for it on the sheet.
- Containment, negative air, decontamination unit, wet methods, HEPA vacuums, double bagging, and
  disposal at a licensed facility. Near-universal and largely uninformative: it is what the
  regulation requires and every bidder recites it.
- Notification to the state agencies. Commonly, and better bidders name the statutory working-day
  clock rather than burying it.
- One mobilization, one phase, first shift, straight time. Near-universal as a pricing basis.
- That the owner or general contractor supplies power and water. Near-universal, and the good bidders
  state it to the phase and amperage per floor rather than as a courtesy.
- Who takes the scrap. Nearly always the bidder.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Any unit price. Rare in a proposal even where every quantity is enumerated.
- Duration or crew size, unless asked by email. Where asked, answers came back as ranges from about
  two weeks to three weeks on a small retail teardown.
- Coat, layer or depth assumptions on the material itself, except at the PCB seam.
- Whether the survey's assumed materials are inside the number. Almost never volunteered, and it is
  the largest silent scope difference in the trade.
- Waste manifesting, occasionally excluded by name while disposal is included, which cannot both be
  true.

Exclusion behavior in this trade has a specific pathology worth naming on its own. Bidders that hold
both a demolition and an abatement licence run their standing demolition exclusion template under a
priced abatement scope, and that template excludes asbestos. Documents were seen pricing an abatement
line on one page while the boilerplate on the next excluded testing and removal of ACM, bulbs,
ballasts, lead-based paint and PCBs; excluding hazardous materials and waste directly beneath a priced
asbestos scope; and asserting that all spoils are assumed clean and unregulated four lines below a
priced asbestos scope. The priced breakout is authoritative and the exclusion list is a pasted
template. Never level off exclusion lists in this trade, and never let an automated reader treat an
exclusion line as a scope statement.

Revision habits, and the causes are worth telling apart because three of the five move no money:

- Breakout restructure. A bidder issues one combined demolition-and-abatement figure, is asked to
  split it, and returns a document that is otherwise byte-identical, same proposal number, same date.
  Zero money moves and it is the most consequential revision seen: the split revealed an abatement
  line barely half the next lowest bid on the sheet.
- Commercial basis restatement. A second complete configuration priced on the assumption an
  owner-controlled insurance program applies, every rate lower by the same percentage, scope
  identical. The two configurations are a menu, and a captured unit price without its configuration
  is unusable.
- Scope departure as an alternate. A base falls by about two fifths between rounds while the roof
  package leaves it and reappears as an add worth almost exactly the drop. The total diff reads a
  saving where none exists. The same bidder later folded the alternate back and the base rose again,
  so three rounds describe a constant offer.
- Sequence concession. A discount of roughly a tenth on a combined number, conditioned entirely on
  being allowed to defer the roof work until a material hoist is operational. No scope changes.
- Reconfirmation. A three-month-old bid re-dated against a conformed set with the cited drawings
  unchanged, differing from its predecessor in exactly two places: the printed date and a revision
  suffix on the job number. A re-dated bid is not evidence that anybody re-read anything.

Two further shapes are not revisions at all and must not be diffed. A bidder may issue an email round
that carries no price, because it is escalating a documents gap rather than pricing one. And the
general contractor may file the identical document under both the demolition and the abatement trade
tokens, which produces byte-identical pairs by the dozen. Hash before counting bidders.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Abatement scope sheets have run about forty rows on a large gut and about sixty rows on a small
teardown, and the inversion is the point: the small job needs more rows because it has fewer material
classes and more unanswered boundary questions. On the gut, the survey's material table was pasted in
and did most of the work.

That paste is worth pausing on. When the survey's own material table becomes the scope sheet, the
project silently adopts the consultant's taxonomy, the consultant's locations, the consultant's units
and the consultant's assumptions about what was never opened. Every one of those is a decision, and
none of them was made by the general contractor. Target roughly the survey's row count plus a block of
fifteen to twenty rows the survey will never contain, listed below under what enumerates.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every material row the survey states, at the survey's own location granularity. This is the
  irreducible core and it is the only part the market will answer consistently.
- Observed quantity and assumed quantity, as two separate rows for the same material. This is the
  single most valuable split in the trade. Surveys carry a table of what was tested and quantified and
  a second block of material assumed present behind walls, above ceilings and in areas not opened. One
  bidder was seen writing the assumed block out of its flooring row on the face of the bid, carrying
  roughly a fifth less flooring than the field and reading as competitively priced; another priced the
  assumed materials as two separate add alternates, one for abatement and one for demolition, which is
  the instrument to ask everyone for. Every other bidder answered the question invisibly inside a lump
  sum.
- Materials that tested negative but must be treated as asbestos anyway, as their own rows. These
  arrive because a contaminated layer above them cannot be separated. On one project the answers split
  four ways across the bid list: yes, no, a question mark, and an assertion that the main flooring
  quantity already encompassed them. The general contractor was still asking the question weeks later.
- Each regulated material class as its own row: asbestos, PCB, lead, mercury components, universal
  waste. They are bought, excluded and priced independently, and a single hazardous materials row
  will be answered differently by everyone.
- For PCB caulking and sealant, the substrate depth as an explicit quantity. A bidder priced six
  inches of substrate either side of the joint in its base and twenty-four inches as an add, then
  stated that if a second round of testing came back positive the price would have to be renegotiated
  depending on concentration. The assumed depth is the price, and the escalation path is open-ended.
- The roof, as its own block and never inside a general abatement line. Roof sealants, flashing
  compounds, coping and parapet seam sealants, pitch pockets, box window glazing compound and vent
  pipe cement are small quantities at the highest unit rates in the trade, and they leave and re-enter
  base bids as a package. On one project the roof block ran between a fifth and a third of the
  abatement base depending on the bidder.
- Demolition to access, split by what conceals the material: plaster ceilings adhered to structure,
  terracotta above ceilings, furring and perimeter walls, fixed casework, wall track fastened into ACM
  flooring. Section 5 explains why a single access line is unanswerable.
- Containment count and configuration. A bidder asked outright whether one full containment or two
  smaller ones was preferred, which is a priced design decision nothing in the documents answers.
- Mobilizations, with a stated fee per additional mobilization. Enabling work for a hoist, floor-by-
  floor work after demolition releases a floor, and refrigerant recovery were each a separate
  mobilization on one bid. Stated additional-mobilization fees varied by more than two to one between
  competitors on the same building.
- Shift, explicitly. One bidder priced second shift as its basis for a reason invisible on any
  drawing: getting asbestos waste trailers loaded and clear of a dense downtown street.
- The regulatory notification, as its own row, with its working-day clock and its fee.
- The abatement work plan and any non-traditional work plan, as their own rows. They were answered
  not required on one project and excluded outright on another.
- The industrial hygienist and the third-party clearance, as two separate rows even though this
  package cannot carry either. See section 5.
- Post-abatement floor preparation: adhesive and mastic removal, levelling compound removal, grinding
  and shot blasting, each by name. Nearly every bidder stops at the ACM and excludes each further
  step, and the flooring package expects a prepared substrate.
- Disposal manifests and closeout documentation, separate from disposal.
- Access resources as priced rows: which elevator and for how long, whether the hoist exists and when,
  whether waste loads out through a hoist or an elevator or a chute, whether debris may be live loaded
  from the street.
- The occupied portion of the building, as an explicit exclusion row. Where a tenant stays in
  occupation, at least one bidder will price work inside it anyway.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Room by room enumeration. No document seen prices a room. The survey's own location granularity,
  which is typically a floor band or a named area, is the floor.
- Containment setup, negative air, decontamination units, wet methods, HEPA vacuums, double bagging
  and labelling. Every bidder recites the same list because regulation requires it. Collapse to one
  procedural line per containment.
- Means and methods within a material row. Glove bag versus full containment matters where it changes
  the mobilization, not as its own row.
- The waste stream split by destination facility. Internal to disposal unless a diversion target is
  being tracked.
- Individual fitting, bulb or ballast counts below the material row. The count is an attribute of the
  row, and it is the leveling denominator, not a set of rows.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In abatement
that test fires hardest on the observed-versus-assumed split, on the regulated material classes other
than asbestos, on the roof block, and on demolition to access.

There is a second test specific to this trade, and it is about documents rather than work. For every
material row, ask which document says so, which issue of it, and whether any other document on the
project says something different. A scope row that names a material without pinning its source will be
answered against whichever survey the bidder happened to open.

Silence is a finding here more sharply than in most trades, because the survey creates an expectation
that everything is enumerated. A bidder silent on the roof block, on the assumed materials, or on PCB
is not offering a cheaper version of the same scope. It is offering a different scope, and only a row
on the sheet makes that visible.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the party that must pick the work up, and
if no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| Excluded by the abatement contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Third-party air monitoring, post-abatement visual inspection and final air clearance (every bidder seen, without exception) | An independent monitoring firm hired by the owner or general contractor, because regulation forbids self-clearance |
| The hazardous materials survey (every bidder seen) | Owner or general contractor, via an environmental consultant |
| The industrial hygienist (most bidders, though a minority carry the fee inside their own number) | Owner or general contractor |
| Any material not identified in the cited survey (every bidder seen) | Nobody, until a change order |
| Unidentified ACM discovered during demolition (nearly every bidder) | A change order, and this is the trade's largest open risk |
| Materials testing below one percent asbestos content (some bidders, by name) | Unassigned, and it is a real third category that is neither regulated material nor ordinary debris in the bidder's reading |
| Lead paint abatement (most bidders; some carry lead under occupational worker-protection procedure instead) | See section 5; the two treatments are not the same purchase |
| PCB remediation (about half, while the specialists commonly carry it) | A specialty subcontractor, or unassigned |
| Mercury components and radioactive exit lights (about half) | Unassigned, or folded into universal waste |
| Demolition to access ACM on surfaces noted to remain (commonly, including by bidders holding both packages) | Contested, and frequently unowned |
| Plaster ceilings adhered to structure and residual terracotta above ceilings (some bidders) | Unassigned, and these are the layers concealing the ACM |
| Mastic, adhesive, levelling compound removal, grinding and shot blasting (nearly every bidder) | Flooring package, or unassigned |
| Patching, repair and preparation of surfaces for new finishes after abatement (nearly every bidder) | The following trade, and it is a recurring real gap |
| Power and water (nearly every bidder, several specifying phase and amperage per floor) | General contractor |
| Negative air provision (rarely, and it is a warning sign when it appears) | General contractor, though every other bidder carries it inside containment setup |
| Temporary protection, poly, barriers and weather protection (most bidders) | General contractor, frequently unassigned |
| Permits and fees, sidewalk and overhead protection permits (commonly) | General contractor |
| Police and fire details (every bidder seen) | General contractor |
| Cutting, capping and make-safe of utilities (every bidder seen) | The building systems trades |
| Slab scanning, x-ray and layout (nearly every bidder) | General contractor |
| Shoring, bracing, lintels, scaffolding and staging (nearly every bidder) | Specialist, or general contractor |
| Hoist and elevator operator time (commonly, while requiring dedicated use of both) | General contractor |
| Salvage for owner or general contractor reuse, and its storage and reinstallation (most bidders, while claiming scrap title in the same document) | Unassigned |
| Historic items and conservation (occasionally, in a single blanket line) | General contractor, and where the sheet carries a large protect-and-salvage block this leaves it unanswered |
| Overtime, premium time, second shift and multiple mobilizations (nearly every bidder) | General contractor, priced as a change |
| Bonds (nearly every bidder, quoting an adder as a percentage of contract value) | General contractor, priced as an alternate |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table is boilerplate, and in this trade a specific kind: the demolition template running
under an abatement scope. A repeat bidder reproduces the same list verbatim across projects and
building types, and language circulates between competitors. Read it for the trade map, not for the
project, and where it contradicts a priced line, believe the priced line.

What is project signal, because it was written for this building:

- A per-row annotation naming which document supports the row. One bidder annotated five material rows
  with which of two competing surveys did or did not show them, and one row with the fact that the
  second survey showed it on a single floor where the first showed it on ten. That document is the
  best leveling artifact in the corpus and also the clearest proof the project has a documents
  problem.
- A stated assumption about extent that no document fixes: the depth of contamination into a
  substrate, the flooring area abated per floor to enable a hoist, the number of finish layers.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the report. When a bidder writes that the report identifies
  components but supplies no square or linear footage, so it cannot price them, that is a documents
  problem to route upstream, not a leveling adjustment to absorb.
- A recommendation that further testing be done. Bidders recommend additional testing on windows,
  envelope caulking and facade sealants, and recommend that the differences between two surveys be
  clarified. These are the project's request-for-information list, pre-written by the market.
- An assumption naming another company on the same bid list as performing a seam. One bid assumed all
  demolition-to-access would be done by a named competitor, an assumption no document supported, which
  vanished in the next round and reappeared as an exclusion.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

Violating these converts the lump sum into a change order.

- The cited survey is the scope, and nothing outside it is included. Universal, and it is the whole
  commercial architecture of the trade. One bidder wrote the boundary precisely, excluding hazardous
  materials to the extent they are not quantified and included in our proposal, which converts a
  boilerplate exclusion into a statement that the priced quantities are the contract.
- Quantities are the survey's, and the survey's assumed block may or may not be inside them.
- One mobilization, one phase, first shift. Near-universal, with a stated fee per additional
  mobilization where the bidder is thinking about phasing at all.
- Continuous, exclusive access to the work area, with occupants and non-fixed furniture removed by
  others. Universal, and it is the assumption that fails first in an occupied building.
- Power and water by others, commonly specified to phase and amperage per floor. An abatement
  containment is an electrical load, not a courtesy.
- A dedicated elevator or an operational hoist for waste loadout, for the duration. Common on
  multi-storey work, and it collides directly with the elevator being a demolition object and with
  another bidder excluding all costs of using the elevator.
- Floors turned over broom swept before abatement, and demolition-to-access complete.
- Straight time, with premium shifts excluded, except where a bidder prices second shift as its basis
  for site-logistics reasons.
- The bidder takes all scrap and salvageable metal, sometimes explicitly valued against metal market
  prices at the proposal date, so the offer erodes as the market moves.
- Insurance basis. Whether an owner-controlled program applies moved every unit rate on one bid by the
  same five percent.
- Disposal cost movement. One bidder priced disposal at current rates and reserved against an
  uncertain market; another published a rate card stating that disposal charges depend on the nature
  of the material and the location of the facility.
- Commercial terms that change what a number is worth. Bidders were seen requiring the general
  contractor to accept every exclusion or the proposal is void; asserting that the proposal
  constitutes the entire agreement and supersedes the contract documents; requiring their own counsel
  to approve both the subcontract and the general contractor's prime contract with the owner;
  refusing pay-when-paid; capping retainage or refusing it; charging interest and collection costs on
  overdue balances; and capping total liability at their own fee while running a hazardous-materials
  indemnity back to the client. Capture these, because a proposal that supersedes the specification
  makes any per-specification scope reading worth nothing.
- Award conditions that break a package. A bid void unless every exclusion is accepted conflicts
  structurally with how a general contractor actually buys, which is row by row across several
  bidders plus plugs.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

Abatement has almost no furnish component and an unusually formal custody structure, because the
seams are set by regulation rather than by market convention. Three of them cannot be negotiated at
all.

### The survey is the hinge

Before a survey exists, this package does not exist as a priceable scope. Every bidder excludes
hazardous materials, states that it assumed none are present, and offers to price once a report is
provided. On one pre-survey bid list the abatement row carried a dash for quantity, was marked not in
contract, and eight of nine bidders answered no. The ninth carried a labelled budget on standard
assumptions, and because the sheet had no base-bid row for it, that honest bid appeared as an add
rather than as a base. A leveling sheet with no row for the pre-survey abatement risk punishes the
only bidder that priced it.

When the survey lands, the shape changes completely and the abatement becomes the one line in the
demolition family that is quantified. Whether it becomes comparable is section 7's problem, and the
answer there is no.

### Everything that goes wrong with this seam goes wrong inside the survey

- Surveys carry a quantified table and an assumed one. The assumed block covers material behind walls
  and above ceilings that nobody opened. Whether a bidder carried it is invisible in a lump sum, and
  the difference is worth about a fifth of a flooring line.
- Surveys carry a third table of materials that tested negative but must be treated as asbestos
  because a contaminated layer above them cannot be separated. Bidders read this table four different
  ways on one project.
- Surveys quantify some hazards and merely identify others. A lead section was seen naming affected
  components with no footage for any of them. Instructed to price whatever the report shows, no more
  and no less, a bidder answered that the report does not show enough to price. That scope was still
  unpriced at the end of the round.
- Surveys get revised, and a second consultant may produce a second survey that disagrees with the
  first. On one project five distinct basis-document identifications circulated across the bid list:
  two consultants, a supplementary summary table with its own date, a date used by only one bidder,
  and a survey named after the ownership entity with a date matching nothing. Bidders priced different
  ones. Two of them cited both and said the quantities differ.
- Bidders restate the survey's counts in ways that do not reconcile with the survey or with each
  other. Three bidders read a bathroom ceiling row at ten times the quantity the sheet carried, a
  discrepancy that is one floor against ten. One bidder carried a pipe fitting count at double the
  field's by listing the same fittings twice under two descriptions. One carried a glazing compound
  row at more than ten times the field's quantity and in a different unit. Another restated four
  linear-foot rows in square feet.
- A bidder may price from its own quantity table entirely. One carried the smallest asbestos
  quantities on a project, sharing not a single figure with either survey, including scope inside an
  occupied tenant area every competitor excluded, and returned the highest abatement number on the
  sheet. A proposal can look more thorough than its competitors and be less connected to the project's
  documents than the one-line bids beside it.

Route all of this upstream. Two surveys that disagree is a request for information, not a leveling
adjustment, and a general contractor was seen asking the question directly on its own sheet: any
inconsistencies in the reports versus what was seen on site. The one answer recorded was that
additional material may be encountered as walls are opened.

### The three seams regulation sets

- Third-party monitoring and clearance. The abatement contractor is forbidden to inspect and clear its
  own work, so this is bought separately, commonly from the firm that wrote the survey. It is a
  service priced as a rate card, not a lump sum: a day rate per monitor shift, an hourly project
  manager rate, a per-sample analysis charge, and a budget derived from an estimated shift count. The
  shift count is set by the abatement contractor's schedule, which the proposal says outright, so a
  slower abater costs the owner more in monitoring. That is a cross-package cost dependency no
  document assigns. Note also who buys it: on one project the monitoring proposal was addressed to the
  owner at the general contractor's own instruction, and the general contractor coordinated a package
  it did not purchase. And it is easy to miss entirely. On that project the same firm's earlier
  contracted engagement covered the survey and not the monitoring, and the gap surfaced months later
  only because the owner's representative noticed that the abatement contract it had signed did not
  include monitoring the abatement.
- The notification clock. A statutory working-day notice, ten working days in this market, runs
  before abatement can begin. It sits in front of everything and it is invisible on the drawings. The
  filing also needs the monitor's licence number and the inspector's licence number, so the monitoring
  buy sits in front of the notification, which sits in front of the abatement. Better bidders flag the
  clock as a schedule item; a leveling sheet was seen asking whether a bidder had already applied.
- Licensing. Every worker licensed, the company licensed, and on public-adjacent work state
  prequalification and diversity certification besides. This is a fourth click-test state: a bidder
  that is not certified is not expensive, it is unbiddable. Abatement is an early, self-contained,
  labor-heavy package, which makes it a common vehicle for meeting a participation goal, and a
  certified bidder can outrank a cheaper one on a basis nothing in the documents shows.

### Lead is two different purchases wearing one word

This distinction decides real money and it is easy to miss.

Treated as licensed abatement, lead is a separate certificated scope, priced from a survey, and
excluded by most bidders. Treated under occupational worker-protection procedure, the same material is
handled inside the demolition number: the crew works to the lead-in-construction standard, and the
waste goes out as general construction debris subject to a leachate characterisation test performed
by others. One bidder wrote that distinction out in full. Others reached the same place informally,
one reasoning that because the building was coming down the small amounts of lead-impacted material
should pass the test and go with the demolition debris, another asserting the demolition subcontractor
should carry it with a leachate test in its scope, a third excluding it as leachate testing only. The
leveling sheet needs to know which of the two it bought, and on one project the estimator's own cell
on that row was written as a question rather than an answer.

### Demolition to access, the seam that goes unowned

Somebody must open the wall, drop the ceiling and strip the finish before the abater can reach the
material, and somebody must put the surfaces back afterwards. Neither end reliably has an owner.

The failure mode is specific. A bidder holding both the demolition and the abatement scope still
excluded demolition required to access asbestos on surfaces noted to remain, so the work had no owner
anywhere on the bid list. Another named a specific competitor as performing all demolition-to-access,
an assumption no document supported, then in the next round downgraded it to by others and added it to
its own exclusion list at the same time. A third excluded plaster ceilings at the deck and residual
terracotta above ceilings, which are precisely the layers concealing the ACM behind them. Only one
bidder on that project answered the general contractor's own written question about how a sub-ceiling
adhered to terracotta comes off, and it answered by accepting the damage risk explicitly.

The reverse direction is just as contested. Patching, repair and preparation of surfaces for new
finishes after abatement is excluded by nearly everyone.

### Other seams

- The flooring boundary. The abatement scope is normally tile and its mastic together, and one bidder
  excluded mastic adhesive removal in the same document that priced the tile-and-mastic row. Beyond
  the mastic sit levelling compound removal, grinding and shot blasting, excluded by name by nearly
  everybody, and the flooring package expects a prepared substrate. Where the gap is priced it appears
  as a square-foot grinding alternate, and two bidders on one building quoted grinding rates within
  about forty percent of each other.
- Wall track fastened into ACM flooring. One bidder claimed the track bases into its own abatement
  scope because they are attached to the ACM and must come out inside containment. A demolition bid
  pricing the same track double-counts, and a demolition scope silent on it leaves the abater carrying
  demolition work.
- The roof, contested three ways. Roof sealants and flashings are commonly on the abatement survey,
  built-up roofing is demolition's, and the new roof is the roofer's, so one roof can split three
  ways. One bidder limited itself to a single layer of roofing and disclaimed hot-mop asphalt and
  mastics adhered to the substrate. Roof ballast and gravel moved in both directions on one project,
  carried as a priced removal by one bidder and deducted as by-others by another at a comparable
  figure.
- The mechanical, electrical and plumbing drop. The trades cut, cap, make safe and drop to the floor
  in manageable pieces, commonly stated as four to six foot lengths, and the abatement or demolition
  contractor removes, disposes, and keeps whatever non-salvage material the trades leave behind. The
  cleanest statements of this seam in the corpus come from this trade.
- Refrigerant recovery, carried by some bidders as one stated mobilization and excluded by others.
- The elevator, simultaneously an access resource and a demolition object. Bidders require dedicated
  use of one for waste loadout while another excludes all costs of using it and a third prices
  decommissioning and removal as separate lines.
- The hoist. On multi-storey work the abatement sequence is built around it: a first enabling
  mobilization abates a stated flooring area and pipe run on each floor so the hoist can be installed,
  and the main abatement follows floor by floor as demolition releases floors. Bidders assume the hoist
  is operational and exclude operator time. A sequencing concession on the hoist was worth about a
  tenth of one combined number.
- Historic fabric. Where a building carries protected material, the abatement bidders exclude
  protection and salvage, sometimes in a single blanket line, while the general contractor's sheet
  carries the block as many separate rows.
- Contaminated soil, tanks and free liquids. Excluded by everyone. No project in this corpus actually
  had them, so the counterparty was never observed.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

Almost all of it. This is the trade where that sentence is closest to literally true.

What abatement contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The hazardous materials survey. The primary and often the only quantity source. Bidders cite it by
   consultant, date, appendix, table or page number, and the good ones tell you which of those they
   used.
2. A site visit. Cited constantly, and one bidder conditioned its whole price on site conditions at
   the time of its walk-through with no responsibility for changes after it. Where a bidder priced
   without a walk, it says so and asks for one before mobilization.
3. The general contractor's issued scope sheet, on larger work, which bidders answer row by row.
4. The demolition drawings, for what is being opened and in what order.
5. A hoist plan and a construction management plan, where they exist, because the abatement sequence
   is built on them.
6. The specification section, cited by some bidders and rarely quoted from, and worth less here than
   elsewhere because several bidders' own terms assert that the proposal supersedes it.

They do not price from the architectural set. They do not measure the building. Every quantity in a
proposal is a restatement of somebody else's document, which is why quantity disagreements between
bidders are almost always document-reading disagreements rather than takeoff disagreements.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of these moves the number:

- Whether a survey exists at all. The single largest determinant of whether the bids on the table
  mean anything.
- Which survey, and whether more than one exists. On one project five basis-document identifications
  circulated and the bidders did not price the same scope.
- Whether the survey's assumed-material block is inside a bidder's number.
- How the survey treats materials that tested negative but sit under a contaminated layer.
- Union versus open shop, the largest single driver, with prevailing wage as a third basis. Market
  recovery subsidy from a benefit fund is a real instrument here and it can fail: one union bidder
  approached its fund and was told no money was available because the project address was not
  grandfathered and would require fully union labor.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies. Worth a uniform percentage across every rate
  on one bid.
- Whether the building is occupied, and whether a tenant area is in or out. On one gut the leveling
  denominator explicitly subtracted an occupied tenant's area, and one bidder priced work inside it
  anyway.
- The containment strategy: one large containment or several small ones, and how many mobilizations.
- Which shift, and why. Waste loadout logistics on a dense street drove one bidder to second shift as
  its pricing basis.
- Whether a hoist or a dedicated elevator will exist, when, and who operates it.
- Whether demolition-to-access is somebody's scope.
- Who supplies power and water, to what phase and amperage, on which floors.
- What is actually behind the finishes, which in this trade means whether more regulated material
  turns up when walls open. Every bidder excludes it and nobody prices it.
- The regulatory notification clock, and whether a notification is already open.
- Whether the project carries a participation goal, and whether the bidder is certified.
- Disposal cost movement, and whether the bidder has reserved against it.

Set-stage behavior is dominated by the survey rather than by the drawing rounds. Before the survey the
package is a budget line or a no. After it, the material rows appear and the proposals get long. Note
that the survey's own timeline is independent of the drawing set's: a second survey can land months
after a construction-document set and reset the whole package while the drawings sit still. Order
rounds by the survey each bid cites, not by the filename, the folder stage or the letterhead date, and
be ready for that to fail too, because bidders cite the same survey under different dates and one
bidder cited its base scope against one survey and its own alternates against another inside a single
document.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet, for flooring, panelling, ceiling materials, duct wrap and most roof sealant areas. The
  dominant unit.
- Linear feet, for pipe insulation runs, coping and parapet seam sealants, glazing compound and
  caulking joints. Routinely confused with square feet across bidders restating the same survey rows,
  so check the unit column before comparing a quantity.
- Each, for pipe fittings, pitch pockets, bulbs, ballasts, mercury switches, windows and fire door
  cores. In small packages the fitting count is the leveling denominator.
- Lump sum, for the package as a whole. The dominant commercial unit despite the enumeration.
- Shifts, hours and per-sample charges, for the third-party monitoring package.
- Mobilizations, with a stated fee for each one beyond the assumed count.
- Days, for negative air machines, HEPA vacuums, decontamination units, chutes and containers on a
  published rate card.
- Square feet, for post-abatement floor grinding, always as an alternate rate.
- Gross square feet of building, as the leveling denominator, with any occupied area named and
  subtracted. On a small teardown the abatement tab borrowed the demolition package's building area
  rather than measuring abated area, so the denominator is a choice and it must be stated.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, quoted between one and a half and three percent by the same
  firm depending on project size, so it is not a fixed company number.

The single most useful pricing fact in this trade sits on an answered leveling tab rather than in any
proposal. Where one bidder unit-priced the survey's material rows, the rates ran about thirty to one
between material classes on the same building, in a consistent direction: large open flooring areas
were the cheapest per unit and by far the largest line by value; small, high-access, sealant-class
items on a roof or at a window were the most expensive per unit and trivial by value; and mechanical
closet materials sat in between at roughly a dozen times the flooring rate. Abatement cost tracks the
material class and the access to it, not the area. An estimator plugging this trade at a blended rate
per square foot will be wrong in both directions at once.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Scope definition, meaning which survey, which tables, and whether the assumed block is in. It
   outranks price as a cause of ranking movement by a wide margin.
2. Labor basis, union versus open shop versus prevailing wage. On one project a bidder published its
   full burdened build-up: base wage, statutory taxes itemised, workers compensation, general
   liability and umbrella, paid leave and sick time, then eleven separately named union benefit funds
   worth about two thirds of the base wage again, then overhead and profit. That build-up is why the
   open-shop and union numbers on the same sheet diverge the way they do. It also shows that the
   overtime premium applies to base and taxes only, so a time-and-a-half hour costs about thirty
   percent more on the burdened rate rather than fifty, and a double-time hour about sixty percent
   more rather than a hundred.
3. Whether the roof block is in the base, out of it, or in an alternate. Worth between a fifth and a
   third of an abatement base.
4. Material class mix, per the rates above.
5. Access, sequence and phasing. Hoist availability, elevator dedication, waste loadout route,
   containment count and mobilization count. A sequencing concession was worth about a tenth of a
   combined number, and stated additional-mobilization fees varied by more than two to one between
   competitors.
6. Whether the regulated classes other than asbestos are in: PCB, lead, mercury, universal waste.
7. Shift and the reason for it.
8. Demolition to access, and post-abatement floor preparation.
9. Insurance basis, worth a uniform percentage across all rates.
10. Occupancy and phasing constraints.
11. Commercial terms: bond, retainage, pay-when-paid, disposal escalation, salvage value.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. Within one small retail teardown the abatement bids
ranged about one and eight tenths times from lowest to highest across eight companies, tightening to
about one and a half times after the estimator plugged the unpriced regulated classes into every
number. Within one large gut renovation the range was better than five to one per gross square foot
across seventeen companies, all nominally pricing one quantified survey.

State plainly what a spread in this trade does not prove, because the five-to-one case is the
important one. The survey being quantified does not make the bids comparable. On that project the
lowest number was a line broken out of a combined figure at the general contractor's request, barely
half the next lowest, with no material list behind it. The highest carried the smallest stated
quantities on the sheet, priced scope inside an occupied area everyone else excluded, and added three
material classes no survey row supported. In between, two bidders excluded the roof block entirely,
one excluded a fifth of the flooring by writing the assumed material out of its own row, three read a
ceiling row at ten times the sheet's quantity, and several cited surveys under names and dates that
matched nothing else on the project. Almost none of that variation is price. Equally, a tight spread
proves nothing on its own: the small teardown's bids converged because the survey there was short and
unambiguous, not because the market agreed on anything, and the two bidders furthest apart on that
sheet were pricing different regulated material classes.

Before ranking anything in this trade, confirm five things: which survey each bid priced, whether the
assumed block is in, whether the roof block is in, which regulated material classes are in, and
whether the printed total is a base bid. Printed totals in this trade roll add alternates, demolition
and abatement into one figure routinely, and a leveling sheet was seen carrying such a total as a base
bid and ranking that bidder last when its own line would have placed it near the front.

Finally, an estimator does not compare base bids here either. It compares base bid plus plugs, and
this trade has an unusual plug mechanism worth knowing: where one bidder prices a scope the rest are
silent on, the estimator copies that bidder's figure across the field as an other-sub value. On one
sheet the PCB substrate alternate was plugged into four competitors and the mercury line into two,
straight off a single competitor's document. The plugs to expect are third-party air monitoring and
final clearance; the industrial hygienist; the hazardous materials survey; the regulated classes other
than asbestos; the assumed-material block; demolition to access; post-abatement floor preparation and
patching; temporary power and water; protection and barriers; permits and fees; the abatement work
plan where a bidder excludes it; and the notification fee. A scope line set naming all of them is
doing the estimator's plugging in advance, and on this trade the plugs frequently exceed the
difference between the top and bottom of the ranking.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly two dozen abatement-native proposals plus a monitoring package and
two answered general contractor leveling tabs, drawn from three projects in one regional market, all
bid by one general contractor, together with three estimator leveling sheets. Because abatement and
demolition are commonly filed as one document under both trade tokens, about a dozen further files in
the same folders are byte-identical duplicates and were read once, through the licensed-trade lens
rather than counted twice. The three projects cover three genuinely different abatement problems: a
single-storey retail teardown with a short, fully quantified survey; an eleven-storey downtown gut
with a live commercial tenant, two competing surveys and seventeen bidders; and a small teardown bid
before any survey existed. Proposals span design development, permit, construction document and
conformed rounds, and several companies appear on more than one project, which is what makes the
boilerplate versus project signal distinction in section 4 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- One market, one general contractor, three projects. Nothing here is tested against public bidding,
  institutional, healthcare or industrial decommissioning work, or any market where abatement is bid
  to an owner directly. Regulatory particulars including the notification clock, the licensing regime
  and the self-clearance prohibition are stated as this market's rules and will differ elsewhere.
- Only one project carried a large multi-storey abatement scope, so the roof block, the hoist-enabling
  sequence, the two-survey conflict and the five-to-one spread all rest on that project alone.
- Only one bidder anywhere in the corpus unit-priced the survey's material rows, so the entire rate
  structure in section 7 rests on a single answered leveling tab. Treat the thirty-to-one ratio
  between material classes as one observation, not a market fact.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus. Nothing shows which exclusions survived
  negotiation, whether the assumed-material block was bought, or who ended up owning
  demolition-to-access.
- No change order history is in the corpus, so the claim that unidentified material discovered during
  demolition becomes a change order is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed.
  The corpus never records what was actually found behind a wall.
- No abatement work plan, submittal package, notification filing, disposal manifest or clearance
  report is in the corpus. Containment design, worker protection levels, air clearance criteria in
  practice, and the manifesting chain are all described from proposal language rather than from the
  documents themselves.
- Disposal appears only as an included cost and, once, as a qualitative note that charges depend on
  the material and the facility. No classification-by-facility rate card of the kind other disposal-
  heavy trades publish appears anywhere, so whether abatement disposal has that structure is unknown.
- Lead abatement as licensed work never happened. Every lead treatment observed was either an
  exclusion or the occupational worker-protection route, so the priced form of licensed lead abatement
  is absent.
- PCB work appears on one project only, at one small joint length, so the substrate-depth instrument
  rests on a single document.
- Only one third-party monitoring proposal is in the corpus, and it was solicited late, so nothing
  here shows how monitoring is normally scoped, competed or timed.
- The corpus has not shown mold remediation, contaminated soil, storage tanks or a licensed site
  professional actually engaged, so those exclusions are recorded without a counterparty ever being
  observed.
- The corpus has not shown abatement in an operating facility that stays fully occupied throughout,
  where phased containment and after-hours work would dominate. The one occupied case was a single
  tenant area carved out of the scope.
