# Finish Carpentry

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the finish carpentry
package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets.
The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real carpenters can bid it and a real estimator can
level it.

One thing governs everything below. Finish carpentry is not primarily a material trade. It is the
building's install labor hub: it receives, distributes and installs the doors, casework, accessories
and specialties that four or five other packages furnish, and it furnishes almost nothing except
running trim and closet shelving. A bidder said it plainly when asked about warranty duration, that
it could not evaluate the question because the vast majority of the scope is the installation of
items furnished by others and most warranty work in this trade is material failure. Every scope line
in this package therefore has to answer two questions, not one: what is the work, and who supplies
the thing.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 062000 is the anchor and is what every leveling sheet uses. A proposal that names a
section at all is as likely to name 064020, interior architectural woodwork, and the trade token is
far stronger than any section number. Do not trust a division label on a proposal cover: a door
installation bid arrived headed Division 09 Finishes, describing Division 08 work, filed in the
Division 06 folder.

The package as bid in the market is a bundle of install labor plus one narrow supply scope. What it
reliably furnishes:

- Standing and running trim: base, door casing, window stool and apron, skirt boards, wall caps,
  pencil molding, cased opening jamb and casing trim.
- Closet shelving and rod, in whichever material the project lands on.
- Plywood stair treads and risers where the stairs are wood-framed.

What it reliably installs but does not furnish:

- Doors, frames and hardware. Nearly always the largest install family in the package, and on some
  projects worth as much as everything else combined.
- Unit kitchen and bathroom casework, including hardware, toe kicks and door adjustment.
- Bath accessories, medicine cabinets, mirrors, shower rods, grab bars.
- Fire extinguisher cabinets, mailboxes, and assorted Division 10 specialties.
- Common area and amenity millwork, where a separate fabricator supplies it.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another
division:

- Doors, frames and hardware installation, which is Division 08 material. Universal, and it is the
  reason a Division 08 supplier will sometimes bid the whole carpentry package: one bidder quoted
  the door material, the door install, and the carpentry as three separately priced blocks so the
  general contractor could buy any combination.
- Bath accessories, fire extinguisher cabinets and mailboxes, which are Division 10. Carried by most
  bidders, and where a bidder is silent an estimator plugs it as a matter of course.
- Setting microwaves, cooktops and wall ovens. Carried by most, and one bidder explained why: those
  are part of the cabinet installation and are the appliance scope carpenters claim.
- FRP panelling in trash rooms, janitors closets and bike rooms. Carried by some, excluded by more,
  and an estimator will keep a row for it either way.
- Stair treads, risers and skirt boards, where the stairs are wood. Contested, and a bidder may
  carry the skirts and caps while asking out loud whether the treads belong to rough carpentry.
- Window installation on renovation work. Not part of this trade on new construction, but on a
  renovation the carpenter is the one already inside the opening, and the package can absorb window
  demolition, new window install, weight-box spray foaming and hoist-bay window removal.
- Countertop installation. Rarely carried, occasionally offered as an add on the condition that the
  tops arrive templated, pre-cut and pre-drilled for a simple drop-in.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like carpentry:

- Welded hollow metal frames, which go to the drywall contractor. Excluded by every bidder seen.
  Knock-down frames at stud walls stay with the carpenter, sometimes only as an add.
- Vinyl and rubber base, which go to the flooring package. On one project the entire unit and
  corridor baseboard was vinyl by flooring, which removes the trade's signature product from its own
  scope sheet and leaves the carpenter with almost no supply scope at all.
- Stone, quartz and solid surface countertops, and their subtops and supports, which go to the
  countertop package.
- Metal handrails, metal pan stairs and their structural elements, which go to steel.
- Custom architectural millwork on projects where it is bid as its own package, usually under the
  adjacent millwork section. The boundary moves project to project and is the single most common
  place for the same item to be claimed by two packages.
- Electronic and electrified hardware, access control, key fob programming and closers. Excluded by
  nearly every bidder, and often unassigned afterward.
- Corner guards and wall protection. Excluded by nearly every bidder and a recurring real gap.
- Aluminum and storefront doors, frames and hardware, and all exterior doors.

Beware of the same item being claimed by two packages. A whole finish carpentry bid may be filed
under the millwork or casework token, and a millwork or casework bid may be filed here. One email
transmitted three separate proposals from the same fabricator, for finish carpentry, custom millwork
and unit casework, so that firm appears on two different leveling sheets with two different numbers
covering overlapping work. Before comparing anything, confirm which of the sibling packages each
number is answering.

## 2. How the market bids

This trade is not a one-number trade. The default output is a short list of scope families, each with
its own price, and each label carrying a verb that says whether the bidder is supplying the material
or only installing it.

- Segmented into roughly five to twelve family lines: the clear majority, on every project.
- Segmented commercially instead, into labor, materials, lift equipment, tax and retainage: a
  minority, and worth recognizing because reading only the grand total of one of those badly
  misstates what was bid.
- A single lump sum with a bullet list: a minority, and usually from a labor-only bidder.
- A full measured takeoff with quantities and units on every line: seen once. That one document ran
  to hundreds of numbered lines across five buildings with an as-shown drawing reference on each,
  plus a rollup of project totals by element.

The verb on the family label is the highest-value token in the whole document. Read it every time.
The same firm will write "installation of kitchen and bath casework" and "supply and installation of
trim" three lines apart, and the difference is the entire commercial shape of the bid. One bidder
tags every line explicitly as Labor, Material, or Labor and Material, which is the most useful
column any bidder in this trade produces, and only that bidder produces it.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Labor type, open shop or union. Stated far more often than in most trades, because it is the
  largest price variable and both sides know it. Where a bidder omits it, an estimator records it
  anyway.
- The furnish or install verb per family, as above.
- The plans date, usually, and sometimes the architect. Naming a spec section is uncommon.
- Trim species and profile, in about half of proposals, sometimes down to the fraction: primed
  finger-jointed pine one by four, three quarters by three and a half inch primed flatstock,
  eleven sixteenths by eight inch stool, MDF in place of the specified pine.
- Unit count, as the scope denominator, because that is what the leveling runs on.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Quantities. Almost never, and where a quantity column exists it is often decorative, carrying the
  value one on every line.
- Labor hours. Stated twice in the whole corpus, once as a base-bid total with a separate alternate
  figure, and once inside a bidder's internal worksheet that was included by accident.
- The material fraction of a furnish-and-install number, unless the bidder happens to price the
  package twice.
- Warranty duration, bond cost, or insurance basis. Supplied only after a general contractor asks by
  email, and the market default answer is a one-year labor warranty with a two-year term available
  at no adder from some bidders and refused by others.
- Blocking assumptions, unless the bidder has been burned before, in which case it says so in
  capital letters.
- Whether the number assumes doors arrive pre-cased. This is the largest single variable in the
  package and most bidders leave it silent.

Exclusion behavior splits the market and the split matters. Most bidders carry a standing exclusion
list, typically fifteen to sixty lines, and it is reproduced verbatim project to project. Two firms
in this corpus reproduce byte-identical lists a whole market apart. Read boilerplate for the trade
map, not for the project. A minority carry no exclusion list at all, and those bids are the ones an
estimator has to plug on every row.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs, and they are more varied here than in most
trades:

- Uniform escalation. Every line moves by the same ratio, the scope text is otherwise identical, and
  the title gains a revision token. No reason documented. This is a multiplier applied to a sheet,
  not a re-estimate, and the per-line ratio is how you tell.
- Commercial absorption. One line changes from a price to the word included and the total falls by
  exactly that amount, with the scope grid untouched. A reader diffing totals records a price
  reduction and misses that the bidder simply ate its equipment cost.
- Material substitution. A shelving line moves from wood to wire, or the reverse, and more than
  doubles or halves. The scope did not change; the product did.
- Breakout restructure. The package is re-presented as separable commercial blocks instead of a
  scope list, so the general contractor can buy pieces. No money moves and the restructure is the
  information.
- Scope-sheet answer. The general contractor issues its scope sheet or its commercial checklist and
  the bidder answers row by row. This is the only revision type that reliably changes what is in the
  number.

Two warnings about revisions in this trade specifically. First, a revision frequently exists only as
an email sentence, and one of the most consequential rounds in this corpus was four numbers in a
paragraph sent between principals outside the estimating channel. A reader looking only for PDFs
will miss it. Second, check the header of every file that claims to be an earlier round. A file
filed as the base round of one project turned out to be a proposal for an entirely different
jobsite, dated five days earlier, and comparing the pair produces a fictitious price movement and a
fictitious scope delta. The same trap appears at whole-proposal scale: a nine-page proposal in one
project's folder is addressed to a competing general contractor and priced off an earlier plan date,
written for someone else's bid and re-sent.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Finish carpentry scope sheets have run 38, 41, 48 and 86 rows. Target roughly forty-five to
eighty-five scope lines for a full multifamily package, and go to the top of that range where the
project has amenity space or existing conditions.

The primary organizing axis is not area and not element. It is furnish versus install. Every
estimator sheet in this corpus organizes around that split, whether it labels the blocks Supply and
Install against Install Only, or Finish Carp against Misc Installs where material is furnished by
others, or simply writes the verb into each row title. Adopt it. A scope line that does not say who
furnishes the thing cannot be answered and cannot be levelled.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- The furnish or install verb, on every single row, as part of the row and not as a note. This is
  the trade's defining enumeration and it is not optional.
- Each trim family separately, because each is a different profile, a different material decision
  and a different install operation: unit baseboard, bathroom baseboard, corridor baseboard, amenity
  baseboard, window stool, window apron, window extension jamb, unit entry door casing, unit interior
  door casing, cased opening jamb trim, cased opening casing trim, kitchen island counter trim, stair
  skirt board, stair wall cap, pencil molding. Bidders that price by family enumerate at exactly this
  grain, and the base marks from the finish schedule map onto it one for one.
- Every named base and millwork mark that appears in the finish schedule. Bidders quote by mark, and
  the most thorough of them state what profile and material they actually carried against each mark,
  which is the only way you learn that two bidders priced the same mark differently.
- Doors split three ways at minimum: door leaf install, frame install, hardware install. One bidder
  installs the leaf and explicitly excludes the lockset. Then split the frames again by construction,
  because welded frames and knock-down frames go to different trades, and split the leaves by
  location, because unit interior, unit entry, common area, stair, exterior and balcony doors are
  answered differently by the same bidder in the same paragraph.
- Special door types on their own lines: barn door slabs and their track hardware, bi-fold doors,
  bypass and pocket doors, louvered doors, glazed panels, vault doors, and any door with an integral
  transom, sidelight or glazing. Each is separately excluded by somebody.
- Casework by type: kitchen wall, base, pantry and wall oven cabinets, island panels, bathroom vanity
  bases, drawer banks, linen cabinets, toilet toppers, amenity casework. And separately, the
  accessibility variants, because removable sink bases and cooktop cabinets are a distinct scope with
  a distinct responsibility question about who designs the removability.
- Closet shelving by closet type and tier count: reach-in, walk-in, laundry, linen, bookshelf. The
  tier count is the quantity that matters, it varies by closet type, and it is routinely disputed.
  One bidder qualified a walk-in at one tier while the unit finish schedule note implied more, and
  named the sheet conflict.
- Shelving material as its own decision line, because wire, melamine and painted MDF are three
  different numbers for identical geometry.
- Each specialty family separately: bath accessories, medicine cabinets, mirrors, shower rods,
  curtain rods, grab bars, fire extinguisher cabinets, fire blanket cabinets, mailboxes, package room
  shelving, lockers, bike racks, corner guards, wire mesh storage. These are cheap individually and
  they are the plugs that add up.
- Appliance handling split from appliance setting. Receiving and distributing appliances, setting
  microwaves, setting cooktops, installing wall ovens, and installing anti-tip brackets are five
  different answers from the same bidder.
- In-wall blocking and backing, naming who installs it and who provides the blocking schedule.
- The material logistics block: receive, offload, distribute to the point of use, store, re-distribute
  from storage, hoist. Bidders answer these separately and the boundary between distribute and store
  is where time-and-material charges are born.
- Cleanup, split into daily cleanup to a central location per floor and removal from that location to
  the dumpster. These go to different parties and the reason is jurisdictional.
- Punch, door adjustment after building acclimation, and the return trip. Almost never priced, and
  one estimator template carries a carpenter's relief clause with an hour cap as a standing row.
- Existing-conditions work on renovation projects: repair, refinishing, relocation and modification
  of existing doors, frames and trim; matching of historic profiles; window demolition and
  replacement; removal of window weights and foaming of weight boxes; storm window installation;
  pull-and-replace of windows for a hoist bay. On a renovation this block alone can run a dozen rows.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual unit enumeration collapses to unit type. Two bidders do enumerate every unit number
  individually, running to a hundred lines through the residential floors alone, but they are pricing
  one number for the whole set and the enumeration is a coverage demonstration, not a price
  structure. Follow the unit type.
- Species, profile, thickness and finish are attributes of a trim line, not lines of their own.
- Individual door counts collapse into a door type line. Even the one fully quantified proposal in
  the corpus counts doors by leaf size and swing type, not by opening.
- Room-by-room enumeration collapses to room type, with one exception: amenity and common area
  millwork genuinely differs room by room, and the two most granular bidders both enumerate it by
  room and by interior-design detail number. Follow them there.
- Levels collapse into a level range unless the base mark or the trim actually changes, which at
  corridors it often does between level one and the upper floors.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
finish carpentry the test fires hardest on the furnish and install seams in section 5, because in
this trade a single word in a row title moves more money than any quantity does.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. Because so much of the package is install labor
for someone else's material, a bidder that says nothing about a family has usually not decided
anything about it, and its silence is a gap to surface rather than an inclusion to assume. The
sharpest version of this is that the lowest number on a leveling sheet is very often the narrowest
scope rather than the keenest price. On one project the lowest cost per unit belonged to a bidder
whose base bid excluded unit casework entirely because it was a separate proposal, and on another the
bottom of the finish carpentry ranking was occupied by a window installation contractor from a
different trade whose file happened to carry the finish carpentry token.

### The structural gap list

An estimator does not compare base bids in this trade. It compares base bid plus adjustments, and
the adjustments are larger here than almost anywhere. On one project the adjustments ran to more than
one and a half times a bidder's base bid, and on the same sheet another bidder carried a negative
adjustment because scope had to be removed. Ranking on base bid alone inverts the order.

These are the plugs to expect, the scope that no bidder reliably carries and that has to be added to
every number before comparing:

1. Bath accessories, medicine cabinets and mirrors.
2. Fire extinguisher cabinets and mailboxes.
3. Appliance receiving, distribution and installation, and anti-tip brackets separately.
4. Countertop installation.
5. Caulking, wood putty, nail-hole filling and paint preparation.
6. Scribing of baseboard and of doors.
7. Hoisting, lifts, scaffolding and material conveyance above the ground floor.
8. Cleanup from the central floor location to the dumpster.
9. In-wall blocking and backing panels.
10. Field drilling for cabinet hardware and pulls.
11. Electronic and electrified hardware installation.
12. Corner guards and wall protection.
13. FRP panelling.
14. Stair treads, risers, skirt boards and caps.
15. Unit entry trim and the wood trim on hollow metal frames.
16. Attic stock, mockups, shop drawings and submittals.
17. Project portal and payment application fees.
18. Bond, and general liability where an owner-controlled program does not apply.

A scope line set that names all eighteen explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging in advance,
and in this trade that is most of the work.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The deviation disclosure, which is where the real information is

Because this trade both supplies trim and installs other people's products, its highest-value
paragraph is neither the inclusion list nor the exclusion list. It is the place where a bidder states
what it actually carried when the drawings did not pin the item down. The most thorough bidders write
a qualified-as clause under nearly every line, and those clauses are the difference between two
numbers that look like they answer the same drawings and do not.

The recurring shapes:

- Profile and dimension against a schedule mark. A base mark shown at six inches carried as five and
  a half, a mark shown at four inches carried as three and a half, with the sentence that a custom
  profile costs more. Two bidders on the same set, quoting the same marks, carried different heights.
- Material against a specified species. One bidder carries MDF throughout and warns that
  finger-jointed pine or poplar is a significant increase. Another carries finger-jointed primed
  pine. A third carries poplar. The drawings said the same thing to all three.
- A specified architectural veneer flitch carried as a generic clear-coated oak veneer on an MDF
  core.
- Panelling shown on an elevation qualified as finished drywall by others, and a wallcovering finish
  called out on the reflected ceiling plan qualified as wood veneer per the elevations instead. When
  a bidder resolves a conflict between two sheets by picking one, that resolution is a scope decision
  the general contractor never made.
- Stacked stair treads shown in a detail carried as one layer of carpet-ready plywood.
- Cabinet construction: particleboard box with melamine interiors where plywood may have been
  intended, flat slab doors where shaker may have been intended, unmatched interiors, each with a
  cost-if-required note.
- An unspecified item carried at a stated per-each allowance, most commonly cabinet pull hardware,
  because the drawings named no product.
- A substitution forced by availability, such as a shelf depth that is not made in the specified
  material and gets carried in a different material at a different depth.
- Spec-section exceptions taken one at a time, which is how a door and hardware installer discloses
  that it will not perform the fire-rated and egress door inspections, will not produce a separate
  inspection report per rated assembly, will drill and tap only with self-tapping screws, will
  protect only to the point of installation, and warrants labor for one year against whatever the
  specification asked for.

Where a proposal contains no qualified-as clauses at all, that is not a cleaner bid. It means the
substitutions are unstated.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of that table is boilerplate. Repeat bidders make up a large share of any list in this trade,
and their exclusion blocks are reproduced word for word across projects a market apart, down to the
extra-work hourly rate and the back-charge protocol. Its presence carries no project information.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- A named schedule mark that is excluded, or carried with a stated substitution. When a bidder writes
  out a specific mark and says what it actually carried, it read the schedule.
- An exclusion justified by reference to a specific sheet or detail, especially one that names two
  sheets in conflict.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. A bidder excluded all trim at barn doors and
  bi-fold doors on the stated ground that none is shown, and excluded casing at all common area doors
  for the same reason while asking for head and jamb details before it could price them. Another
  could not find any location for a base mark that the finish schedule assigns to historic partitions,
  and the general contractor's own answer was to assume it is not in contract.
- A counted quantity that the drawings do not summarize anywhere, such as the observation that only a
  subset of laundry closets actually receives shelving.
- A flagged drawing error. One bidder noted that a corridor ceiling was tagged with a mark that is a
  wood baseboard, assumed it was a mistake, and priced accordingly.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a hoist bay, an existing stair, a historic
  profile, a second or third building, a garage podium.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the price into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- The labor rate basis, stated as a rate code or as open shop against union. One bidder priced at the
  residential carpenter rate and stated that if the union requires the full commercial rate the price
  must increase by about thirteen percent of the base bid. The same bidder on a different project
  priced at the commercial rate from the start. This is the largest single price variable in the
  trade and it is a market condition, not a drawing condition.
- The carpenter and laborer jurisdiction. A bidder that employs carpenters but not laborers will
  unload, distribute and clean to a central location on each floor using carpenters, and will not
  move the debris from that location to the dumpster, because that is laborers' work. This is not a
  courtesy boundary and it does not negotiate. It appears as a scope row on the estimator sheet for
  exactly that reason.
- Delivery conditions on furnished material. Nearly every install-heavy bidder attaches the same set:
  material must arrive fully assembled and ready for installation, labeled per unit, and in sequence
  with the construction schedule; material sent out of sequence is installed on time and material;
  offloading and distribution to the point of use is included but storage and re-distribution from
  storage are not; moving or relocating stored material is time and material. Where these are
  violated the install cost is unbounded, and the drawings say nothing about any of it.
- In-wall blocking. One proposal states in capital letters that the bid is based on all areas having
  proper in-wall blocking and that the installation cost will increase if there is none. The
  estimator template carries a matching row asking the bidder to provide a blocking schedule in its
  shop drawings.
- One mobilization, continuous. This is close to unanimous: a consecutive schedule with no phasing.
  Bidders state it as a qualification, list phased installation in their standard exclusions, and
  price a remobilization fee per occurrence when crews are stood down beyond a stated number of
  hours. On a project with a phased turnover this is the qualification most likely to be violated.
- Baseboard installed in one mobilization per unit, with no additional runs for various flooring
  types. A quiet but consequential one, because it makes the flooring schedule a carpentry cost
  driver.
- Pre-hung interior doors assumed to be split-jamb with the casing pre-applied by the door supplier.
  See section 5. When it holds, the casing scope disappears. When it does not, it is worth roughly a
  quarter of the base bid.
- Drywall returns assumed at window jambs, so extension jambs and casing are excluded. Other bidders
  on the same set supply wood extension jambs. Two legitimate readings of the same detail, and one
  general contractor's own leveling note on this row reads that it could not tell from its own set
  whether the return is drywall or wood.
- Tariff pass-through. Live in this market. Sheet-good pricing is held to conditions at the proposal
  date and increases from new or revised tariffs are excluded and passed through, or a bidder
  reserves the right to adjust for tariffs enforced after the date. A general contractor's commercial
  checklist now routinely asks bidders to confirm that current tariffs are included and to hold
  pricing to a named date.
- Proposal validity, typically thirty to sixty days, with escalation stated afterward, and in one
  case a fabrication deposit of half the millwork value required up front.
- Back-charge protocol. No back charge without a stated notice period and an opportunity to repair,
  and no back charge after a phase is complete without an itemized breakdown and photographs.
- An extra-work hourly rate, stated by several bidders, which is what any scope gap will actually
  cost.
- A full contract-terms addendum, attached by one bidder, overriding conflicting subcontract terms on
  indemnity, risk of loss, warranty, pay-when-paid, delay, lien rights, liquidated damages and
  insurance limits. That is invisible on a price-only leveling sheet and materially changes what the
  low number is worth.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section the rest of the entry serves. In a supply trade the seams are a detail. Here they
are the product.

### Doors: the seam that decides the package

Doors, frames and hardware are the largest install family and the largest source of ambiguity. The
work splits into at least four separable pieces and every one of them is bought differently on
different projects: furnish the leaf, furnish the frame, furnish the hardware, install the assembly.

The commercial shapes seen:

1. A door and hardware supplier furnishes, and the finish carpenter installs. The most common shape
   by a wide margin.
2. The same firm furnishes and installs, which collapses the seam entirely. Seen from a supplier that
   quoted material, install labor and the carpentry package as three separately priced blocks. On
   that package the door material was roughly twice the door install labor, so whoever holds the
   material holds most of the money.
3. A dedicated door installation subcontractor takes only the doors, leaving the trim and casework to
   others. Seen on more than one project, sometimes as a one-line letter for labor and fasteners
   only.
4. A labor-only carpenter installs the leaf and explicitly excludes all hardware, so the locksets go
   to a third party. Unusual and expensive, and it looked like a comparable bid on the leveling
   sheet.

Inside that, the question that moves the most money is whether the unit interior doors arrive
pre-hung on split-jamb frames with the casing pre-applied by the door supplier. If they do, the
carpenter's casing scope at those doors vanishes. If they do not, one bidder priced the jamb and
casing trim at unit interior doors as an add worth about twenty-seven percent of its entire base bid.
The drawings will not tell you. The door supplier's packaging decision will, and it is usually made
after the carpentry bids are in. Any scope line set for this trade must ask the question explicitly
and carry both answers.

The other door seams, each of which someone excludes:

- Welded hollow metal frames go to the drywall contractor without exception. Knock-down frames at
  interior stud walls stay with the carpenter, sometimes only as a priced add. Frames at masonry or
  concrete go to the mason. So the frame scope is split three ways by wall type, and a scope line
  reading "install frames" is unanswerable.
- Frames with integral transoms, sidelights or glazing are excluded by most, and often land nowhere.
- Pocket door tracks and frames must be installed by others before the drywall goes up, which means
  the carpenter would have to mobilize early. One bidder priced early mobilization as an extra.
- Barn door slabs and their track hardware, bi-fold doors, bypass doors and louvered doors each get
  answered separately, and the trim at all of them is commonly excluded on the ground that none is
  shown.
- Undercutting is expected from the supplier, not the installer. Electronic hardware, closers, panic
  devices, access control and key fob programming are excluded by nearly everyone.

### Casework: the delivery seam

The carpenter installs unit kitchen and bathroom casework that a fabricator furnishes, and the entire
cost risk sits in how the material arrives. The recurring conditions, stated by most install-heavy
bidders in nearly identical words:

- Cabinets must arrive fully assembled. Assembly is excluded.
- Cabinets must arrive labeled per unit. One fabricator advertises a bar-coded label system precisely
  because this is the friction point.
- Cabinets must arrive in construction sequence, floor by floor, with the delivery matched to the
  floor scheduled for installation. Backordered material, returns or out-of-sequence deliveries
  convert the lump sum to time and material.
- Cabinet hardware must be pre-drilled by the supplier. Field drilling is excluded.
- Countertops are not the carpenter's, and neither are the subtops, the support brackets or the
  hardwood web frames. Where a carpenter will take tops it is as a priced add conditioned on the tops
  being templated, pre-cut and pre-drilled for a simple drop-in.
- Accessibility variants carry their own responsibility question. Removable sink base and cooktop
  cabinets have to be designed for removability by the cabinet manufacturer, and one installer
  explicitly refuses to make, adjust or modify them in the field.
- On a large enough project the fabricator schedules by full truckload and charges extra for delivery
  outside normal hours, so the delivery window is a cost term.

A national installation subcontractor that follows a casework manufacturer is a distinct animal worth
recognizing. It prices off the manufacturer's bid rather than off the drawings, brings a travelling
non-union crew, carries lodging and per diem for the whole job, requires a single continuous
uninterrupted installation term, includes exactly one mobilization with a stated remobilization fee,
requires the general contractor to unload and to provide a lull at grade and a buckhoist above four
stories, and accepts no responsibility for material once staged. Its quantities cannot be checked
against the set without the manufacturer's document.

### Appliances: the seam that is argued rather than stated

Most carpenters carry setting microwaves, cooktops and wall ovens, on the reasoning that those are
part of the cabinet installation and are the appliance scope carpenters claim. Most exclude ranges,
and nearly all exclude anti-tip brackets. One bidder explained the exclusion in operational terms
worth carrying forward: the anti-tip has to be fixed between the appliance installer bringing the
range to the kitchen and sliding it into place, so the carpenter would stand idle waiting on another
trade at every unit, which is inefficient and invites field arguments. That bidder offered to price
anti-tips alone only if the general contractor would define the scope and commit to a pace, stated as
a number of ranges per day, so it could calculate carpenter hours.

That exchange is the template for the whole trade. Where an install scope depends on another trade's
tempo, the carpenter needs a rate, not a quantity.

### Closet shelving: the material seam

Shelving is one of the two things this trade actually furnishes, and the material is rarely settled
by the drawings. The choices are vinyl-coated wire, white melamine, and painted or primed MDF, and
the money between them is large: a deduct to move from wood to wire ran about forty percent of the
wood shelving line, and a linen stack priced both ways cost about five times as much in wood as in
wire. Additional tiers are a separate decision again, and one bidder priced five extra tiers at
bedroom closets as an add worth nearly as much as the entire base shelving scope.

Two further wrinkles. Tier counts differ by closet type and the unit details sheet and the unit
finish schedule may disagree about them, which bidders flag and ask about. And one bidder conditions
its wire shelving price on the general contractor accepting its preferred vendor, with additional
installation cost for any other, which is a supply-chain condition disguised as a product note.

### The painting seam: caulk, putty, nail holes and scribing

This is the trade's most contested boundary and it runs directly against what painters assume.
Painters bid on the expectation that the carpenter delivers tight, true miters that do not need caulk
to fill. What the carpenters actually say, nearly unanimously, is that they exclude all caulking, all
wood putty, all nail-hole filling on paint-grade material, all backer rod and sealant, and all
preparation for finish paint. Several also exclude scribing of baseboard and of doors to uneven
floors and walls, which is the operation that would have produced the tight fit in the first place.

The positions actually seen span the full range, on the same kind of work:

- Excludes caulking, putty, nail holes, prep and scribing entirely. The clear majority.
- Excludes scribing of baseboard but includes scribing one door per unit if necessary. One bidder,
  and it is the only statement in the corpus that names the true unit of the risk.
- Includes scribing to irregular surfaces and promises a sand-ready finish for the painting
  contractor. A minority, and worth a great deal more than it looks.
- Includes insulating and caulking the gap between frame and rough opening. One door installer.

So on most projects nobody has bought the caulk, the putty, the nail-hole filling or the scribing.
Give each of them its own scope line, name the counterparty, and expect to plug all of them.

### Blocking, and the trades that come before

The carpenter cannot hang shelving, grab bars, accessories, mailboxes, wall-mounted millwork or
cabinets without in-wall blocking, and nearly every bidder excludes furnishing and installing it.
The blocking has to be right before the drywall closes, which means the decision is made months
before this trade mobilizes and by a trade that has no shelving schedule. The best practice seen is a
scope row requiring the carpentry bidder to provide a blocking schedule inside its shop drawings, so
the framer has a document. Where that does not happen, the bidder's qualification is simply that the
installation cost will increase.

Related and equally quiet: gypcrete foam strips at the base of walls have to be removed before the
baseboard goes on, and the carpenter excludes removing them.

### Cleanup and debris: the jurisdictional seam

Union carpentry bidders will clean daily and carry their debris to a central location on each floor,
and will not carry it to the dumpster, because moving it from there is laborers' work and they do not
employ laborers. Open shop bidders will often take it all the way to a general-contractor-supplied
dumpster. Both stances appear as explicit qualifications and both appear as a row on the estimator's
sheet. Do not treat the dumpster line as a courtesy detail; it is a labor agreement.

### Windows on renovation work

On new construction, windows are not this trade. On a renovation, they often are, because the
carpenter is already working the opening. The scope that appeared includes demolition of existing
windows, installation of new windows furnished by others, removal of sash weights and spray foaming
of the weight boxes, installation of removable interior storm windows at existing sash, pull and
replace of windows to create a hoist bay, and installation of pressure-treated blocking and roller
shade blocking at rough openings. The access question drives the price: one bidder priced all window
work from the interior and offered exterior access as an add, and the general contractor's own
superintendents discussed swing staging, a lift with a street closure, and a night shift as the
alternatives, while noting that the window drop has to coordinate with repointing and lead work on
the same elevations.

### Other seams worth their own lines

- Amenity and common area millwork. Whether the carpenter furnishes it, installs it, or neither
  changes project to project, and one bidder priced it as install-only labor while another furnished
  and installed the same rooms.
- Stairs. Wood-framed egress stairs bring plywood treads, risers, skirt boards and wall caps, and
  concrete or metal pan stairs bring none of it, so the stair scope depends entirely on which stairs
  are which construction. Bidders enumerate the stairs by number for exactly this reason. Handrails
  are steel and go elsewhere.
- Vinyl and rubber base. Where the flooring package carries the base, the carpenter's supply scope
  can collapse to almost nothing, and the scope sheet has to say so explicitly or every bidder will
  price a different assumption.
- Wall panelling and cased openings shown on interior-design elevations. Repeatedly qualified as
  finished drywall by others, which is a real fork: either the drywall contractor builds and finishes
  a portal, or the carpenter trims it, and the two answers cost very different amounts.
- Corner guards, wall protection, lockers and bike racks. Excluded by nearly everyone, often with a
  note that more information is needed to price, and frequently unassigned afterward.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

This is the section the drawings cannot cover, and in this trade it is unusually large, because the
package is defined less by what is drawn than by how the project is bought.

What carpenters actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The enlarged unit plans and unit details. This is the primary document. Base, casing, sill, apron,
   shelving and closet configuration all come from here, and the most granular bidders reference an
   enlarged unit plan sheet on every line.
2. The unit finish schedule and the base and millwork mark list. Marks come from here, and a bidder's
   substitutions are stated against them.
3. The unit count. Every estimator sheet normalizes every bid to a cost per unit and ranks on it. Unit
   count is the leveling currency of this trade. If the set does not make it unambiguous, every bid
   becomes incomparable.
4. The door schedule, for the split between unit interior, unit entry, common, stair and exterior
   doors, and for frame construction.
5. The shelving or closet schedule and the unit details showing tier counts.
6. Interior design elevations and details, for amenity millwork, cased openings and corridor base.
7. The specification, rarely, and mostly to take exception to it.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff. One proposal in the entire corpus carried measured
quantities, and its author was pricing for a different general contractor at the time.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Whether the labor is union or open shop, and if union, whether the residential or the full
  commercial carpenter rate applies. The largest single driver, and the two rate bases differ by
  roughly a seventh of the whole package.
- Whether the bidder employs laborers, which decides where its cleanup obligation stops.
- Whether unit interior doors will arrive pre-hung on split-jamb frames with casing pre-applied. The
  largest scope variable, worth about a quarter of a base bid, and decided by the door supplier.
- Which hollow metal frames are welded and which are knock-down, and which sit in masonry.
- What the closet shelving will actually be made of, and how many tiers.
- Whether the window jamb is a drywall return or a wood extension jamb. A general contractor could
  not answer this from its own set, and the item was worth several percent of the package.
- Whether the base is wood or vinyl, and whether the corridor and back-of-house base is in this
  package at all.
- What the millwork or casework package took, and therefore what is left here. The sibling packages
  are the boundary, not the drawings, and one bidder in the corpus scoped its bid by reading the
  millwork scope sheet because no finish carpentry scope sheet had been issued.
- Whether in-wall blocking will be present and correct.
- Whether the material will arrive assembled, labeled per unit, and in construction sequence.
- Whether the schedule is continuous or phased, and how many mobilizations that implies.
- Site access, hoisting, lift availability and conveyance above the ground floor.
- Trim species and profile, wherever the schedule gives only a mark. Bidders have carried MDF,
  finger-jointed pine and poplar against the same drawings.
- Tariff conditions at the time of purchase.
- Warranty term. The market default is one year on labor; two years is available from some bidders at
  no adder and refused by others, and no bidder will warrant material furnished by others.
- Insurance basis, whether an owner-controlled program applies, and bond cost. Both quoted only when
  asked, and bond is quoted at a percentage of contract value.
- Whether a mockup is expected, and whether shop drawings and submittals are required.

The scope sheet problem is worth stating on its own, because it is the single most consistent gap
between what carpenters bid and what the package actually is. Bidders in this trade ask for a scope
sheet. One wrote that it expected the next step to be a detailed finish carpentry scope sheet so it
could adjust its scope to the general contractor's intentions, and offered its own proposal as raw
material for drafting one. Another asked for the leveling scope sheet so it could align its takeoff.
A third, bidding without one, used a competing general contractor's scope sheet from an earlier round
and later found that it had inherited assumptions it had never checked. Where a general contractor
does issue the sheet, the bids converge and the answers become row-by-row. Where it does not, every
bidder invents its own boundary and the leveling becomes an exercise in reconstruction.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders price around missing information openly and label
it, carrying items on a stated assumption: a skirt board assumed on both sides, a shelf material
assumed, a wall panel assumed. Budgetary quotes appear with the word budgetary in them. By permit and
conformed sets the same bidders switch to citing marks, detail numbers and counted quantities, and
their qualified-as clauses multiply. Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its folder's set
stage often disagree, and that in this trade a proposal may be based on no document at all: several
one-page bids cite no set, no date and no spec section.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator sheet, without
  exception, and most plug rows are priced per unit.
- Each, for doors and their hardware, for cabinet boxes by type and dimension, for bath accessories,
  for fire extinguisher cabinets, for mailboxes, for shelves, for stair treads and risers, for cased
  openings, and for access panels. This is the trade's natural counting unit and the one measured
  proposal in the corpus counts almost everything this way.
- Linear feet, for base, trim, door casing and window trim. The natural unit for the supply half of
  the package and the one that appears in a takeoff rollup.
- Square feet, for filler panels, FRP, wood panelling and countertops.
- Square feet of building area, which is how an estimator plugs trim. This is worth knowing because
  it is the exception: on the one sheet carrying the estimator's own unit prices, every family except
  trim is plugged per unit or per each, and trim alone is plugged against total building area.
- Hours, for extra work, back-charged repair and for the labor content of a negotiated add. Stated
  hourly rates for out-of-scope work appear in several proposals.
- Crew days and apartments per day, which is how the work is actually planned. A bidder's internal
  worksheet, included in its proposal by accident, priced a four-carpenter crew at a daily rate, set
  bath accessory installation at four apartments per day, and priced cabinet installation at a rate
  per kitchen.
- Pairs, for hollow metal door openings, alongside a separate leaf count.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, quoted at two percent by several bidders.

Cost drivers. In this trade the commercial questions outrank the physical ones, so they are ranked
that way:

1. Labor type: union against open shop, and within union, the residential rate against the full
   commercial rate. Tracked on every bidder on every leveling sheet as a first-class field.
2. How much of the package is furnish and how much is install only. This is not a discount, it is a
   different bid, and it is the reason the cost-per-unit spread on these sheets is so wide.
3. Whether unit interior doors arrive pre-cased on split-jamb frames.
4. Whether unit casework is in this package or a sibling one.
5. Closet shelving material and tier count.
6. Whether the base is wood or vinyl, and how much corridor and amenity base the project carries.
7. Unit count and the amount of amenity and common area space, which is what actually scales the
   labor.
8. Trim material: MDF against finger-jointed pine against poplar, and stock profile against custom.
9. Delivery discipline: assembled, labeled, sequenced material against the alternative, and blocking
   completeness alongside it.
10. Phasing, mobilization count and remobilization.
11. Hoisting and lift availability, and floor height.
12. Amenity millwork complexity, which is priced room by room and detail by detail.
13. Tariff exposure on sheet goods, warranty term, bond and insurance basis.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per unit among genuine whole-package bidders ran about 2.3, 3, 3.5 and 4.5
times. Bidder counts per project ran from about eight to about thirteen. But the spread in this trade
is mostly not a price signal. It is a coverage signal: on one project the lowest cost per unit
belonged to a bidder that had excluded unit casework into a separate proposal, and the highest
belonged to the only union bidder, which was also the only one furnishing the casework. Two entries
sitting below the whole band on two different sheets were not carpentry bids at all, one being a
baseboard supplier and the other a window installation contractor.

Which leads to the pricing convention that matters most here. An estimator does not compare base
bids. It compares base bid plus adjustments, and in this trade the adjustments are unusually large
and unusually asymmetric. Observed adjustments ran from zero to about two thirds of a base bid on one
project, to more than one and a half times the base bid on another, and one bidder carried a negative
adjustment because scope had to be removed from its number. The largest adjustments consistently land
on the lowest base bids. A leveling sheet in this trade that leaves the adjustment row at zero has
not levelled anything, and on one project in this corpus that is exactly what happened.

A few ratios worth carrying, all read from bidders who priced the same package twice:

- Where a bidder priced its whole package both furnish-and-install and install-only, the install-only
  figure was about three fifths of the full number on a small project and about three quarters on a
  large one. The material fraction of this package shrinks as the job grows.
- Furnished trim material ran roughly three tenths of the install labor it serves.
- On a doors package priced as separable blocks, door material was roughly twice the install labor.
- Moving closet shelving from wood to wire saved about two fifths of the shelving line, and a linen
  stack priced both ways cost about five times as much in wood.
- Adding the door jamb and casing trim that a split-jamb pre-cased door would have made unnecessary
  ran about a quarter of a base bid.
- Moving from the residential carpenter rate to the full commercial rate added about an eighth of a
  base bid.
- Finishing wood base cost about forty-five percent of what furnishing and installing it cost, from
  the one firm in the corpus that sells both the carpentry and the painting and was asked to split
  them.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about forty finish carpentry proposals and four estimator leveling
sheets, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one
general contractor. The proposals span design development, permit, construction document and
conformed sets; the bidder pool includes several firms that appear on two, three or four of the
projects, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4
testable. Three same-bidder revision pairs and one three-round sequence were read side by side, and
one of those pairs turned out not to be a pair at all.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back of house space, in
  one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors,
  institutional, healthcare, hospitality, education or public prevailing-wage work, where the
  standing and running trim scope is usually smaller and the architectural woodwork scope much
  larger.
- Only one project involved renovation of an existing building, so the window replacement block and
  the existing-conditions block in section 3 rest on that project alone.
- Only one proposal contained a measured takeoff, and only one contained a productivity worksheet, so
  every quantity and rate convention in section 7 that cites a per-day or per-kitchen basis rests on
  a single document that was written for a different general contractor.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, whether the caulking and scribing plugs were actually bought, or who ended up
  with the corner guards.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the delivery-sequence and blocking
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the general contractor issued a finish carpentry scope
  sheet before bids were due and every bidder answered it row by row. Every leveling sheet here was
  reconstructed after the fact from proposals that had each invented their own boundary, so the
  convergence effect claimed in section 6 is inferred from bidders asking for the sheet rather than
  observed from a project that had one.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the finish carpentry, millwork and casework packages were
  awarded together to one firm, so the collapse case is priced but not tested.
- Stair work is thin. Wood stair scope appears on three projects but always as a small line or an
  alternate, and no project in the corpus has a feature stair.
- The corpus has not shown how this package is priced where the doors are steel throughout, where
  there is no wood base at all, or where the closet systems are a manufacturer-furnished proprietary
  package installed by the manufacturer.
