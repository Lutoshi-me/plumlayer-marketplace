# Smoke Curtains

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the elevator smoke
curtain package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling
sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the
words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. This is a trade whose entire leveling problem is a count, and
the count is not on any schedule.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The scope is elevator hoistway opening protection: a rolling fabric curtain, held in a head box
above the elevator entrance, that drops on a fire alarm signal to block smoke migration through the
hoistway, with an egress means so a person can get out from under it. It exists because the code
requires hoistway opening protection where the lobby is not otherwise enclosed, so the driver is a
code section, not a design intent.

Bidders come in three kinds, and establishing which kind is looking at the project matters before
any number is compared:

- Overhead door dealers who sell a coiling curtain from a door manufacturer's line. The largest
  group. These companies also sell the coiling doors, and their curtain quote is very often a block
  inside a document that also prices doors, and sometimes trash chutes.
- Specialty partition and folding wall houses who sell a purpose-built elevator smoke containment
  product. These quote curtains only and are the most likely to give a per-unit rate.
- Accordion fire door manufacturers offering an elevator smoke containment system as an equal to a
  specified curtain. Seen once, and the most different document in the trade: longest lead time,
  longest exclusion list, and the only one that separates control panels from curtains as their own
  counted item.

The packaging consequence is the same as in the door trade. A printed total in this package
frequently spans two or three scopes. Read the block, not the bottom of the page.

Section numbers do not identify this scope reliably. It has been cited as 083300, 083343, 083400 and
083480 across four projects, and one bidder assigned it to the same section as the coiling doors in
the same document. Two different estimator workbooks in the same office use two different section
numbers for it. Route on the words elevator smoke curtain or smoke containment curtain, never on the
number.

## 2. How the market bids

The proposal is short. One number, a count, an opening size, and a bulleted feature list is the
normal shape, and it usually fits on one page.

Almost every proposal states its own count. This is the single most useful property of the trade and
the reason the leveling method in section 7 works at all. The count is stated one of three ways:

- A flat count with a size. The most common.
- A count decomposed by hoistway and level range, naming the elevators and the floors. Less common
  and far more useful, because it exposes the bidder's reading of which openings are protected.
- A quantity table, one row per hoistway with a floor range, a size and a quantity, plus a separate
  row for control panels. Seen once and it is the best shape in the trade.

A minority print a per-unit rate alongside the total. Where a bidder does, its rounds become
readable: one such bidder moved through four rounds on one project and its per-unit rate moved by a
couple of percent while its total moved by about a quarter. The whole movement was count.

What a proposal will normally state:

- The count, the nominal opening width and height, and the product model.
- The feature list: curtain material, the listing standards it meets, guide material and finish,
  head box mounting, motor and controller voltage, battery backup and its duration, the egress means
  (a rewind or open switch, a lift strap, or both), and auto reset behavior after the alarm clears.
- Delivery lead time and shop drawing lead time. Commonly stated, which is unusual in this cluster
  and reflects that these are made to order.
- That testing with building officials is included. Commonly.

What a proposal will normally not state:

- Which specific openings are protected, unless the count is decomposed by hoistway and level.
- Whether the head box is surface mounted or recessed, unless the product model implies it. This is
  the largest single price variable in the trade and it is frequently readable only from the model
  number.
- Warranty duration. Usually one year, stated rarely, produced on request.
- Insurance and bond. Almost never in the document, always available within a day by email.
- Whether an alternate is being quoted in place of the specified product, though the exceptions
  paragraph often makes it obvious.

Revision habits:

- Count changes at a frozen unit rate. The dominant revision shape and the cleanest. A count moving
  by one, or by two as a level enters the scope, with every other word identical.
- A labor line restructured with no scope change. A crew and duration line becoming a per hour line
  has taken several percent out of a number with counts, sizes and exceptions all byte-identical.
- A negotiation ladder with silent feature substitution. One bidder's number fell by more than a
  quarter between set stages at an identical count while the curtain height grew, a key switch
  control dropped out, and a lift strap and mid-curtain switch appeared. The price movement and the
  feature movement were not reconciled anywhere in the document.
- A quantity reduction driven by the general contractor. The largest single price movement in the
  corpus came from a bidder adding the words customer takeoff to its cited basis and dropping an
  entire hoistway from its quantity table. The rate barely moved. Competition did not produce that
  saving; a count did.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets in this trade have run about twenty to twenty-six rows, most of which are the
specification's feature list and the related sheets. The priced rows number two or three. The rows
that matter are the count rows.

### What enumerates

- The count of protected openings, decomposed by hoistway and by level. This is the whole trade.
  Write it as one row per hoistway naming its floor range and its opening count, not as a single
  total. An estimator sheet that does this has been seen, and it is the only sheet shape that lets a
  bidder's answer be checked. Every level that is in question gets its own row.
- Opening width and height per hoistway, because they vary between hoistways on real projects and
  because they drive the product.
- Head box mounting condition as its own row: surface mounted above the ceiling, or recessed into a
  soffit with recessed guides. These are different products at very different rates, and the recessed
  form pulls blocking, soffit framing and access panels into other packages. A single curtains row
  cannot carry this.
- Guide finish as its own row where the lobby finish is stainless, marble or millwork. Guides that
  must match a brushed stainless finish, or that land in a stone or millwork jamb, are a real cost
  and a real coordination item.
- Egress means as its own row: which side the open switch is on, whether there is one on both sides,
  whether a manual lift strap is included, and whether a test key switch is included.
- Battery backup and its stated hold-open and operating durations.
- Control panels, counted separately. Where a system uses a group control panel serving a limited
  number of curtain motor circuits, the panel count is a function of the curtain count and it is a
  real line item. Only one bidder in the corpus exposed it, and it was a meaningful part of the
  number.
- Fire alarm interface, its own row, with a named party. See the gap list.
- Testing and commissioning with the authority having jurisdiction, its own row.

### What collapses

- Curtain material, listing labels, motor voltage, hood material and auto reset behavior are
  attributes of the line. They are the substitution surface and belong in the description, but they
  are not separately biddable.
- Identical openings within one hoistway collapse to one row with a count and a floor range.
- Freight collapses into the line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

The test fires on exactly one thing above all others: whether a given opening is protected. On real
sets bidders have carried counts of eighteen, twenty, twenty and thirty for the same building, and
nine, twelve, twelve and thirteen on another. Every opening whose protection is genuinely in
question deserves its own row, because a reasonable bidder can carry it, exclude it, or be silent,
and the three outcomes differ by a full unit price.

The ground floor is the recurring one. Whether the lobby level opening needs a curtain, and whether
a below-grade level does, is the question the corpus asks over and over. One bidder handled it
correctly by carrying the disputed floor as a priced alternate rather than assuming it either way,
which is the only clean handling seen and worth asking every bidder for.

### The structural gap list

Scope no bidder carries, which must be added to every number before comparing:

1. All electrical: line voltage power to the controller, conduit, wiring, boxes, disconnects, and
   all low voltage wiring. Excluded by every bidder seen.
2. Fire alarm work and the tie-in that signals the curtain to drop. Excluded universally, and the
   estimator sheets record the responsible party as an open question rather than an assignment. This
   is the most reliably unowned scope in the package.
3. Blocking, backing and structural support for the head box and guides. Excluded universally.
4. Soffit and ceiling work, and access panels, where the product is concealed. Excluded universally,
   and much larger where the product is recessed.
5. Cutting or fitting the elevator lobby finish, whether marble panel or millwork, to receive the
   guides. An estimator recorded this as an open question against two details; no bidder addresses
   it.
6. Permits where required.
7. Bond, general liability where an owner-controlled program does not apply, and any warranty beyond
   one year.
8. Elevator trim and accessories, excluded explicitly by the one bidder whose exclusion list is long.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

Most bidders here carry a short by-others note rather than an exclusion list, and it is boilerplate:
wiring by the electrician, blocking not by us, openings and supports by others. One bidder in the
corpus carries a long structured exclusion list, and reading it is the fastest way to see the whole
by-others map of the trade, including items nobody else names: receiving and storage at site,
storage container fees, split shipment charges, expedited fees, custom factory finish on the head box
and guides, and the stainless guide finish that other bidders include as standard.

| Excluded by the curtain contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| All line and low voltage electrical work, conduit, boxes, disconnects, hookups (every bidder seen) | Electrical contractor |
| Smoke detectors, fire alarm work and wiring, and the alarm tie-in (every bidder seen) | Fire alarm contractor, frequently unassigned |
| Wood blocking, backing, and hood, spring and operator supports (every bidder seen) | Framing or drywall |
| Soffit work, ceiling work and access panels (commonly, and always where recessed) | Drywall |
| Permits and fees (commonly) | General contractor |
| Bond cost and sales tax (some bidders; most include tax) | General contractor |
| Service contract and maintenance (some bidders) | Offered to the end user after completion |
| Receiving, offload and site storage (one bidder, explicitly) | General contractor, and universally assumed by the others |
| Custom factory finish on head box and guides (one bidder) | Painting, or an add |
| Elevator trim and accessories (one bidder) | Elevator contractor |

Qualifications that are really pricing bases:

- Mobilization count. Prices are commonly based on one mobilization for installation. This is a real
  constraint on a building where curtains are installed floor by floor as the elevators are turned
  over, and additional mobilizations are chargeable.
- Opening readiness. One bidder makes it explicit that additional field checks and remobilizations
  caused by openings not being ready are chargeable, and requires a signed contractor checklist
  before it will mobilize at all.
- Field dimensions and shop drawing approval. Delivery is quoted as a number of weeks after
  approvals and field dimensions, so the lead time clock does not start at award. On the system with
  the longest lead time, fabrication follows the elevator submittals and requires client approval of
  shop drawings first, which chains this trade behind the elevator package.
- Control panel proximity. One system requires its group control panel to sit within a stated cable
  distance of the furthest motor circuit, which is a layout constraint, not a price.
- Certification. Factory certified installers are required for listed products. This can eliminate a
  bidder rather than reprice one.
- Deposits. Half on release with the balance on delivery is seen, alongside a requirement for a
  signed quote and final field verification before material release.
- Tariffs. Named and excluded, or named and disclaimed, in the later rounds.

Where an exclusion carries project signal it looks like this: a statement that no formal
specifications were provided at bid time and that the price is subject to formal specification
review. That is a documents gap with a stated reason and it should go upstream as an RFI, not be
absorbed as a bidder quirk.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

- Elevator. The curtain protects the elevator opening and is not in the elevator package. The
  elevator entry in this knowledge base records that elevator contractors exclude smoke curtains to
  specialties, which matches from this side: no curtain proposal comes from an elevator company.
  The real dependency runs the other way. On the longest-lead system, curtain fabrication follows
  the elevator submittals, so the curtain cannot be built until the elevator entrances are approved.
- Fire alarm. The most important unowned seam. Somebody must run the signal that drops the curtain,
  and no curtain bidder does. Estimator sheets have carried this as a literal question.
- Electrical. Power to the controller, and in some products low voltage to each motor circuit. The
  curtain bidder supplies a factory wired assembly and stops there.
- Drywall and framing. Blocking for the head box, and where the product is recessed, the soffit that
  conceals it plus access panels for service. The recessed form of this product transfers real cost
  into the drywall package, and no one prices that transfer.
- Elevator lobby finishes. Guides land in a jamb that may be stone, cast marble, tile or millwork.
  Whether that finish is cut to receive the guides, and by whom, is contested and undocumented. Ask
  it explicitly.
- Overhead doors. Same market, same document, frequently the same total. See section 1.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What curtain contractors price from:

1. Floor plans, counting elevator openings hoistway by hoistway and level by level. This is the
   primary act and it is a takeoff, not a schedule read. There is no smoke curtain schedule on these
   sets.
2. Elevation, head and jamb details at the elevator entrance, for mounting condition and guide
   finish. Where those details exist, they answer the recessed question; where they do not, bidders
   assume.
3. The specification, for the basis of design product and the listing requirements.
4. Elevator entrance dimensions, for width and drop height.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each moves the number:

- Whether the lobby level and any below-grade level require protection. This is a code interpretation
  applied to the specific building, made by the fire protection engineer or the authority having
  jurisdiction, and the drawings frequently do not settle it. It is the trade's central open
  question.
- Whether the head box is recessed. The recessed product class has run to roughly twice the per-unit
  rate of the surface mounted one from the same bidder, and it moves scope into drywall as well. A
  product-class alternate offered on one project was worth about ninety percent of the base bid.
- What the elevator lobby finish is at the jamb, and who cuts it.
- Who owns the fire alarm interface.
- The elevator submittal date, which gates fabrication on at least one system.
- Whether the install can be done in one mobilization, which depends on the turnover sequence.
- Lead time exposure. Delivery has ranged from about six weeks to about twenty weeks after approvals
  across the products seen, and on the longest one the lead time is explicitly purchasable: a stated
  percentage add compresses it by roughly half. Lead time is a priced schedule instrument here, not
  a fixed constraint.

Set-stage behavior. Counts grow as sets develop, and they grow because levels enter the scope, not
because openings are added. One project's counts went from fourteen to fifteen to seventeen across a
year as a below-grade level came in. The basis of design product has also been observed to change in
a conformed set, after which four bidders quoted four different products against it.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, per protected opening. The universal unit and the correct denominator.
- Each, per group control panel, on systems that use them.
- Percent, for a stated expediting adder on the long-lead system.
- Crew days or installer hours where a bidder exposes its labor line.

Estimator sheets have normalized this trade three ways, and the choice matters:

- Cost per curtain against a single GC-determined count. The right denominator, and the sheet that
  used it also recorded each bidder's own count on its own row and adjusted every bid to the common
  count before ranking. That adjustment, applied in both directions including a large negative
  adjustment to the bidder who carried too many, is the correct method for this trade.
- Cost per curtain with no count normalization at all, dividing every bid by one denominator while
  the bidders carried different counts. This flatters the bidders who carried fewer.
- Cost per elevator stop. Seen once. It is a different concept from a protected opening, the two
  numbers were not the same on that project, and the sheet did not reconcile them. A bidder omitting
  a whole floor is invisible in a per-stop column.

Cost drivers, ranked:

1. The count of protected openings. Larger than every other factor combined.
2. Head box mounting: recessed versus surface. Roughly a factor of two per unit.
3. Product class and whether side guides and a bottom bar are required, which is the same question
   from the product side.
4. Opening width and drop height.
5. Guide finish, particularly a finish matched to brushed stainless.
6. Egress and control features: switches on both sides, lift strap, test key switch.
7. Battery backup duration.
8. Labor basis and mobilization count.
9. Lead time, and whether it is being bought down.
10. Tax treatment and insurance basis.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. Per protected opening, the spread within a single
project has run about 1.3 times, about 1.66 times, and about 2.3 times on the three projects with
enough bidders. Bid lists run three to six companies, and the same company appears across projects.
Per-unit rates across the whole corpus cluster within a range of roughly one to one and a half
against each other once product class is held constant, and the recessed product sits well above
that band.

What the spread does not prove. The 2.3 times spread on one project is measured after the estimator
normalized every bid to a common count, so it is a genuine price spread, and it is still not a
measure of competitiveness: it spans a recessed product and a surface product, and it spans a
domestic long-lead engineered system and a dealer-supplied catalogue curtain. Conversely, the
tightest spread in the corpus, about 1.3 times, sits on the project whose sheet normalized to
elevator stops rather than to protected openings and whose bid form breakout rows were returned
empty by everyone. That is convergence around an unasked question, not agreement. And on the project
where two bidders' totals landed close together, they had carried counts differing by two units at
different sizes, so the closeness of the totals was arithmetic coincidence. Count convergence and
price convergence are independent in this trade, and the count is the one to establish first.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about twenty elevator smoke curtain proposals from six companies, plus
three estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four mid-rise residential projects in one regional
market, all bid by one general contractor. Proposals span design development, permit, construction
document and conformed sets. Several companies appear on more than one project and one appears on
all four, which is what makes the revision-habit observations in section 2 testable.

The corpus is thin and unusually consistent, which cuts both ways: the count problem shows up on
every project, which gives high confidence in section 7, but nothing here has been tested outside
one building type and one code jurisdiction.

What the entry cannot yet claim:

- Everything here is elevator hoistway opening protection in mid-rise residential buildings in one
  market. The corpus contains no draft curtain, no atrium smoke curtain, no perimeter or
  compartmentation curtain, and no deployable barrier at an escalator or open stair, all of which
  are sold by an overlapping market and none of which behave like this.
- The code interpretation that drives the count is jurisdiction-specific. Which levels require
  protection, and whether the ground floor does, cannot be generalized from four buildings in one
  city.
- Only one project in the corpus carried the recessed product class, so the claim that recessed runs
  to roughly twice the surface rate rests on one project plus one product-class alternate on
  another.
- Only one bidder exposed group control panels as a counted item, so that scope line rests on a
  single document, though the constraint behind it is a property of the system.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing shows which of these
  exclusions survived negotiation, and in particular nothing shows who ended up owning the fire
  alarm tie-in.
- No change order history is in the corpus, so the mobilization and opening-readiness qualifications
  are read out of proposal language rather than observed converting.
- Labor basis is almost entirely open shop, with one prevailing wage bid from a company that priced
  open shop elsewhere. There is not enough here to say anything about a labor delta.
- The corpus has not shown what happens when this scope is bought inside the elevator contract or
  inside a fire protection package, only when it is bought from the door and specialty market.
