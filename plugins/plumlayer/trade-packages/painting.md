# Painting

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the painting package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling
sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the
words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, 
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real painters can bid it and a real estimator can level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 099000 (Painting and Coating) is the anchor. It is the section a proposal names when it
names one at all, which it commonly does. One project ran the package under 099900 instead, with no
difference in content, so the section number is a weak identifier and the trade token is the strong
one.

The package as bid in the market is "painting and wall coverings", not painting alone. Wallcovering
(spec section 097200 where it is separately specified) collapses into the painting package by
default and is carried by the same bidder: nearly every proposal prices or excludes wallcovering as
part of its own scope, and very rarely cited 097200 as a distinct section. But it is a detachable
block, not a merged one: about half the proposals price it as its own number, its own department, or
an add alternate, and on one project an entirely separate specialty wallcovering vendor ran
alongside the painting bidders.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another spec section:

- Wallcovering and wall protection, as above, which is the default.
- Concrete floor sealer at back of house rooms. Some bidders carry it, a similar number explicitly
  exclude it, and an estimator noted that it was carried in the flooring package
  instead.
- Epoxy floor coatings at bike rooms, pet rooms and similar back of house rooms. Occasionally
  carried, commonly excluded; an estimator keeps it on the painting scope sheet on most projects
  regardless, as a coordination row.
- Painted MDF or wood closet and linen shelving. Some bidders carry it, rather more exclude it as
  "casework and closet shelving".
- Caulking and sealants at painted joints. Carried by about half of bidders, always bounded to
  painted-to-painted surfaces.
- Line striping at garage and parking. No bidder seen carrying this and many excluded it
  explicitly, yet an estimator may still carry it as a painting scope row on half the projects,
  priced as a plug, if needed.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like painting:

- Custom murals and hand-applied decorative finishes. Proposals have named and excluded them; no
  bidder on those projects carried them.
- Limewash and other manufacturer-proprietary decorative wall finishes. Some proposals carry it and
  others may name and exclude it on the same project, which is a potential scope gap.
- Intumescent and fire-rated coatings. About half of bidders exclude them explicitly and no bidder
  seen carrying them.
- Electrostatic coating at elevator doors and similar factory-quality metal finishes. Some bidders
  exclude it; none carried it.
- Metallic and specialty architectural paints at elevator surrounds and guard rails. Bidders
  may carry it, maybe after the estimator pushes a scope sheet at them; the estimator might plug it on the rest.
- Textile, acoustic and sound panel systems. A proposal might carry it, but the estimator's own note 
  assigned it to the drywall contractor, so beware of the same item being claimed by two trades.

## 2. How the market bids

Painting is a nearly degenerate proposal trade. The default output is one number and a bullet list.

- Single lump sum with no internal breakout: the majority of proposals, on every project.
- Segmented into two to four numbers: commonly, on every project. When a proposal segments, it
  usually segments by scope family (painting / exterior / wallcovering), and less often by building
  or by area. It essentially never segments by element.
- Occasionally priced line by line. Almost never carrying measured quantities with units and unit prices.
  Sometimes priced by area segment even with no quantity at all.

So the realistic expectation is that a painting proposal will not tell you quantities and will not
tell you how the number decomposes. Almost never a takeoff, and even then, that
bidder may rank near the top of the estimator's price list, so quantified does not mean competitive.

What a proposal typically will state:

- A list of included surfaces, phrased either as surfaces (walls, ceilings, doors and frames, base,
  trim, stairs) or as coating systems per substrate (substrate, primer coats and product class,
  finish coats and sheen). The coating-system framing shows up commonly.
- The basis document: a named drawing set with a date, and sometimes a spec section. Nearly all name
  a set and date; naming the spec section is commonplace but far from universal.
- Labor type, union or open shop. Stated in about half of proposals, but an estimator will record it
  for every bidder anyway, so it is treated as a first-class comparison field even when the proposal
  omits it.
- A paint manufacturer and product grade, commonly.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Quantities of any kind. Almost never present.
- Coat counts per surface, unless the proposal is written in coating-system form. Absent in most.
- Go-back and punch allowance. Stated very rarely.
- Warranty duration. Stated rarely, and usually only after an estimator asks by email.
- Insurance basis, bond cost, and project-portal or payment-application fees. Stated rarely, again
  usually only after being asked.
- Mock-up unit expectations. Almost never stated by a bidder; an estimator may carry it as a scope
  row.
- Prevailing wage applicability. Stated by some bidders, always as a negative, that the price is
  non-prevailing-wage.

Exclusion behavior splits the market cleanly and this split matters more than anything else about
proposal shape. Most proposals carry an explicit exclusion list, typically ten to twenty lines. A
minority carry no exclusions at all. A bidder with no exclusion list is not offering broader scope;
it is leaving every boundary open, and at leveling an estimator has to plug or ask on every one. A
bidder with no exclusions at all may well be the low bidder, and will still need large plugs before
it is comparable.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs:

- Most change price only, with the scope text either byte-identical or explicitly stated as
  unchanged. Movements can be large: a drop of more than half, of about a quarter, of about a fifth.
  A reason is almost never documented.
- A revision sometimes changes scope materially, adding wallcovering install, a floor sealer, or a
  counted repaint item. The trigger is usually the general contractor issuing its own enumerated
  scope sheet and the bidder answering it row by row.
- A revision may change nothing but add a deduct alternate, in response to a value-engineering
  request.

Two consequences for a reader. First, a revision in this trade is usually a negotiation artifact, not
a document-change artifact, so do not assume an R1 reflects an addendum. Second, the revision is
frequently not a document at all: it may exist only as an email sentence, and a general contractor
may re-solicit on a later set and accept a written "no change" confirmation as the revised bid.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Painting scope sheets have run 28, 40, 43 and 82 rows. Target roughly forty to eighty scope lines for
a full multifamily painting package. Fewer than about twenty-five and the sheet stops being able to
distinguish bidders: the thinnest sheet seen, at 28 rows, is where the low bidder needed the largest
plug, because the sheet had no row for the exterior scope the bidder was silent about.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Area or room type, wherever the coating system or the access changes: residential units, corridors,
  lobby and amenity, back of house, stairwells and stair enclosures, garage or parking, basement,
  mezzanine, penthouse, retail or commercial tenant space, exterior. This is the primary organizing
  axis in every estimator sheet, and commonly the axis the proposals adopt too.
- Substrate, wherever it changes the coating system: gypsum board, concrete masonry, cast in place or
  exposed concrete, hollow metal, wood, metal stair assemblies, exposed structure and deck, exposed
  mechanical and piping, existing plaster. These appear as separate priced rows in a quantified
  proposal, and commonly as separate coating-system bullets elsewhere.
- Every named finish mark that appears in the finish schedule and is not a plain paint: each
  wallcovering mark, each wall protection mark, each specialty coating mark, each sealed or coated
  floor mark, each decorative or mural mark. Most proposals cite scope by mark, as does every
  estimator sheet, and a scope sheet may list the full mark range as rows.
- Doors, frames and trim as their own group, separate from walls and ceilings, and separated again by
  wood versus hollow metal because the coating systems differ. A distinct group in every estimator
  sheet and in most proposals.
- Windows: sills, aprons and casings separately from the window units themselves. Sills and aprons
  are routinely included and the window and sash routinely excluded in the same sentence, which some
  bidders spell out, so a single "windows" line will always be ambiguous.
- Caulking and sealants, broken by joint condition: door trim to wall, window to wall, base to wall,
  counter and backsplash to wall, painted to painted generally. Some proposals enumerate by
  condition, as do half the estimator sheets.
- Preparation and general conditions as their own block: nail hole filling, general prep, light
  sanding and crack filling, protection of adjacent surfaces, priming, hoisting and lift access for
  own work, cleanup to dumpster, mock-up unit, project-portal or payment fees, insurances. A
  dedicated block on half the estimator sheets; almost never in proposals.
- Go-backs and punch, its own line, with a stated hours-per-unit basis, for the reasons set out in
  section 4.
- Existing-conditions work on renovation projects: repaint of existing to remain doors and frames,
  refinish or restain of existing millwork, repaint of existing windows, patch-and-paint of existing
  plaster, remove and reinstall of existing cornice and moulding. On a renovation project this block
  alone can run six rows, and each one tends to be answered differently by different bidders.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Walls, ceilings, soffits, columns and bulkheads within one area collapse to one line. Every
  estimator sheet seen does this, and proposals almost never separate them.
- Coat count, primer product, finish product and sheen are attributes of a line, not lines. They vary
  by substrate, which is already an enumeration axis.
- Individual door, frame or column counts collapse into a doors-and-frames line. Even a quantified
  proposal counts them as a single each-priced row.
- Room-by-room enumeration collapses to room type. No document seen prices a single room.
- Levels collapse into a level range unless the coating scope actually changes. A proposal might
  price "corridor and common area, levels 2 through 11" as one line while pricing level 1 and the
  mezzanine separately, which correctly tracks where the finish changes.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
painting, that test fires hardest on the seams in section 5 and on the specialty coatings in section
1, which is why those get lines even when the drawings show them as a single mark on a schedule.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. If a scope item is one that some bidders carry and
others exclude, and a given proposal says nothing about it, that silence is a scope gap to surface,
not an inclusion to assume. A minority of proposals are silent on wallcovering entirely while it
exists on the set.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the trade that must pick the work up, and
if no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| Excluded by the painter | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Custom millwork, casework, built-ins, amenity millwork (most bidders) | Millwork contractor, prefinished at the shop |
| Staining, clear and natural finishes; prefinished doors and trim (most bidders) | Millwork or door supplier |
| Priming and back-priming of running trim and doors (a minority) | Door and trim supplier, factory primed |
| High-performance, intumescent, electrostatic coatings (about half) | Specialty applicator, or unassigned |
| Epoxy floors, traffic coatings, floor sealer, floor finishes (about half) | Flooring package, or unassigned |
| Line striping at garage and parking (commonly) | Site or striping contractor, frequently unassigned |
| Garage perimeter walls, ceilings and floors (commonly) | Frequently unassigned, and a recurring real gap |
| Fry reglets, outside corner trims, wallcovering termination trims (about half the documents that price wallcovering) | Drywall contractor, and must be installed before the wallcovering |
| Wall patching, skim coating, plaster patching, abrasive or excessive prep (about half) | Drywall or plaster contractor |
| Fire caulking, acoustical sealant, sanitary caulking, plumbing fixture caulking, Division 10 specialty caulking (commonly) | Firestopping, drywall, plumbing, specialties |
| Caulking to unpainted surfaces or where only one side is painted (about half) | Frequently unassigned |
| Repair of damage caused by other trades; re-work; touch-up of others' damage (about half) | General contractor, or back-charged |
| Off hours, overtime, unscheduled remobilization (about half) | General contractor |
| Police details, permits, parking (some bidders) | General contractor |
| Sand blasting and lead abatement (some bidders) | Abatement contractor |
| Color coding, pipe identification, banding, stenciling, signage, room numbering (commonly) | Frequently unassigned |
| Gas meters and gas piping (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned |
| Painting interior of ducts behind grilles and registers (occasionally) | Frequently unassigned |
| Touch-up of field welds and shop-applied primer (occasionally) | Steel contractor |
| Removal and reinstallation of wall and ceiling mounted devices and fixtures (rarely) | Electrical, before and after |
| Floor protection beyond drop cloths (some bidders) | General contractor |
| Exterior work generally, with hollow metal doors and frames often the only exterior item kept (a minority exclude exterior wholesale) | Depends entirely on whether cladding arrives prefinished |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate: a repeat bidder reproduces the same exclusion list on every
project, so its presence carries no project information. Repeat bidders make up a good share of any
bid list, and they reuse their lists verbatim. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the
project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- A named finish mark that is excluded. When a bidder writes out specific mark numbers and excludes
  them, it read the finish schedule and found something it will not do. Some bidders do this.
- An exclusion justified by reference to a specific sheet or detail. A bidder might exclude unit
  entry trim and doors by pointing at a rendering sheet.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. A bidder might exclude all wallcovering on the
  stated ground that it was not shown, while the estimator's sheet keys the same wallcovering to a
  sheet and a mark. When a bidder and the general contractor disagree about whether scope exists on
  the set, that is a documents problem, not a bidder problem, and it is worth an RFI.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a penthouse, a sub-basement, a retail
  shell, a second building, an existing structure to remain. A minority of proposals carry one.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Spray application is assumed, and the price is explicitly conditioned on being allowed to spray and
  on logical sequencing with other trades; out-of-sequence work is chargeable. A minority say so
  outright.
- Doors and frames sprayed with the finish coat before the finish flooring goes in. Occasionally
  stated.
- Trim, doors and woodwork arrive factory primed; if they do not, extra charge. Commonly stated.
- The drywall substrate is the painter's problem only to the level the specification requires.
  Bidders disclaim responsibility for drywall quality control, for repairing defective metal, for
  cleaning welds and poor factory priming, and for concrete spillage left by others. Some bidders
  spell this out.
- Standard prep is bounded quantitatively: defects no larger than a coin, and if more than a stated
  percentage of any surface needs prep, it leaves the scope and goes to time and material. A couple
  of bidders state an explicit threshold and hourly rate.
- Coating standard is stated as one primer coat plus two finish coats to a named industry standard,
  or to a named painting-industry practice standard. Some bidders cite each.
- Material pricing held for a stated number of days from the proposal date. Some bidders.
- Union or open shop, which is the single largest price driver in this trade and is treated as such:
  an estimator's sheet may carry an explicit union-versus-open-shop delta line.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### Wallcovering: the seam that matters most

Wallcovering appears in four distinct commercial shapes, and the drawings will not tell you which one
applies:

1. Painter furnishes and installs. The most common shape by a wide margin.
2. Painter installs only, material furnished by the owner or by a supplier. Some bidders. When the
   material is owner-furnished, three additional scope lines appear that exist in no other
   configuration: receive, inventory, store off site and re-deliver the owner-furnished goods;
   provide the quantities the owner must order; and provide all adhesives. Those belong on the
   leveling sheet as three separate rows.
3. Painter offers it as a detachable alternate, priced separately and identically across revisions.
   Occasionally.
4. A separate specialty wallcovering vendor bids it directly and the painting bidders are silent.
   Rarely.

The specialty-vendor case is the one that exposes the real dependencies, because the vendor prices
them as conditions rather than mentioning them as courtesies:

- The substrate must be sanded smooth, dust-free and primed by others with one of two named
  wallcovering primers. The painter's ordinary primer choice can void the wallcovering warranty.
- Low-VOC and zero-VOC primers require a minimum four-week cure before wallcovering can be applied.
  That is a schedule constraint hidden inside a materials note, and it belongs on the scope line.
- Termination trim (fry reglet, outside corner trim) is installed by the drywall contractor before
  the wallcovering, not after.
- Specific marks must be installed before millwork, mailboxes, kickplates and door hardware, with a
  stated substantial cost increase otherwise.
- The vendor's priced scope is defined by its own shop drawing, not by the contract documents, and it
  explicitly warns the general contractor to compare against its own takeoff. Two definitions of the
  same scope have to be reconciled at leveling.
- Wallcovering material is thought about in linear yards, while the drawings and the general
  contractor think in square feet, and a mark's material quantity may be stated as not
  square-foot-based at all.

### Other seams

- Doors and frames. They arrive factory primed from the door supplier; the painter applies the
  finish, and on wood doors finishes all six sides. Whether "all six sides" is in the scope is called
  out explicitly by some bidders and on half the estimator sheets, which means it is contested often
  enough to be worth its own words.
- Concrete floor sealer and epoxy floors. Genuinely contested between painting and flooring. An
  estimator's note on the row may read that it is carried in flooring, and a bidder may offer a
  credit to remove it on that basis.
- FRP panels, textile panels and acoustic panels. Appear on finish schedules alongside paint marks
  and get pulled toward painting, but the estimator's own notes on those rows assign them to drywall.
  Beware of a bidder pricing a textile panel the estimator has already noted as drywall's.
- Exterior cladding. The dominant exterior variable is whether siding, trim, fascia and panels arrive
  prefinished or are field painted. A bidder's own value-engineering suggestion may be to switch to a
  prefinished cladding product, which takes a large sum out of its number; and field-painting the
  cladding can be worth more than the entire rest of the exterior scope, carried as an add alternate
  by one bidder and inside the base bid by another. This is a materials decision owned by another
  trade that swings the painting number hardest, and it does so on about half of projects.
- Exposed structure, deck and mechanical. Sometimes painted with a dryfall system, sometimes excluded
  outright, in roughly equal measure. A bidder may price it as a procedure (dust removal, solvent
  wipe of the mechanical, single dryfall coat) rather than as a surface.
- Millwork joints. Bidders assume the carpenter delivers tight, true miters that do not need caulk to
  fill, and some say so. If the carpentry scope does not carry that, it lands back on the painter as
  extra work.
- Electrical, media and load panels. Expected prefinished; painting them is extra, as some bidders
  spell out.
- Plaster on renovation work. The painter's line is "paint only", with patching, skim coating and
  fire-rating restoration belonging to the plaster or drywall contractor. On renovation work an
  estimator may carry six separate plaster rows, and bidders answer all of them as paint only.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

This is the section the drawings cannot cover, and it is the reason a scope line has to carry more
than a surface and a quantity.

What painters actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The finish schedule and the room finish schedule. This is the primary document. Marks come from
   here (paint marks, wallcovering marks, sealed-floor marks, specialty marks), and most proposals
   cite marks or the schedule directly. A bidder may use the mark range itself as its entire scope
   statement.
2. The unit count. A minority of proposals state a unit count as the scope denominator, but every
   estimator sheet normalizes every bid to a cost per unit and ranks on it. Unit count is the leveling
   currency of this trade. If the set does not make the unit count unambiguous, every bid becomes
   incomparable.
3. The door schedule, for the doors-and-frames line and the wood-versus-hollow-metal split.
4. Area and level organization, for the corridor, amenity, back of house and garage segmentation.
5. The specification, mostly for coat counts, the coating standard and the paint manufacturer.
   Bidders cite the section number but rarely quote anything from it.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff. Measured quantities are almost never present. The
normal method is area-type plus unit count plus judgment.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Union versus open shop labor. The largest single driver.
- Prevailing wage applicability.
- Whether spray application will be permitted and whether the sequencing will hold.
- The floor release schedule and the turnover sequence, which drive punch and remobilization.
- How many go-back hours per unit the general contractor expects. See below.
- The warranty duration required. The stated market default is one year; an owner may want two, a
  specification requirement invisible on the drawings, and the adder is material.
- Insurance basis (whether an owner-controlled program applies, and the cost of conventional general
  liability if it does not), and bond cost. Both are quoted as a percentage of contract value,
  rarely, and usually only after an estimator asks by email.
- Project portal and payment-application fees. Sometimes an estimator scope row, almost never in a
  proposal.
- Whether a mock-up unit is expected.
- Whether trim and doors arrive primed.
- Whether millwork and casework arrive prefinished.
- Whether wallcovering is owner-furnished.
- Whether exterior cladding arrives prefinished.
- Site access, staging, parking availability and free parking. A bidder may make it an explicit
  assumption.
- Ceiling height and lift access. A quantified proposal will band its rates by height ("up to twelve
  feet"), so height is a rate variable, not just an access nuisance.
- Occupied or phased work, and any restriction to normal business hours.

The go-back problem is worth stating on its own because it is the single most consistent gap between
what painters bid and what the work costs. A stated go-back and punch allowance is almost never
carried. On about half of projects an estimator adds a go-back and punch plug at four hours per unit,
at a labor rate, to every bidder's number before comparing them. Where a bidder does state its
allowance, it may carry two hours per unit and charge an hourly rate beyond that, which is half the
estimator's assumption. Any painting scope line set should carry a go-backs and punch line with an
explicit hours-per-unit basis, because no proposal will supply it.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders openly price around missing information and say
so: door and frame types "not shown, assuming"; wallcovering priced "with the allowance provided per
finish schedule" or at a carried rate per linear yard rather than a quantity. Design-development
proposals commonly do exactly this. Treat a wallcovering line at design development as an allowance
carry, not a quantity. By construction documents and conformed sets, the same bidders switch to
citing mark ranges and counted quantities. Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its folder's
set stage occasionally disagree: a bid may price a construction-document round off the earlier permit
set and say so, or cite two different sets in the same document.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet for wall and ceiling area, banded by substrate and by height. The priced unit in a
  quantified proposal, and the basis for plugs on most estimator sheets.
- Linear feet for base and running trim. Rarely.
- Each for doors with frames and both faces, and for columns. Rarely.
- Linear yards for wallcovering material, which is the wallcovering supply chain's unit and does not
  match the square feet the drawings imply. Some documents use it, including a specialty vendor.
- Square feet for wallcovering installed area, which is how the general contractor asks for it.
  Rarely, and usually supplied by email on request rather than in the proposal.
- Locations or each for miscellaneous metal items such as guard rails, elevator door frames and
  access panels. On half the estimator sheets.
- Hours for go-backs, punch and time-and-material repair, always expressed per unit for go-backs.
  Some proposals and half the estimator sheets.
- Gallons of paint and of primer, as an internal rollup. Rarely.
- Units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator sheet.
- Percent of contract value, for bond and for general liability insurance. Rarely.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Labor type, union versus open shop. Tracked on every bidder in every leveling, and a sheet may
   carry an explicit union delta line.
2. Whether spray application and clean sequencing are available. Named as the explicit basis of the
   "competitive" price by a minority of bidders, with additional charges stated otherwise.
3. Total finished surface area, driven by unit count and by how much amenity and common area the
   project carries.
4. Coat count and product grade. Contractor-grade product and a three-coat system are the defaults.
5. Substrate mix. Masonry needs block filler, exposed concrete needs a masonry primer, metal needs a
   direct-to-metal system, existing plaster needs prep the painter will disclaim.
6. Whether the exterior cladding is field painted.
7. Whether wallcovering is in, out, or owner-furnished. A deduct alternate removing wallcovering and
   substituting painted accent walls can be worth about ninety-five percent of the wallcovering
   segment, so it behaves as a nearly pure add-or-delete block.
8. Ceiling height and lift access.
9. Prep level actually required, versus the drywall finish level specified.
10. Number of colors and the presence of accent walls or stripes. A bidder may price accent colors
    and stripes differing from the drawings as a per-square-foot adder plus material.
11. Go-back hours per unit.
12. Off hours, overtime, remobilization count and phasing.
13. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per unit runs wide, seen at 1.5 times, 2.3 times, 2.65 times and 6.3 times.
Bidder counts per project run from twelve to twenty-two. A painting bid list is wide and noisy, and
the top of the range is usually a bidder who has priced a different job, not a bidder who is
expensive.

One more pricing convention worth knowing: an estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base
bid plus adjustments, where adjustments are plugs added to every bidder to cover what the market
systematically will not carry. The plugs to expect are go-backs and punch, caulking and sealants,
exterior painting, garage painting and striping, sealed concrete at back of house, epoxy floors, FRP
panels, exposed piping, and specialty metallic paint at miscellaneous locations. Those nine items are,
in effect, the trade's structural gap list, and a scope line set that names all nine explicitly is
doing the estimator's plugging for it.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on a few dozen painting-package proposals, one specialty wallcovering
vendor quote, and four estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in
one regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The proposals span design development,
permit, construction documents and conformed sets, and the bidder pool includes several companies
that appear on more than one project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal
distinction in section 4 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back of house space, in
  one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors,
  institutional, healthcare, industrial or public prevailing-wage work.
- Only one project involved renovation of an existing building, so the existing-conditions scope
  block in section 3 rests on that project alone.
- Only one proposal contained a measured takeoff, so every quantity convention in section 7 that
  cites unit prices rests on that single document.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation and which were bought out.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the go-back and sequencing qualifications
  convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed in the
  field, so treat it as untested here.
- The corpus has not shown interior wall protection systems (corner guards, crash rails, wall
  panels), which typically attach to this package in healthcare and institutional work.
- The corpus has not shown shop-applied versus field-applied finishes on structural steel, and it
  does not reveal where intumescent coating is actually bought when the painter excludes it.
- The corpus has not shown how painting is packaged when there is no wallcovering on the project at
  all. Every project here carried at least one wallcovering mark.
