# Overhead and Coiling Doors

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the overhead and
coiling door package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator
leveling sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried
by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real door companies can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

This is a small, high value, product-driven package. On a mid-rise residential building it is
usually three to five doors, and it is bought from a manufacturer's dealer rather than from a
contractor.

The most important packaging fact is that this package and the elevator smoke curtain package are
sold by the same companies, and very often in the same document. Of the bidders seen, most quote
both scopes, and the majority of documents in the corpus were filed under two trade tokens at once
because they physically are one file. The same document also reaches into trash chutes and
compactors on some bidders' letterhead. Treat overhead doors, smoke curtains and refuse equipment as
three scopes sold by one market, and expect to separate them yourself.

The practical consequence: never level a printed total in this trade. A printed total in this
package commonly spans the doors and the curtains, and sometimes the chutes as well. The base bid
for the door package is a block subtotal inside the document, not the number at the bottom.

Section numbers are weak identifiers here and cannot be relied on to route scope:

- Coiling doors have been cited as 083300, 083310, 083323 and 083326 across the corpus, and one
  bidder moved the same unchanged sectional door from one section to another between its own rounds.
- Sectional doors appear under 083600 and 083610, and also under a coiling door section.
- Smoke curtains have been cited as 083300, 083400, 083343 and 083480, sometimes under the same
  section as the coiling doors in the same document.

The trade token and the product description are strong identifiers. The section number is not.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that this package pulls in even though it sits elsewhere:

- Elevator smoke curtains, as above. Near universal.
- Trash and recycle chutes, chute doors, sanitizing units and compactors. Carried by some bidders
  as another block in the same document.
- Low voltage control wiring from the operator to the controller. Commonly carried, and it is a
  named priced line in the itemized proposals.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package:

- Fire shutters and accordion or folding fire doors. Sold by a different specialty market and, on
  the projects seen, bid against the smoke curtain scope rather than the door scope.
- Line voltage power, conduit, disconnects and fire alarm interface. Excluded by every bidder seen.
- Openings, jambs, lintels, structural supports, hood supports and finishes. Excluded by every
  bidder seen.

## 2. How the market bids

The proposal in this trade is a product configuration list. What is being sold is a specific
manufacturer's model at a specific size with a specific operator and specific safety devices, and
the price attaches to that configuration rather than to an opening on a drawing.

Three proposal shapes account for essentially the whole market:

1. A priced line table, one line per product family, with quantity, unit price and extended price,
   followed by a specification block naming the model, the size, the finish and the operator. The
   most common shape and the most useful, because the unit price is directly readable.
2. A section-referenced list, one price per specification section, with the doors under each section
   listed by size and count as bullets. Common. Unit prices are recoverable only where a section
   holds one door.
3. A component enumeration, one block per door with every component of that door listed as a
   sub-bullet, sometimes keyed to the door schedule mark, and one price for everything. Seen from a
   minority of bidders, and it is the shape that tells you the most about what is actually included.

Counts and sizes are almost always stated. This is the one trade in this cluster where the bidder
tells you exactly what it is pricing.

What a proposal will normally state:

- The manufacturer and the model number of each door. Nearly always.
- The clear opening width and height of each door. Nearly always.
- The operator: horsepower, voltage, phase, control station type, and whether an auxiliary chain
  hoist is included.
- The safety devices: sensing edge, photo eyes, monitored safety device, warning light and buzzer.
- Whether freight, installation and tax are inside the number. Usually stated as one-word answers
  in a summary block.

What a proposal will normally not state:

- The door schedule mark. A minority key their lines to marks. Where a bidder does, its proposal is
  directly checkable against the schedule and everyone else's is not.
- Fire rating in minutes. Products are named as fire doors or by model, and the rating is left to
  the model number. This matters, because the rating is a live contradiction on real sets.
- Warranty duration. Almost never volunteered, and reliably produced within a day when asked.
- Insurance basis, bond cost, general liability as a separate figure. Almost never volunteered, and
  again produced on request.
- Lead time. Rarely stated for doors, unlike the curtain half of the same market.

Revision habits. A revision in this trade is usually a negotiation artifact or a commercial answer,
not a documents answer. The shapes seen, in rough order of frequency:

- The same quote body refiled at a later set stage with nothing changed, sometimes with a new quote
  number so it ranks as a second competitive row. Watch for a page footer that still carries the
  original print date while the letterhead date advances.
- A labor line restructured with no change to product, count or size. A crew and duration line
  becoming a per hour line has been seen to take several percent out of a number twice, on two
  projects, with everything else byte-identical. This is not a scope change and should not be read
  as one.
- A scope line added or removed. A door entering the list raises the number visibly and is the
  cleanest revision in the trade.
- A product class change with no deviation statement. A high speed door replaced by a standard
  rolling steel door at the same opening takes a third out of a package while reading as a price
  drop. Diff the model numbers, not the totals.
- Commercial answers appended by email: a warranty add, a general liability credit, a workers
  compensation figure, a carry for something discussed on the phone. These are frequently the entire
  substance of a round and exist only in the email thread wrapping the quote.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets in this trade have run about twenty to forty-five rows for three to five doors, and
most of those rows are reference material rather than priced scope: the specification's approved
manufacturer list, the related sheets, and the basis of design product. The priced rows are few.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every door, individually, by its schedule mark. This is the primary axis. A door is an each, and
  the market prices it as an each, so a scope line per door mark is the grain the market can answer
  without translation. Where marks exist on the schedule, use them; the bidders who use them are the
  ones whose bids can be checked.
- Opening size, on the line. Width and height change the price materially and are the only thing
  distinguishing two otherwise identical doors.
- Door type as a separate line even at the same opening size, because the product classes do not
  substitute for each other: rolling service door, insulated rolling door, rolling grille, high
  speed rolling door, sectional door, fire rated coiling door, coiling counter shutter. A high speed
  door and a standard rolling steel door at the same opening differ by a factor that dwarfs any
  competitive spread.
- Fire rating, as its own attribute on the line and as its own row where a rating is required.
  Ratings are a documented conflict source; see section 6.
- The operator and its control scheme, as its own line per door: horsepower, phase, control station
  location, auxiliary chain hoist, and specifically what activates the door. Whether a door opens on
  an interior push button, a motion sensor, a card or tag reader, or a loop is a real scope
  difference that some bidders bury inside a product description. A bid excluding all activation
  beyond an interior push button is not comparable to one carrying motion sensors and a wide scan
  sensor, and neither will say so in an exclusion list.
- Safety devices as a line: sensing edge, photo eyes, monitored safety device, warning light.
- Low voltage control wiring, its own line, because it is the boundary that moves. Some bidders
  carry it inside the door price, some price it as a separate line, and the scope of what it covers
  differs between them.
- Finish, its own line where a custom color or an anodized finish is called for. Standard color and
  custom powder coat are different prices and the schedule usually does not distinguish them.
- Elevator smoke curtains, always their own package with their own rows, even though the same bidder
  will price both. See the smoke curtain entry.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Identical doors at the same size, type, operator and finish collapse into one line with a count.
  Bidders price them that way and splitting them buys nothing.
- Slat gauge, insulation R-value, cycle rating, windlock type, hood material and bottom bar profile
  are attributes of the line, not lines. They belong in the line's description because they are the
  substitution surface, but they are not separately biddable.
- Freight and delivery collapse into the line. Nearly every bidder includes them and none price them
  separately.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be silent
about it, and the three outcomes produce meaningfully different numbers. In this trade the test
fires hardest on four things, and they are the four rows a thin sheet is missing: activation method,
control wiring extent, fire rating, and whether tax is inside the number.

Silence is a finding here in a particular way. Because bidders enumerate their products so
completely, an opening that appears on the drawings and does not appear in a proposal's product list
is a real gap, not an assumed inclusion. Comparing the proposal's own count against the schedule's
count is the single highest-value check in this trade and it is almost always possible, which is not
true of the other trades in this cluster.

### The structural gap list

The scope no bidder carries, which the estimator must add to every number before comparing:

1. Line voltage power to the operator, conduit, raceways, disconnects, boxes and final connections.
   Excluded by every bidder seen, without exception.
2. Fire alarm interface and the tie-in that drops a fire-rated door or a curtain on alarm. Excluded
   universally, and the party who owns it is frequently an open question on the leveling sheet
   rather than an assignment.
3. Opening preparation: framing, jambs, lintels, structural supports for the hood and springs,
   saw cutting, and blocking. Excluded universally.
4. Field painting and finishes at the opening. Excluded universally.
5. Warranty beyond the manufacturer's standard. Excluded universally and priced only on request.
6. Access panels where a product is mounted above a ceiling.
7. Bond and general liability where an owner-controlled program does not apply.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

The exclusion list in this trade is short, near-identical between competitors, and almost pure
boilerplate. Three lines cover most of it and they are printed on the form: electrical by others,
opening preparation by others, standard warranty only. Reading exclusion lists here yields almost no
differentiation, which is precisely why the differentiation has to be found in the product
descriptions instead.

| Excluded by the door contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Line voltage conduit, wiring, disconnects, boxes, final connections (every bidder seen) | Electrical contractor |
| Fire alarm work, tie-in, smoke detection devices (every bidder seen) | Fire alarm contractor, frequently unassigned at bid time |
| Framing, jambs, lintels, structural and hood supports, saw cutting (every bidder seen) | General contractor, structural or masonry |
| Field painting and finishes at the opening (commonly) | Painting |
| Roof curbs where chutes share the document (where chutes are carried) | Roofing or general contractor |
| Access panels above ceilings (commonly, on curtain scope) | Drywall |
| Warranty beyond manufacturer standard (every bidder seen) | Priced on request as an add |
| Permits (occasionally) | General contractor |
| Maintenance and service after completion (some bidders) | Offered directly to the end user |

Qualifications that are really pricing bases, and each of these converts the number if violated:

- Tariff clauses. The strongest commercial qualification in this trade and close to universal in the
  later corpus. Two shapes are seen: prices must be revalidated before an order is accepted, or the
  seller reserves the right to require an approved change order for any supplier increase announced
  after the proposal date. A bid without a tariff clause and a bid with one are not the same
  instrument.
- Validity windows measured in days, commonly thirty, with an explicit right to reprice on a
  manufacturer increase.
- Field verification. Prices are subject to field verification and dimensions may move; this is
  stated commonly and is a real risk on renovation work where openings are existing.
- Contract terms. Some bidders make the price subject to mutually agreeable contract terms, and one
  requires a signed quote plus a deposit before material release, which is a schedule instrument as
  much as a commercial one.
- Labor basis. Stated by about half of bidders. Open shop is the common answer on this work. Where
  both bases appear in one bid list, the union bidders have been the more expensive, but the corpus
  is too thin for that to be a rule, so record it rather than assume its direction. The same company
  has been seen pricing prevailing wage on one project and open shop on another, so labor basis is a
  project fact, not a company attribute.
- Certification. Factory certified installers are required for listed products, which constrains who
  may install regardless of price and can eliminate a bidder rather than reprice it.

Where the exclusion list does carry project signal, it looks like this: a numbered objection to a
specific specification paragraph. A bidder writing out that a named clause cannot be met by any
manufacturer, or that the specification body and its own named basis of design product describe
different products, has read the specification and found a defect. Those paragraphs are the
project's RFI list and should be routed upstream, not absorbed at leveling.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

- Electrical. The defining seam of this trade and it has three positions, not two. Some bidders
  carry all low voltage conduit, wiring and connections from the door to the controller and stop at
  the line voltage feed. Others carry factory-wired operators and controls only, set in place, with
  every raceway by the electrician. Others price control wiring as a named separate line. All three
  read as electrical by others in a summary. The line to write is not electrical by others; it is
  who runs the low voltage between the operator, the controller and the activation devices.
- Fire alarm. A fire rated coiling door and a smoke curtain both drop on an alarm signal, and no
  bidder in the corpus carries the tie-in. This is the most reliably unassigned scope in the
  package. Name the party on the scope line.
- Structure and masonry. Hood supports, spring supports, jambs and lintels are excluded by everyone
  and are frequently drawn as a detail rather than scheduled, so nobody prices them and nobody
  notices until the door arrives.
- Painting. Field painting at openings is excluded here and, on the painting side, hollow metal and
  miscellaneous metal at exteriors is often the only exterior item a painter keeps. Confirm the door
  and its frame have an owner on both sides of the seam.
- Doors, frames and hardware. Coiling and overhead doors are carved out of the door, frame and
  hardware package by the market and go to this specialty. The seam is worth checking in one
  direction: the fire rating stated in the door, frame and hardware schedule and the rating in the
  specification for the same coiling door have been observed to disagree on a real set, and an
  estimator recorded it as a contradiction on the leveling sheet.
- Refuse equipment. Where chutes and compactors ride in the same document, the chute scope carries
  its own by-others list including roof curbs, plumbing connections and smoke detection. Separate
  the blocks before leveling either.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What door contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The door schedule, for marks, sizes and types. This is the primary document, and the minority of
   bidders who key their lines to marks are working from it directly.
2. Wall sections and enlarged details at the opening, for mount condition. Whether a door is between
   jambs or face of wall, and whether headroom is standard or low, comes from the detail and changes
   the product. Low headroom is a named model variant, not a note.
3. The specification, for the approved manufacturer list and the basis of design product. Bidders
   cite the section number and then quote a product, and the whole competitive question is whether
   that product is on the list.
4. Elevations, for finish and color.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- What activates the door. Push button, motion sensor, card or tag reader, loop detector, or a
  combination. Drawings show a door; they do not show its activation scheme, and the spread between
  a push-button-only bid and a fully sensored one is real.
- Cycle rating expected, which is an operations question about how hard the door will be used and is
  never on a sheet.
- Whether the opening will be ready when the installer mobilizes, which drives a remobilization
  charge nobody prices in the base.
- Phase and voltage available at the door, frequently left as to be determined in the proposal
  itself.
- Tariff exposure at the time of purchase.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies.
- Whether the specified product is actually procurable. This is the trade's sharpest documents
  problem. A specification whose named basis of design product is a different class from the product
  its own body describes has been seen and disclosed by a bidder as an exception to the entire
  section. So has a specified feature that no manufacturer makes. Where a bidder objects clause by
  clause, believe it and raise an RFI.

The rating contradiction deserves its own note because it is the one documents defect the estimator
found rather than the market. Where the fire rating for the same coiling door differs between the
specification and the door, frame and hardware schedule, no bidder will surface it, because bidders
price a model number and the model carries whatever rating it carries. It surfaces only when someone
reads both documents against each other. A scope line carrying an explicit rating in minutes forces
the question at bid time rather than at submittal.

Set-stage behavior. Product class is not stable across set stages in this trade. On one project the
same bidder carried high speed doors at design development and standard rolling steel doors at
permit, at the same openings, for a third less. On another, opening sizes moved between sets in both
directions. Never compute a round-over-round delta without diffing the product model and the opening
sizes first; in this trade a price change is more often a product change than a market movement.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, per door. The universal unit. Every itemized proposal prices per door and the estimator
  normalizes to cost per door.
- Lump sum for control wiring, priced as one line covering all doors.
- Percent, for a stated expediting or tariff adder where one exists.
- Crew days or installer hours, where a bidder exposes its labor line. Uncommon but revealing, and
  the only place in this trade where labor is separable from product.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Product class at the same opening. High speed versus standard rolling steel is the single largest
   variable in the package and can be worth a factor of two or more on a door.
2. Whether the specified basis of design product is actually what the specification body describes.
   Where those conflict, bidders price different things and the resulting spread is a definition
   failure rather than a competitive one.
3. Opening size and count.
4. Fire rating and the release mechanism.
5. Activation scheme and sensor package.
6. Finish: standard color versus custom powder coat versus anodized.
7. Cycle rating and spring specification.
8. Whether low voltage control wiring is inside the number.
9. Labor basis and crew loading.
10. Tariff exposure and validity window at the time of purchase.
11. Tax treatment, which is inside the number for most bidders and a separate line for some.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. Normalized to cost per door, the spread within a
single project has run about 1.6 times and about 1.75 times on the two projects where enough
bidders competed. Bid lists in this trade are short: three to five bidders is normal, and one
project drew a single bidder across two rounds. That is a thin market, and a thin market rewards
scope definition far more than it rewards competition.

What that spread does not prove. A 1.75 times spread per door on a package of four doors is not a
measure of competitiveness, because on that same project the bidders were not pricing the same
doors: opening sizes differed between rounds, one bidder's high speed door was another's standard
rolling steel, and the two most expensive columns belonged to one company's two rounds of the same
scope ranked side by side. Cost per door is also a weak denominator when the doors are not alike;
four doors of four types averaged together produce a rate that describes no door on the project.
Where the sheet's own scope rows carry marks and sizes and the bidders answer them line by line, the
comparison is real. Where the sheet carries one furnish-and-install row, the per-door rate is
arithmetic performed on incomparable things. Both shapes appear in the corpus.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about twenty overhead and coiling door proposals from seven companies,
plus three estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four mid-rise residential projects in one regional
market, all bid by one general contractor. Proposals span design development, permit, construction
document and conformed sets, and several companies appear on more than one project, which is what
makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable.

This is a thin corpus and the entry is written to that. Packages ran three to five doors each, so
nothing here is tested at the scale where the trade behaves differently.

What the entry cannot yet claim:

- Nothing here comes from a project with a large door count. Parking structures, distribution,
  industrial and healthcare work all carry orders of magnitude more doors and would likely produce
  unit pricing, tiered rates and volume behavior none of these documents show.
- The corpus has not shown a counter shutter, a security grille at a retail frontage, a bi-fold or
  hangar door, or an exterior sectional door at a loading dock in a cold climate, so the product
  vocabulary in section 3 is only as wide as four residential buildings made it.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that field verification and opening readiness
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language, not observed.
- Only one estimator sheet recorded a fire rating contradiction, so that finding rests on a single
  project, though the mechanism behind it is general.
- Labor basis appears almost entirely as open shop. There is not enough union pricing here to say
  anything about the delta, and the corpus explicitly contradicts any assumption that the direction
  is stable, since one bidder priced prevailing wage on one project and open shop on another.
- The corpus has not shown how this package is bought when it is large enough to be its own contract
  rather than a purchase order, and the commercial terms seen, deposits, signed quotes before
  material release, thirty day validity, are purchase order terms.
