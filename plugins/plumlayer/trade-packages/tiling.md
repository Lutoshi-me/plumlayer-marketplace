# Tiling

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the tiling package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The entry
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real tile setters can bid it and a real estimator can
level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 09 3000 (Tiling) is the anchor, and it is what the leveling sheet is keyed to on every
project. Proposals themselves are more likely to head their pricing block with the older 09300
Ceramic Tile, and the two are used interchangeably. Where an acoustic underlayment is specified it
carries its own section number in the 09 6000 range, and that number appears on the tiling scope
sheet rather than on a separate one, which is the first sign that this package pulls in more than
tile.

The most important packaging fact about this trade is that it is rarely bid alone. The companies who
bid it are floorcovering houses, and most of them price the whole floorcovering package with tile as
one segment inside it: resilient plank, carpet, wood, rubber tread and riser, vinyl and rubber base,
sealed concrete, entrance mat. On any given bid list some bidders return a tile-only number and
others return a floorcovering number several times its size, and both land in the same ranking. This
is the single largest reading hazard in the trade. A whole-package number ranked against a tile-only
number is not a high bidder, it is a different scope, and every leveling sheet seen contains
at least one.

Two other lanes bid against the same package and neither one is furnish-and-install. Tile
distributors and manufacturers quote the material alone, delivered, sometimes with the setting
materials and backer board included and sometimes with waste and attic stock folded into the stated
quantities. Separately, a tile setter may bid installation labour alone against material somebody
else is furnishing. Both appear on the ranking next to complete bids, the supply-only number lands
at the bottom and the labour-only number lands in the middle, and neither is comparable to anything
else on the sheet unless the package mode is recorded against it.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another spec section:

- Waterproofing membrane and crack isolation membrane at wet areas and at floors. Universally
  understood to be the tile setter's to install when it is carried at all, but whether it is in the
  base bid, in an alternate, or excluded outright splits the market three ways. See section 5.
- Acoustic underlayment mat under bathroom and corridor floors. Specified in another section and
  bid by the tile setter more often than not, though a good share exclude it and it is the most
  commonly plugged line on the sheet.
- Cement backer board and tile underlayment. Excluded by most bidders and carried by a few, with at
  least one bidder pricing it as an add alternate rather than taking a position.
- Stone and quartz thresholds and saddles at bathroom entries and unit entries. Nearly always the
  tile setter's, though which of the two locations is included varies.
- Metal edge trims, transition profiles, and termination strips. Nearly always the tile setter's,
  and always worth their own line because a bidder will include them at some locations and exclude
  them at others inside the same bullet list.
- Floor preparation and self levelling. Almost every bidder includes a token amount of minor prep
  and prices anything beyond it hourly or as an allowance.
- Floor protection over finished tile. Split evenly between base bid, alternate, and exclusion.
- Gypcrete sealer. Sometimes carried as labour only with the sealer furnished by others, sometimes
  carried outright at named levels, commonly excluded.
- Interior brick and thin brick at feature walls. Marked on the finish schedule alongside tile and
  pulled toward this package, usually as an add.
- Elevator cab floor tile. Frequently material-only, with the install by the elevator contractor.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like tile:

- Cast marble and cultured marble shower surrounds and pans. The largest carve-out in the trade and
  the one that reshapes the whole package. See section 5.
- Shower doors and shower glass. Ordinarily a separate glazing or specialties package, though a cast
  marble supplier may quote the doors inside its own panel package.
- Solid surface finishes, countertops, and window sills. Excluded by tile bidders as routinely done
  by others.
- Terrazzo, pedestal pavers, and poured decorative floor finishes. Excluded when named.
- Historic marble reinstallation, refurbishment and repair on renovation work. Appears on the tiling
  scope sheet as a block of rows and the tile bidders answer all of them negative.
- Acoustical wall panels, reinforced plastic panels, and wood wall finishes, which sit on the same
  finish schedule and are excluded.

## 2. How the market bids

Tiling is an itemized trade, and the discriminating fact about a proposal is not how many numbers it
carries but at what grain it enumerates. Four grains appear, and the same bidder will move between
them.

Enumeration by finish schedule mark is the dominant grain by a wide margin. The proposal lists
TL-01, TF-3, TW-8B, U-TW-1 or whatever the architect's mark series is, usually with the manufacturer,
colour, size, thickness and finish transcribed from the schedule, and often with a square footage
against each. This is what most bidders do at construction documents and conformed sets.

Enumeration by unit type is the second grain and the most useful one for leveling. A bidder prices
each bathroom type or each unit type as a single each, with the count of that type and the wall and
floor area of one instance stated in the line. One bidder does this purely, quoting a
price per bathroom across eight bathroom types. Another does a hybrid, pricing three lump lines but
enumerating every mark inside every unit type with that type's count behind it. Where a bid arrives
in this shape it is directly comparable to any other bid on the project in a way that mark-based
bids are not, and no estimator sheet asks for it.

Enumeration by assembly element is the third grain and the most revealing about the shower. The
bidder prices waterproof shower walls, waterproof tub surround walls, the pitched mud bed under the
pan, the tiled shower floor and the setting materials as separate elements rather than as marks.
Bidders that price this way are the ones whose exclusion lists actually name shower niches, curbs
and drains.

A bare lump sum with no grain at all is what you get at earlier set stages. This is worth stating
plainly because it is a set stage effect and not a bidder effect: the same company on the same
project returned a single number split only into material and labour at permit set, and returned a
mark-enumerated, area-segmented proposal at construction documents. Do not read a lump sum at
design development or permit as a bidder that will not itemise.

Where a proposal segments its total, the axis is area crossed with material family. Units, corridors
and amenity are the near-universal area split, and inside each one the bidder totals tile separately
from resilient, carpet, base, waterproofing, preparation and protection. One bidder uses an
identical segment matrix on every project it appears on, which is a house format rather than a
response to any particular set. Segmentation by building and by level appears where the project has
more than one building.

Package mode is a first-class attribute of a tiling bid and has to be recorded before anything is
compared. Furnish and install is the default. Furnish only comes from distributors and
manufacturers. Install only comes from setters bidding against owner-furnished or
supplier-furnished material. Partial appears inside otherwise complete bids: one bidder carries
furnish and install everywhere except the elevator cabs, where it is material only, and says so on
its own line.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The finish marks it is pricing, transcribed with manufacturer, colour, size and finish. Nearly
  always at construction documents.
- The drawing set date it priced from, and often the architect's name and addenda acknowledged.
  Nearly always.
- Whether sales tax is included. Nearly always, and it is almost always in.
- Its own square footage for the major marks. Commonly, and those square footages do not agree
  between bidders on the same marks.
- Whether minor floor preparation is included and what the hourly rate is beyond it. Commonly.
- Regular business hours as the pricing basis. Commonly.
- Whether waterproofing is in the base bid or offered as an alternate. Commonly, and this is the
  question that most changes what the number means.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Labour type, union or open shop. Rarely stated, and an estimator records it for every bidder
  anyway.
- A waste factor. Stated by very few, and where it is stated it runs from ten to fifteen percent and
  is folded into the quantity, which makes that bidder's square footage incomparable to a measured
  area.
- Attic stock, unless the specification calls it out. Where it does appear it runs at three to five
  percent and it is a priced line, not a note.
- Movement joints and control joints. Excluded outright by several bidders in so many words, carried
  by very few, and silent in most. This is a real gap and it is discussed in section 3.
- Grout sealing. Commonly excluded or answered as not applicable.
- Whether the tile itself is a real price or an allowance carry. At earlier set stages it frequently
  is an allowance and the proposal may not say so.
- Warranty term. Stated occasionally, usually only when the general contractor asks.
- Bond and insurance basis, which come out as alternates when the scope sheet demands them.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs:

- Some revisions move the price and nothing else, with the takeoff byte for byte identical months
  apart. That is a negotiation artifact, not a document artifact, and the movement can be large in
  either direction.
- Some add a single scope item, and the item is very often the acoustic underlayment or the
  waterproofing. Where that happens the delta between the two rounds is the cleanest price for that
  item you will get, because everything else is held constant.
- Some restructure the breakout without moving much money, presenting the same scope on a different
  matrix. In this trade a restructure carries information even when the total is unchanged, because
  the new breakout usually exposes a line the earlier round buried.
- Some are brand package substitutions, where the specified tile is swapped mark for mark for a
  volume manufacturer's equivalent and only the material component moves. This is the trade's
  standard value engineering move and it is discussed in section 7.
- Some correct an error. One revision's substantive change was fixing a jobsite address that named
  the wrong town entirely, and changing the stated pricing basis from a takeoff the general
  contractor had furnished to the drawings themselves.

Two warnings about revisions. A document filed and named as a later revision of a tile bid may be a
separate scope proposal altogether: one carried the name of a second revision and contained only
floor protection, with no tile in it. And a proposal may exist only inside an email thread, with the
number and all of its boundaries stated in the body of a reply and no formal document attached at
all. Those email-only bids are real bids and they are frequently where the scope actually gets
defined, because that is where the general contractor asks the questions the proposal did not
answer.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Tiling scope sheets have run 33, 46, 51 and 56 rows. Target roughly 45 to 70 scope lines for a full
multifamily tiling package. The thinnest sheet seen is also the one with the widest bid
spread and the most plugging, and the sheet with the tightest spread is the one that transcribed the
tile coursing rules off the drawings into scope rows. Grain buys comparability in this trade more
directly than in most.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every finish mark on the schedule, without exception, including the ones that are trim tiles rather
  than field tiles. Marks are how this trade reads a set and how every bidder writes its proposal.
  Enumerate them even where the drawings group them, because a bidder will accept some and question
  others individually.
- The wet area assembly, broken by element rather than by mark: shower wall tile, shower floor tile,
  shower pan, shower curb, shower niche, shower ceiling, tub surround wall tile, tub face tile, bath
  floor tile, bath wall base tile, kitchen backsplash. Every one of these is carried by some bidders
  and excluded by others, and several of them are excluded on the stated ground that they were not
  specified at all.
- Waterproofing membrane, split floor from wall, as its own lines. Not an attribute of the tile
  line. Bidders carry it in the base bid, as an alternate, at floors only, at walls only, or not at
  all, and the value of it can approach a tenth of the whole tile package.
- Crack isolation and uncoupling membrane, separately from waterproofing, because several bidders
  price them as different products and the leveling sheet asks about them on different rows.
- Acoustic underlayment mat, with the specified thickness and product on the line. It is the single
  most commonly plugged item in this trade.
- Cement backer board and tile underlayment, its own line. Most bidders exclude it, one prices it as
  an alternate, and one carries it as a large base bid line keyed to a wall type detail. Comparing a
  bidder that carries it against one that excludes it is comparing two different assemblies.
- Pitched mud bed and site-built shower pan, separately from a prefabricated pan. These are
  alternative assemblies at very different prices and a project may use one at standard units and
  the other at accessible units.
- Shower drain, its own line, because tile setters exclude it and point at plumbing.
- Thresholds and saddles, split by location: unit entry, bathroom entry, roll-in shower. Split again
  by material where the schedule names stone versus quartz, because availability differs and one
  bidder disclosed that only one colour is stocked.
- Metal trims and transitions, keyed to the architectural detail that shows them where such a sheet
  exists. One bidder prices a separate line for every transition detail number crossed with the two
  finishes it joins, which is the finest grain seen and comes straight off the transition
  detail sheet. Trim is also where bidders contradict themselves inside a single document, including
  it at named locations and excluding it at unit wall tile four bullets later.
- Grout, by type and by area, because a specification will call standard latex modified grout
  generally and an epoxy or water cleanable grout at lobby and public floors, and a bidder will
  exclude the epoxy while carrying the standard.
- Setting materials as their own block: thinset, medium set at large format tile, mortar split
  between floor and wall where a bidder prices them differently, mud, caulk, sealers.
- Movement joints and control joints, its own line. Several bidders exclude them explicitly, one
  carries them per industry standard, and most are silent. Silence here is a gap and not an
  inclusion.
- Floor preparation, split between the token minor prep every bidder includes and the real prep
  beyond it, with the hourly rate or the per unit allowance stated.
- Floor protection over finished tile, split between the tile areas and the rest of the floor, and
  split again where fire rated protection is required because it costs an order of magnitude more
  than ordinary board.
- Gypcrete sealer, with who furnishes and who installs stated on the line.
- Tile extent rules, as rows in their own right: how many courses above the shower floor, how many
  above the tub deck, whether the coursing is centred on the back wall, whether tile runs to the
  underside of the ceiling, whether the tub face is tiled, whether the backsplash is full height,
  whether tile runs behind vanities and under cabinets, and how tile terminates where it stops.
  These read like drawing notes rather than scope, and they are the rows that most reduce bid
  spread, because they are the questions every bidder otherwise answers silently and differently.
- Attic stock and extra materials, with the percentage.
- Elevator cab floors, with the furnish and install split stated.
- Hoisting, material distribution and the floor crates are delivered to.
- Existing conditions work on renovation projects: reinstallation of historic marble, refurbishment
  and repair of existing marble, replacement of historic items. On a renovation this block runs
  several rows and the tile bidders decline all of it.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual rooms collapse to room type or to a named common area. No document seen prices a single
  residential room, and common areas are named individually only in the small feature spaces such as
  the mail room, the pet spa, the elevator lobby and the amenity kitchen.
- Levels collapse to a level range unless the finish changes. Bidders routinely price elevator
  lobbies from the second level up as a single line while pricing the ground floor separately, which
  correctly tracks where the finish changes.
- Manufacturer, colour, size, thickness and finish are attributes of a mark, not lines of their own.
  The mark is the enumeration unit.
- Individual pieces of trim collapse into a trim line per profile and finish. Even the bidder that
  enumerates transitions by detail number does not count pieces.
- Freight collapses into a freight line per supplier at most. Bidders that break it out use one line
  per supplier and no finer.
- Mobilisation collapses into a mobilisation line per work family if it is carried at all, and only
  one bidder seen carries it.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
tiling, that test fires hardest on the shower assembly and on the substrate, which is why sections 3
and 5 give those more lines than the drawings appear to justify.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. If a scope item is one that some bidders carry and
others exclude, and a given proposal says nothing about it, that silence is a scope gap to surface,
not an inclusion to assume. Waterproofing, backer board, acoustic underlayment, movement joints and
floor protection are all items where a majority of proposals say nothing at all.

### The structural gap list

An estimator does not compare base bids in this trade. It compares base bid plus adjustments, where
the adjustments cover what the market systematically will not carry. The plugs to expect, in rough
order of how much money they move:

1. Waterproofing membrane behind and under tile at wet areas. The largest plug in the trade, and on
   one project the difference between the bidder that carried it in base bid and the bidder that
   added it was more than a third on that line alone.
2. Acoustic underlayment mat. Plugged on more than one project, with bidder answers on the same row
   ranging by more than a factor of two.
3. Floor preparation beyond the minor amount everyone includes.
4. Floor protection over finished tile.
5. Cement backer board or tile underlayment.
6. Shower pans, curbs and niches wherever the drawings do not settle who furnishes them.
7. Thresholds and saddles, particularly at the location the bidders split on.
8. Metal trims at the locations the bidder excluded.
9. Grout upgrade at public floors where an epoxy grout is specified and a standard grout is bid.
10. Hoisting and material distribution to the working floor.
11. Gypcrete sealer.
12. Insurance basis where an owner controlled programme may or may not apply, warranty term, and
    bond.

A scope line set that names all twelve explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging for it.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate. Repeat bidders reproduce their exclusion list verbatim across
projects a year apart, and several of the bidders in any tiling bid list appear on more than one
project. Read the boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- An exclusion whose stated reason is that the scope is not specified, rather than that it belongs to
  somebody else. One bidder excluded shower niches, stone curbs and jambs, and unit bathroom
  waterproofing on exactly that ground. Those are documents gaps the bidder found while reading, not
  scope it is pushing away, and each one is worth an RFI.
- A scope item excluded pending clarification, with the bidder declining to price it at all. One
  bidder refused to price unit shower floor tile on that basis while pricing everything around it.
- A named mark excluded or questioned individually. When a bidder writes out a specific mark and
  says the schedule defines it as one thing while a detail draws it as another, it read the finish
  schedule against the elevations and found a real conflict.
- A note that the drawings contradict themselves. Bidders in this trade find and report these
  routinely: a detail calling a bullnose piece where the schedule defines field tile, two adjacent
  marks that do not match each other in style, colour or size although the drawings use them
  together, elevations showing horizontal coursing where the typicals and the schedule call vertical
  stack, an enlarged plan showing an offset drain where the quoted pan has a centre drain. Every one
  of those was disclosed voluntarily by a bidder and none of them was on the general contractor's
  scope sheet.
- A material availability disclosure. One bidder noted that the specified threshold colour is not
  stocked and it carries a different one; the estimator recorded the same finding independently.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- The substrate must be delivered at a ready grade finish, flat and in tolerance, free of debris, and
  with contaminants such as old adhesive, patching compound, curing compound, paint and joint
  compound mechanically removed by others. Stated by most bidders and it is the trade's single most
  consistent qualification. A material supplier's warranty may be void outright where the subfloor is
  uneven, which puts substrate tolerance on the warranty's critical path and not just on the
  leveling sheet.
- Minor floor preparation is included and anything beyond it is billed hourly plus materials. Nearly
  universal. The stated hourly rate has moved upward year over year in the same bidder's proposals.
- Work is priced for regular weekday business hours, with the hours stated.
- The general contractor must provide temperature and humidity control suitable for installation and
  curing, and the mechanical system must be operational before installation. Stated by some bidders,
  and it is a real schedule constraint hidden inside a materials note.
- The owner or general contractor must furnish a written moisture and alkalinity report on the
  concrete substrate before installation, per an industry standard the bidder names. Stated by some
  bidders, and it is a deliverable somebody has to produce.
- Material pricing is held for a stated period, typically thirty days, and sometimes explicitly does
  not cover escalation.
- Pricing is subject to current tariff and duty rates, with the bidder reserving the right to adjust
  if they change. Stated by several bidders and by suppliers, and it is a live condition in this
  trade in a way it is not in most.
- Substitutions require written approval.
- Title to the material remains with the bidder until the contract price is paid, with a stated right
  to remove installed work. One bidder, and it is worth reading before it is agreed to.
- A supplier's volume discount applies to the quantities quoted, so reducing the order changes the
  itemised prices.
- Delivery conditions. Crates must be taken to a specific floor rather than the ground floor, a
  forklift with a stated minimum fork length is required to unload, breakage from unloading with
  short forks is not the supplier's responsibility, damage must be noted on the bill of lading, and
  storage costs accrue if delivery cannot be accepted on the agreed date. These come from the supply
  side, they are conditions on the general contractor rather than on the tile setter, and none of
  them is deducible from any drawing.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The shower assembly: the seam that matters most

Nothing else in this trade moves as much money or is as badly served by the drawings. The unit
shower and tub surround is the largest single scope in a multifamily tiling package, and it arrives
in four commercially distinct shapes that a finish schedule showing a wall tile mark will not
distinguish between.

1. Site built and tiled. The tile setter waterproofs the walls and floor, builds a pitched mud bed,
   sets a tiled shower floor, and tiles the surround. The most common shape.
2. Tiled surround over a prefabricated pan. The tile setter carries a manufactured shower pan and
   tiles the walls above it. Common, and it is what a bidder means when it excludes mud set while
   still pricing the shower.
3. Tiled surround with a proprietary bonded waterproofing kit. The tile setter installs a
   manufacturer's integrated pan, drain and waterproofing fabric system rather than a fluid applied
   membrane over a site built bed. Notably more expensive than the fluid applied alternative, by
   roughly a factor of two on one project, and it is the item bidders volunteer for value
   engineering when they are asked for ideas.
4. Cast marble panels and pans, with the tile scope removed. A specialty manufacturer furnishes
   moulded panels, pans and often the shower doors, and somebody else installs them. Where this
   applies, the unit shower and tub tile scope disappears from the tiling package entirely and is
   replaced by a supply package plus a separate installation bid. The kitchen backsplash stays a tile
   scope.

Reading a set will not tell you which applies. On one project the general contractor bid all four
against each other, and had to make the tile bidders break out the value of the waterproofing, the
pan and the surround tile separately before any of them could be compared. That breakout happened in
an email thread, not in any proposal.

The fourth shape deserves its own paragraph because its seams are unlike anything else in the trade.
The cast marble manufacturer sells material only, excludes field verification and installation
outright, maintains a list of certified installers and warns in writing that a non certified
installer should read the installation instructions before bidding the work. The general contractor
that goes this route buys the material from the manufacturer and the labour from a tile setter, and
the manufacturer is the one who makes the introduction. Ownership of the shower pan then becomes
genuinely contested: the manufacturer describes the pan as a finished unit that only needs hooking
up to plumbing, and the tile setter installing the panels says pans are normally the plumber's and
excludes them. No document seen resolves it. The manufacturer also produces its own takeoff
from the general contractor's unit type matrix, offers it as a convenience, and disclaims
responsibility for its accuracy, so the enclosure count is a supplier's number that somebody has to
verify.

### The substrate seam

Who delivers the wall the tile goes on, and in what condition, is contested on every project and is
almost never on the drawings.

- Cement backer board and tile underlayment. Most bidders exclude it, which leaves the wall in the
  framing or drywall package. One bidder carries it as a large base bid line keyed to a wall type
  detail. One prices it as an add alternate. Where the tiling scope excludes it and the drywall
  scope does not name it, it is unassigned.
- Substrate acceptability. One bidder names several acceptable substrates including standard drywall,
  cement board and coated glass mat board, which tells you the framing package's board selection is
  a tiling decision as much as a drywall one.
- Floor flatness and levelling. Every bidder includes a token minor prep and excludes self levelling,
  grinding, ramping and large scale patching. On concrete slabs the self levelling and moisture
  mitigation lines are large and are consistently priced as alternates rather than base bid.
- The gypcrete seam. Gypcrete is somebody else's, its sealer is contested between the flooring
  package and unassigned, and where a bidder carries the sealer it may be labour only with the
  material by others, or it may be bounded to named levels.
- Acoustic underlayment. Specified in an acoustics section, sitting physically between the gypcrete
  and the tile, and bid by the tile setter more often than by anyone else. One bidder carrying it as
  an alternate carved out the area under shower floors specifically, which is a boundary worth
  copying because nobody else states it.

### Other seams

- Grout and caulk against painting. Tile setters carry caulk at their own tile joints, and at least
  one excludes caulking altogether in the same line as painting. Painters bound their caulking to
  painted to painted surfaces and exclude caulking where only one side is painted. The tile to
  painted wall joint therefore sits between the two packages and is a recurring gap.
- Thresholds against doors and frames. Thresholds and saddles at unit entries and bathroom entries
  are the tile setter's, they are counted per opening, and their accessibility slope is a dimensioned
  requirement on the schedule. Which of the two locations is included varies bidder to bidder.
- Plumbing. Shower drains are excluded by tile setters and pointed at plumbing. Removal and
  reinstallation of plumbing fixtures and electrical devices around the work is excluded. One bidder
  stated its only requirement from another trade was water from the plumber.
- Elevator cabs. Cab floor tile is frequently material only from the tile setter with the install by
  the elevator contractor, and it is small enough to be forgotten entirely.
- Countertops and solid surface. Excluded by tile setters as done by others, but the kitchen and
  vanity backsplash immediately above the counter is the tile setter's, so the joint between them
  needs a line.
- Interior brick and thin brick. Marked on the finish schedule with the tile, excluded by tile
  bidders as typically done by others, and offered back as an add by the same bidders. If no other
  package names it, it is a gap.
- Floor protection. Split between the tile setter, the general contractor and a separate protection
  proposal. One bidder priced protection as a standalone proposal after the fact, and it distinguished
  fire rated protection at unit floors from ordinary rigid board at tiled floors, at an order of
  magnitude difference. Where the tile setter installs protection it may exclude removing it.
- Stairs. Tread, riser, tactile warning strip and landing tile travel with the flooring package
  rather than the tile package on most projects but are quoted by the same bidder either way.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What tile setters actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The finish schedule. This is the primary document by a very wide margin. Marks come from here, and
   most proposals transcribe manufacturer, collection, colour, size, thickness and finish straight
   out of it. A bidder reading the schedule carefully against the elevations is where the trade's
   best drawing findings come from.
2. The unit type schedule and the unit count. Every leveling sheet normalises every bid to a cost per
   residential unit and ranks on it, so unit count is the leveling currency of this trade. Bidders
   that price by bathroom type or unit type work directly off this schedule. If the set does not make
   the unit type mix and count unambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable.
3. Enlarged bathroom and kitchen plans and elevations, for the per bathroom areas, the coursing, the
   niche and curb conditions, and the threshold counts.
4. The transition and threshold detail sheet where one exists. One bidder priced a separate line for
   every detail on it.
5. The wall type details, for the substrate and backer board question.
6. The specification, for the coat of setting materials, the grout type by area, the membrane
   requirements, the attic stock percentage and the acoustic mat product. Bidders cite the section
   number and occasionally quote a requirement, and the sharpest of them catch where the specified
   product does not match the specified property.
7. A takeoff furnished by the general contractor, where one is provided. At least one bidder priced
   entirely off a GC furnished takeoff and said so, which makes the GC's own takeoff a bid document.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Labour type, union or open shop. The largest single driver, and union bidders sit at the top of
  every ranking seen without exception.
- Prevailing wage applicability.
- Tariff and duty rates on imported tile, and material escalation generally. Live enough that
  multiple bidders and suppliers cap their price validity because of it.
- Which of the four shower assemblies applies.
- Whether the cast marble route is being taken, and if so who installs the panels and who owns the
  pan.
- Whether the tile is being carried at a real price or as a material allowance. At earlier set stages
  it frequently is an allowance, and the value engineering log converts named designer tile into a
  stated allowance per square foot.
- Whether waterproofing is expected in the base bid, and whether it applies at floors, at walls, or
  both.
- Whether the acoustic underlayment is the tile setter's.
- Whether the backer board arrives with the wall or comes with the tile.
- Who furnishes and who installs at the elevator cabs.
- The waste factor and attic stock percentage each bidder is carrying, and whether it is folded into
  the stated quantity.
- The floor crates and pallets are delivered to, and whether a forklift and operator are available at
  unloading. This is a real cost that lands on the general contractor and it is stated only by
  suppliers.
- Lead times from approved shop drawings, and which items are long lead. Bidders that state it quote
  a week for shop drawings and several weeks for material.
- The insurance basis, whether an owner controlled programme applies, and the cost of conventional
  general liability if it does not.
- Warranty term. One year is the stated default and where two years is required the difference is
  priced as a deduct to go back down to one, not as an add.
- Project portal and payment application fees, quoted as a small percentage of contract value.
- Whether a model unit is expected in advance and remains part of the work.
- Site access, hoisting and staging, and whether the contractor's hoist can take the material.

Set stage behaviour. At design development and permit set, bidders return lump sums, carry tile as a
material allowance, and price around missing information without always saying so. By construction
documents and conformed sets the same bidders switch to mark enumeration with stated quantities. A
proposal's stated basis and its folder's set stage will sometimes disagree, and a bid may price a
construction document round off an earlier permit set and say so in the fine print. Read the stated
plan date, not the folder.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet for wall and floor tile, by mark. The dominant unit everywhere.
- Linear feet for tile wall base, for trim and transition profiles, and for trim tiles that run as a
  band.
- Each for thresholds and saddles, for shower pans, drains and curbs, for transition strips at unit
  entries, and for bullnose and trim pieces where a bidder counts them.
- Each per bathroom type or per unit type, where a bidder prices the whole bathroom as one item.
  This is uncommon and it is the most comparable structure available.
- Each per panel, per pan and per door in a cast marble supply package, taken off against a unit type
  by floor matrix.
- Square feet for membranes, mats and floor protection, at rates well below the tile rates.
- Hours for labour where a bidder prices labour as a single line, and hours for floor preparation
  beyond the included minimum.
- Bags, buckets or units for grout, mortar and mud where a bidder itemises setting materials.
- Percent of contract value for payment portal fees and for bond.
- Residential units, as the normalising denominator for the whole package. Every leveling sheet.

Working quantities per unit, taken from a general contractor's own scope sheet where it wrote its
assumptions down as scope rows:

- Kitchen backsplash, roughly 20 to 24 square feet per unit.
- Bathroom floor and tile base, roughly 50 square feet per bath.
- Tub and shower surround, roughly 82.5 square feet per bath.
- Common area wall tile, 8 feet tall where the drawings do not show a height.

Those are the estimator's plugs, not measured quantities, and the bidders' own square footages came
in below the assumption on the backsplash and above it on the bathroom floor and the surround. They
are the right starting point when a set is too early to take off and the right thing to check a
bidder's number against when it is not.

Waste and attic stock. Waste runs from ten to fifteen percent where a bidder discloses it, and it is
usually folded into the quantity rather than shown separately, which makes that bidder's square
footage larger than a measured area by exactly that amount. Attic stock runs from three to five
percent and appears as a priced line where the specification demands it.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Package mode. Whether the number is furnish and install, furnish only, install only, or partial
   swamps every other variable and is the first thing to establish.
2. Package breadth. Whether the bid covers tile alone or the whole floorcovering package. The same
   company appears near the bottom of one ranking as a supply only number and near the top of another
   as a whole finishes number, so the company name is never the comparable unit.
3. Labour type, union versus open shop. Union bidders occupy the top of every ranking, and on the
   smallest project the union number was more than six times the selected open shop number.
4. Which shower assembly is bid, and whether waterproofing is inside or outside the base bid.
5. Tile material grade. This is what value engineering moves. The trade's standard move is a brand
   package substitution, swapping the specified designer tile for a volume manufacturer's equivalent
   mark for mark, or converting a named product to a stated material allowance per square foot. The
   labour is untouched and only the material line moves. Substitution packages seen took
   double digit percentages out of the affected segments. Watch for a substitution that quietly
   changes the tile format as well as the brand, because that changes the installed appearance and
   the labour.
6. Tile format and pattern. Small format mosaic, penny round and thin stacked tile carry
   substantially higher installed rates per square foot than large format field tile, by several times
   at the extremes. Field cutting a larger tile down to a smaller one and laying the two in a mix is
   a real labour scope that a finish schedule shows as one mark.
7. Total wet area, driven by unit count and by how many baths per unit.
8. Whether the acoustic underlayment, backer board and floor protection are in or out.
9. Tile extent decisions: to the ceiling or not, the tub face tiled or not, full height backsplash or
   not, behind the vanity or not. On one project the tile to ceiling alternate was worth roughly an
   eighth of the entire tile package, and the tub face alternate was trivial. On another project on
   the same bid cycle a different bidder priced the tub face above the tile to ceiling alternate.
   These alternates are not consistently valued across the market and each one needs its own number
   from each bidder.
10. Grout type by area, and whether an epoxy grout is required at public floors.
11. Floor preparation actually required versus the substrate delivered.
12. Freight, tariff exposure and material escalation.
13. Hoisting, distribution and the delivery floor.
14. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per residential unit ran at 2.1, 2.5, 3.3 and 6.4 times. Bidder counts per
project ran from six to fifteen. The wide spreads are almost entirely an artefact of package
breadth, package mode and labour type rather than of competitiveness, and once the list is restricted
to open shop furnish and install bids of comparable breadth the spread narrows to roughly two to one
on every project. The tightest spread belonged to the project whose scope sheet transcribed the tile
coursing rules off the drawings, and the widest to the project with the fewest scope rows.

Two more conventions worth knowing. Tile cost per unit does not fall with project size: the smallest
project seen by unit count carried the highest cost per unit by a wide margin, because the
fixed common area and amenity tile is spread over fewer units. And the low bidder is frequently not
the selected bidder. On one project the selected bidder ranked fifth of fourteen, and what
distinguished it was that it returned the completed scope sheet, a value engineering log with seven
priced substitutions, and a cover letter naming three drawing discrepancies it had found.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly thirty tiling and tile-adjacent proposals, two cast marble
shower surround supply quotes, one installation-labour-only bid against cast marble material, two
material-supply-only quotes from distributors, and the tiling tabs of four estimator leveling sheets,
drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general
contractor. The proposals span design development, permit, construction documents and conformed sets.
The bidder pool includes several companies that appear on more than one project, which is what makes
the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable, and two companies that
appear on the same project in different package modes, which is what makes the package-mode warning
in section 1 concrete rather than theoretical.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back of house space, in
  one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors,
  institutional, healthcare, hospitality, industrial or public prevailing-wage work, and the wet-area
  emphasis throughout would look very different on a project without residential bathrooms.
- Only one project involved renovation of an existing building, so the existing-conditions and
  historic marble block in section 3 rests on that project alone, and the tile bidders declined all
  of it, so the corpus does not show who actually performs that work.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation and which were bought out. In particular, the shower assembly question is
  visible only in the bidding, never in the award.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the substrate tolerance and business hours
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- Only two proposals disclosed a waste factor and only two disclosed attic stock, so the ten to
  fifteen percent and three to five percent ranges in section 7 rest on very few documents.
- The per-unit working quantities in section 7 come from one general contractor's scope sheet on one
  project, checked against one bidder's stated areas. They should be treated as a starting point to
  verify, not a norm.
- Movement joints and control joints are excluded or silent almost everywhere, so the corpus shows
  that the gap exists but shows nothing about who closes it or what it costs.
- The corpus has not shown exterior tile, paving, pool or wet-deck tile, or tile over structural
  slabs on grade with the associated waterproofing and drainage complications.
- The corpus has not shown large format panel or slab installation, or gauged porcelain, which
  carries different handling, substrate and labour requirements from the field tile seen here.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the tiling package was procured entirely as owner
  furnished material with a separate install contract, though the cast marble carve-out is a partial
  view of what that arrangement looks like.
- Terrazzo appears nowhere, so the boundary between tiling and terrazzo is asserted from the
  exclusion lists rather than observed.
