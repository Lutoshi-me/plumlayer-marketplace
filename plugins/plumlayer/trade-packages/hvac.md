# HVAC

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the HVAC package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The
entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real mechanical contractors can bid it and a real
estimator can level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Division 23 is the anchor, and section 230000 is the number a proposal cites when it cites one,
which it usually does. Unlike trades where the section number is a weak identifier, here the
division number is strong: bidders write "the HVAC work (Division 23)" as their opening line, and
estimator sheets key the whole package to it.

The package as bid is heating, ventilation, air conditioning, ductwork, and the piping that serves
them. In multifamily work that means the in-unit heating and cooling equipment, the ventilation
system, all sheet metal and air distribution, refrigerant and condensate piping, and everything
mounted on the roof to serve those systems. It is one of the two or three largest packages on a
residential project and routinely runs several times the size of the interior finish trades.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another discipline:

- Generator fuel oil systems. The tank, the duplex pump skid, the containment piping, the fill and
  vent, the leak sensors, and often the first fuel fill. This is piping work, not air work, and it
  travels with HVAC by convention rather than by logic. It is also the single most volatile line in
  the package: on one project four bidders gave four mutually incompatible answers spanning a factor
  of three, and one excluded it outright on the grounds that its company does not do that work.
- The generator flue or exhaust. Usually a factory-fabricated double-wall stainless assembly, almost
  always carried by this trade.
- Garage carbon monoxide detection and the exhaust it controls, along with garage jet fans. Some
  bidders carry it, some exclude it by name, and it is common enough on estimator sheets to be worth
  its own rows whether or not anyone prices it.
- Dryer exhaust ductwork, dryer wall boxes, and exterior dryer caps. Almost always the duct side
  belongs here even though the dryer itself and its vent kit ride with the appliance package. Watch
  the boundary carefully, because the two trades describe the same physical run in different words.
- Kitchen hood exhaust ducting to the exterior. The hood itself commonly sits inside an appliance
  allowance, and the duct from it to the wall commonly sits here. One bidder states this explicitly,
  another excludes all exhaust hoods, so this is a real and recurring gap.
- Heat pump water heater exhaust ducting. Where the domestic hot water system uses in-unit heat pump
  water heaters, those units discharge cool air and need ducting or a louvered enclosure, and the
  duct side lands here while the appliance is plumbing's. This is invisible unless you know the
  domestic hot water system type.
- Elevator machine room and communications room dedicated cooling, and elevator hoistway venting.
  Both carried commonly, both worth their own lines because the hoistway vent needs an electrical
  interlock to the fire alarm.
- Stair pressurization fans, drives, and pre-test and post-test verification.
- Through-penetration firestopping for the trade's own penetrations. Carried by about half of
  bidders and excluded by the rest, and it is one of the most common deduct alternates offered.
- Roof curbs, roof rails, equipment stands, and pipe supports. Sometimes furnished and installed
  here, sometimes furnished only, sometimes excluded outright, which is why section 5 gives the roof
  its own treatment.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like HVAC:

- Building automation and direct digital control. This is the largest and most consequential carve
  out in the trade. Almost every HVAC proposal carries stand-alone controls only, meaning local
  thermostats, a manufacturer's own system controller, and low voltage wiring to devices, and
  explicitly excludes the building automation system. When a controls vendor bids the automation
  package direct to the general contractor it can be worth around fifteen percent of the entire HVAC
  base bid, against under two percent for the stand-alone controls the mechanical bidder carries. A
  scope list that does not separate these two things will be off by an order of magnitude on the
  same row. Some specifications assign the automation work to its own division, and the mechanical
  proposal will then say so and coordinate rather than price it.
- Testing, adjusting, and balancing when it must be certified and third party. Bidders routinely
  carry their own in-house or non-certified balancing in the base and price certified third-party
  balancing as an add alternate worth low single-digit percentages of the base. Reading a base bid
  as though balancing is settled is a mistake in this trade.
- Commissioning by a third-party agent. Excluded by nearly every bidder seen.
- Mechanical screens and rooftop equipment screens. These leave the package entirely and are bid by
  screen or interior specialty companies against a specification section in the 10 series, or filed
  under miscellaneous metals. They are priced by linear foot of screen with a configured frame
  count, and they exclude roof flashing and support blocking exactly as the mechanical bidder does,
  so the same roof detail is disclaimed from both sides.
- Demolition and make safe on renovation work. Frequently a separate contract or a separately priced
  breakout, sometimes bid by a different company than the new work. See section 6.
- Sheet metal work shown only on architectural drawings. One bidder excludes it by that description
  alone, which is a documents-scope exclusion rather than a physical one, and it lands hardest on
  exactly the items that tend to be drawn on architectural sheets: dryer vents, wall caps, and hood
  ducts.

## 2. How the market bids

HVAC is a lump sum trade that enumerates heavily. The default output is one number with a long
supporting list of what that number covers, and the list is usually keyed to equipment marks.

The shapes seen, roughly in order of frequency:

- A single lump sum with an equipment tag table above it. The tags come straight off the drawing
  schedules, each with a count, and every row is priced at zero. Counts are given, money is not.
  This is the most useful proposal shape for a leveller because the counts are directly checkable
  against the schedules.
- A single lump sum with a printed include and exclude grid. Two-state (yes and no), three-state, or
  four-state. Four-state grids distinguish furnish and install, furnish only, install only, and
  exclude. Another common four-state grid uses furnished and included, excluded, not applicable to
  this project, and questionable at this time needing further clarification. That last state is the
  most valuable answer in the trade and no other trade in the corpus offers it: a bidder telling you
  where it does not yet know is telling you where the documents are ambiguous.
- A long prose scope narrative, numbered clause by clause, with manufacturer, mark and count for
  every piece of equipment and linear footage for the piping. Still one lump price.
- A general contractor's own scope sheet answered row by row. On the largest projects this runs to
  well over a hundred and fifty rows across a dozen technical sections plus general conditions,
  compliance, temporary services, and testing blocks.
- A price in an email body with three or four inclusion words. Uncommon but not rare, and these are
  real bids that get logged, ranked and compared.
- Marketing prose with a phase-by-phase execution plan and no enumeration at all. Rare, and it is
  the shape a scope list has to defend against, because nothing in it can be levelled row by row.

Segmentation is almost always commercial rather than physical. A proposal will separate a base bid,
alternates, and value engineering deducts, but it very rarely splits the base by building, area, or
scope family. On one project the general contractor asked on its bid form for a three-way breakout
between the residential building, the amenity space, and everything else, and not one bidder
supplied it. Assume that if you want the number split by area, you will have to ask for it and you
may not get it.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The equipment schedule marks it is carrying, with counts. Nearly always present in some form, and
  this is the trade's defining habit. The tag families are architect-specific and engineer-specific
  vocabulary discovered from the schedules, never a fixed prefix set. The same building's outdoor
  units may be tagged one way on one project and quite differently on the next.
- The drawing set date and specification date it priced from. Nearly always, and often with a site
  visit field marked as none.
- Labor market. Stated as standard wage or non-prevailing-wage by some bidders, and recorded for
  every bidder by the estimator regardless. See section 7, because this is the largest single
  variable in the trade.
- Sales tax treatment. Nearly always stated as included, which is unusual across trades and worth
  noting when a bidder is silent.
- Named manufacturers, per element. Very common, and by far the most common basis for a value
  engineering offer.
- Permit and inspection responsibility, submittals, operations and maintenance manuals, owner
  training and as-built drawings. Carried by nearly everyone as a block of unpriced rows.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Any decomposition of the number. Real unit pricing appears occasionally on discrete equipment, and
  almost never on the distribution system, which is where roughly forty percent of the cost lives.
- Duct quantities. Sheet metal is described (galvanized, rectangular and round, sealed, insulated,
  hung per the sheet metal industry standard) and essentially never measured.
- Labor hours, except in demolition and refrigerant reclaim work, where an hour cap is the norm.
- Bond cost, unless asked. Quoted as a percentage of contract value when it is given, and the
  percentages seen range by more than a factor of two between bidders on the same project, which is
  large enough to move a ranking on its own.
- Insurance basis. Whether an owner-controlled program applies, and what conventional general
  liability would cost if it does not, comes out only in answer to a direct question.
- Escalation and tariff exposure. Some bidders disclaim tariffs explicitly and reserve the right to
  adjust; most are silent, which does not mean they have absorbed it.
- Warranty duration. The market default is one year on labor. Where a specification requires two,
  the adder is worth around two percent of the base, and it will not appear until someone asks.

Exclusion behavior splits the market and the split matters. Most proposals carry an exclusion list.
The shortest is a single printed sentence of six or seven items that the same bidder reproduces
verbatim on every project across several years, which makes it boilerplate and carries no project
information. The longest run to fifty or more rows and genuinely map the trade boundary. A bidder
with a one-line exclusion sentence is not offering broader scope, it is leaving every boundary open.
On one project the highest bidder in the field also had one of the shortest proposals, with an
exclusion paragraph longer than its inclusion, so the most expensive number also needed the most
plugs.

Revision habits, and this trade revises more than most:

- Most revisions change price only, with the scope text either byte-identical or explicitly
  unchanged. A grind of several rounds moving the number down by five to fifteen percent in total is
  normal, and a reason is almost never documented. One estimator recorded that a drop was attributed
  by the bidder to aggressive repricing rather than to any scope change.
- A revision may move nothing at all. Two documents filed as consecutive revisions can be the same
  file with the same byte count, one still carrying the other's date on its face. Another pair
  differed only in page layout. Check the document's own date and revision markings, never the
  filename.
- A revision may exist only to reconfirm an unchanged number against a newer drawing set, sometimes
  months and a whole set stage later, with no stated review of what changed in between.
- Watch for the price drop that is not a price drop. A base bid can fall by very close to the value
  of a scope item the bidder itself named in a prior round's comment column, because the scope
  silently left the base. Detecting it requires reading the previous round, not just the delta.
- Watch equally for the price rise that is not a price rise. When a general contractor issues a
  comeback questionnaire, a bidder's base can climb by several percent purely by absorbing items
  that were outside it before: certified balancing, a longer warranty, firestopping, and a damper
  scope. The number went up and the bidder did not get more expensive.
- The largest unexplained movement seen was a near doubling between two rounds with byte-identical
  scope text and no stated reason. Do not assume revisions move downward.
- Genuine document-change revisions are the rarest kind. When a bulletin or addendum does drive a
  change, it is often priced as an entirely separate quote appended to the unchanged original rather
  than as a reissued number, so a reader who takes the last page of a file as the proposal can get
  the delta and miss the base.
- Alternate manufacturer lists narrow across rounds. A bidder that opens by offering three brands or
  equal will typically be down to one or two by the final round, as vendors confirm what can
  actually be furnished and to spec. That narrowing is information, not noise.

## 3. Scope grain rules

HVAC scope sheets have run about twenty-seven, forty-one, fifty-seven and eighty rows across
projects, and the general contractor's own issued scope checklist on one project ran to about a
hundred and sixty-five. Target sixty to a hundred scope lines for a full multifamily HVAC package,
and expect the sheet to grow between rounds: on one project the current sheet carried about thirty
more rows than its superseded predecessor, and every added row corresponded to a question an earlier
round of answers had exposed. The scope list is a learning artifact, and its growth is the record of
what the last round did not resolve.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every equipment schedule mark family, with its count. Outdoor heat pumps and condensing units,
  in-unit air handlers and fan coils, ceiling cassettes, branch selector boxes, dedicated outside air
  units, energy recovery ventilators, exhaust and supply fans, stair pressurization fans, garage jet
  fans, electric unit heaters, cabinet heaters, wall heaters, baseboard, plenum heaters, in-floor
  convectors, sound attenuators, dehumidifiers, and condensate pumps. This is the primary organizing
  axis of the trade and nearly every proposal adopts it. Read the schedules, not a generic list, and
  discover the tag vocabulary this engineer used.
- Air distribution devices, counted. Registers, grilles and diffusers routinely run into the
  thousands on a large residential building and are counted as one family by most bidders.
- Dampers, split by kind, because they are answered differently and priced very differently.
  Radiation dampers, fire dampers, fire smoke dampers, motorized dampers, volume dampers, backdraft
  dampers, and corridor-to-apartment balancing dampers each deserve a row. Motorized dampers in
  particular are the single most contested small item in the trade: bidders carry them at intake
  only, at intake and exhaust, or not at all, and one asked the general contractor to raise a formal
  question about whether they are required before deciding.
- Access panels, by type and location. This is not an accessory, it is a leveling row. Counts run
  into the low thousands on a large building. Enumerate apartment panels, panels at fire and smoke
  dampers, panels at exhaust fans, panels at motorized dampers, panels at ceiling equipment, and
  amenity panels separately, and record whether each is fire rated. Note the scope chain the drawings
  will not show you: keeping the in-unit motorized dampers forces an access panel at each one, so a
  damper decision silently drives a panel count in the thousands.
- Ductwork by material and service, not by quantity. Galvanized rectangular, galvanized round,
  flexible, ductboard, rated duct assemblies at vaults or special exhausts, stainless generator
  exhaust, and duct lining. Rated duct systems in particular are worth their own row because they
  can be worth a couple of hundred thousand on their own and are a standing value engineering target.
- Duct insulation, banded by location and rating, because indoor, rooftop and exterior duct carry
  different specifications and different wrap ratings, and lining is a separate decision from wrap.
- Refrigerant piping and line sets, with the associated accessories: pipe portals, roof pipe
  supports, penetration enclosures, ultraviolet-protective guards on exposed runs, and nail shields
  where framing fasteners could reach a concealed line.
- Condensate piping, condensate pumps, secondary drain pans and safety switches. Separately from
  refrigerant, and separately from the riser, for the reasons in section 5.
- Fuel oil system as a block, not a line. Tank, containment, pumps and controls, fill and vent,
  leak detection, piping to the equipment, welding, fire watch, coring, and the first fill. Six or
  more rows. It attracts the widest dispersion of any subsystem.
- Ventilation architecture as its own decision row, not as an equipment count. Whether the building
  ventilates through a central rooftop dedicated outside air system with common risers and corridor
  distribution, or through individual energy recovery ventilators in each apartment with their own
  wall penetrations, is the largest single lever on the number. See section 6.
- Roof-mounted equipment support, split by who does what. Curbs, rails, stands, sleepers, blocking,
  vibration isolation, flashing, and counterflashing are five to seven separate rows because
  different bidders draw the line in different places within the same detail.
- Exterior wall terminations: intake and exhaust wall caps, dryer caps, louvers, and their installation.
- Controls, split into at least three rows: thermostats and local controls, the manufacturer's own
  system controller and any protocol gateway, and the building automation system. The third is worth
  many times the first two combined and is usually not this bidder's.
- Testing and balancing, split into in-house balancing, non-certified third-party balancing, and
  certified third-party balancing. Three states, three prices, and bidders sit in all three.
- Startup, commissioning, and owner training as separate rows from balancing.
- Firestopping of the trade's own penetrations, its own row, because it is carried by roughly half
  the market and is a standing deduct alternate for the rest.
- Coring, sleeving and slab scanning, with a diameter threshold, because bidders bound their coring
  inclusion by hole size and exclude floor scanning separately.
- Rigging and hoisting, counted by mobilization. Bidders price a discrete number of crane
  mobilizations, so a phasing change that adds one is a real cost.
- Coordination drawings and three-dimensional modeling as its own row. It is a standing deduct
  alternate on every project and is worth a meaningful sum on large jobs.
- Permits, inspections, submittals, operations and maintenance manuals, as-builts, attic stock and
  spare parts, filters and the pre-turnover filter change, labeling and tagging.
- Commercial rows, because in this trade they change what the number is worth: bond rate, insurance
  basis, payment portal fees, retainage schedule, escalation validity window, tariff exposure,
  workforce and diversity participation commitments, and experience modification rate.
- Demolition and make safe as its own block on renovation work, with a refrigerant reclaim hour
  allowance stated.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual fan coils, air handlers or heat pumps within one tag family. Collapse to the tag with a
  count. No proposal seen prices a single unit.
- Room-by-room and floor-by-floor duct routing. Collapse to the space type and the system.
- Fittings, transitions, boots, hangers, supports, sleeves and escutcheons. These are attributes of
  the ductwork and piping rows and no document prices them apart.
- Individual grilles and diffusers by mark. Collapse to the family count unless a specific mark is
  the subject of a value engineering item.
- Pipe valves and fittings. One row, everywhere.
- Gauge, seam type and sealer for duct. Attributes, not lines.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied here,
the test fires hardest on the roof, on the electrical boundary, on controls, on balancing, and on
every damper and access panel decision, which is why those get rows even when the drawings show them
as a single symbol repeated on a plan.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. If a bidder is silent on curb installation, on
flashing, on motorized dampers, or on whether balancing is certified, treat that as a gap to surface
rather than an inclusion to assume, because the market genuinely splits on all four.

### The trade's structural gap list

These are the plugs an estimator adds to every number before comparing, because the market
systematically will not carry them. A scope line set that names all of them is doing the plugging in
advance.

- Certified third-party testing and balancing, where the base carries in-house or non-certified.
- Building automation and direct digital control.
- Third-party commissioning.
- Firestopping, where a bidder excludes it or prices it as a deduct.
- Coordination drawings and three-dimensional modeling.
- Bond, and general liability insurance where no owner-controlled program applies.
- Warranty extension beyond one year where the specification requires more.
- Architectural access panels, both the count and who installs them.
- Roof curb installation, flashing and counterflashing.
- Motorized dampers wherever a bidder has carried them partially or not at all.
- Equipment pads, housekeeping pads, sleepers, dunnage and supplemental steel.
- Loose disconnects, starters and variable frequency drives, and all line voltage wiring.
- Seismic restraint and vibration isolation.
- Temporary heat, temporary conditioning and temporary filters.
- Demolition and make safe, and rigging for removal of existing equipment.
- Fuel oil system where the bidder does not perform that work.
- Garage carbon monoxide detection.
- Dryer exhaust beyond whatever portion of the units a bidder actually carried.
- Duct cleaning and duct leakage testing.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Much of the table above is boilerplate, and one recurring bidder reproduces the same seven-item
exclusion sentence on every project across three years and four jobs. Repeat bidders are a large
share of any HVAC bid list and they reuse their lists verbatim. Read boilerplate for the trade map,
not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that
bidder's other proposals:

- A named equipment mark that is excluded, or carried with a stated reservation. When a bidder writes
  out specific marks and says its price is a budget carry because the schedule shows no manufacturer
  or model for the basis of design, it has read the schedule and found a hole in it.
- An exclusion or inclusion justified by reference to a specific detail. A bidder qualifying that
  refrigerant roof penetrations are assumed to go through a parapet wall with a named weatherproof
  enclosure per a cited detail is telling you it studied the detail and what it will do if the
  detail is wrong.
- A stated disagreement with the specification. One bidder quoted copper condensate piping and told
  the general contractor that the specification's own material is not permitted at that building
  height. That is an issue for a formal question, and it is hiding inside a quote.
- A stated disagreement with the drawings. A bidder writing that the drawings show motorized dampers
  at both intake and exhaust and that its base bid carries none is documenting a deliberate
  departure, which is far more useful than a silent one.
- A bidder-driven request that the general contractor raise a formal question in order to shed
  scope. Treat this as a price movement in progress, not as a clarification.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: an adjacent tenancy requiring off-hours
  work, a rated duct system serving a vault, a post office or retail space, an existing structure.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Flexible duct length limits. Commonly stated as a maximum run inside apartments with hard round
  pipe making up the difference beyond it, and a shorter limit in common areas. A ceiling layout
  that pushes past the limit is a cost.
- Working hours. Stated as a specific daily window by some bidders, with everything outside it
  chargeable.
- Escalation validity. Escalation is commonly included only if the bidder is authorized to buy
  within a stated number of days from the quote, and the price may change if release is delayed.
  This is the most commonly violated qualification in the trade because buyout routinely takes
  longer than the window.
- Tariff exposure. Some bidders disclaim any provision or contingency for future tariffs and reserve
  the right to adjust with documentation. Others are silent, which does not mean they absorbed it.
- Storage. Equipment storage included for a stated number of months after submittal approval, with a
  monthly charge beyond it. Long lead mechanical equipment plus a slipped schedule makes this real.
- Retainage. Some bidders qualify a maximum retainage percentage, or a step-down schedule that
  reduces to zero past halfway and ends at a reduced final retainage.
- Lien waivers. At least one bidder will execute only partial and final lien waivers and refuses
  anything broader.
- Delay. A bidder may reserve reimbursement for delay costs and lost revenue if the project is
  delayed beyond a stated period once commenced through no fault of its own.
- The right to renegotiate if specifications, materials or equipment change after award.
- Quote validity, commonly thirty days, occasionally as short as fifteen.
- Labor market, stated as standard wage. This is the single largest price driver in the trade and is
  treated as a first-class comparison field by the estimator even when the proposal omits it.
- Basis of design sourcing. Where a specification names a manufacturer whose submittals must come
  through one named manufacturer's representative, a bidder sourcing the same brand through a
  different regional representative may not be able to submit it. Which representative holds the
  territory is a purely commercial fact, invisible on any drawing, and it can disqualify an
  otherwise compliant bid.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This trade has the largest seam surface of any package on a residential project. It touches
electrical, plumbing, roofing, drywall, framing, structure, appliances, fire alarm and fire
protection, and it does so at dozens of physical points. The seams below are where the money and the
arguments actually are.

### The roof, which is contested from both sides

The roof detail is the sharpest seam in the trade, and no two bidders draw it the same way. Within
one project on one detail, the positions seen were:

1. Curbs furnished and installed by the mechanical contractor, set-in-place flashing by others, and
   counterflashing and waterproof sealing back to the mechanical contractor.
2. Curbs furnished and located only by the mechanical contractor, installed by the roofing
   contractor.
3. Curb installation and the setting of roof flashing both excluded outright.
4. Equipment stands fastened to pressure treated sleepers, where the mechanical contractor spots the
   sleepers, the roofer flashes them into the roof, and the mechanical contractor then fastens the
   stand to the sleepers. Three parties, one detail, one sentence.

Add to that: roof rails and support systems, wind baffles, minimum elevation of equipment above the
finished roof, waterproof sealing at duct-to-unit connections and at penetrations, roof duct
supports and pipe supports, penetration enclosures for refrigerant lines, and the protective
covering on exposed refrigerant runs. Every one of these has been carried by someone and excluded by
someone else. Give the roof its own block of rows and force an answer on each.

The mechanical screen surrounding all of it is not in this package at all, and its own proposal
excludes roof flashing and support blocking in exactly the same words. So the flashing under the
screen supports and the blocking beneath them are disclaimed by the screen vendor, the roofer and
the mechanical contractor simultaneously.

### Electrical, the longest boundary

Nearly every mechanical proposal excludes all line voltage. The boundary is finer than that
sentence suggests and needs several rows:

- Power wiring and final connections to equipment. Electrical, without exception.
- Loose disconnects. Usually furnished and installed by electrical. Some mechanical bidders furnish
  disconnects for outdoor units only. Factory-installed integral disconnects arrive with the
  equipment and are the mechanical contractor's by default, which is why one general contractor asked
  its bidder to confirm which equipment comes with integral disconnects and to break out the cost of
  any loose ones it had carried.
- Motor starters. Commonly furnished only by the mechanical contractor and installed and wired by
  electrical, or excluded entirely.
- Variable frequency drives. The clearest furnish-only item in the trade. Commonly furnished by the
  mechanical contractor and installed by electrical, though some bidders carry drives for specific
  fans complete.
- Control wiring. Low voltage to devices is the mechanical contractor's; all conduit for it is
  electrical's; line voltage to control panels and control components is electrical's.
- The communication cable between an outdoor condenser and its indoor air handler. Called out
  explicitly by one recurring bidder as electrical's, which means it is contested often enough to be
  worth naming.
- Fire alarm interlocks: duct smoke detectors, smoke detector installation, elevator hoistway vent
  connection to the fire alarm, and controls integration with fire alarm and smoke detection.
  Smoke detectors are commonly furnished by others and sometimes installed by the mechanical
  contractor, which is one of the few genuine install-only items in the trade.
- Refrigerant leak detection interlocks where the refrigerant class requires them.
- Note that a change of equipment manufacturer can change the electrical scope, not just the
  mechanical price. One bidder offered a brand substitution and flagged potential savings on the
  electrical package as well from the change. A brand decision made inside the HVAC package can move
  another trade's number.

### Plumbing, and the condensate seam

Condensate is the standing seam with plumbing and it is answered the same way by nearly everyone:
the mechanical contractor runs condensate to an open drain and the riser is the plumber's. What
varies underneath that sentence:

- Whether condensate pumps are carried, and one bidder qualifies that it will carry them only if the
  plumber provides no drain, which makes its own inclusion conditional on another trade's design.
- Secondary drain pans and overflow safety switches, commonly one per in-unit air handler and
  commonly carried here.
- The final tie-in point itself, which one bidder states is provided by the plumbing contractor,
  meaning the last connection belongs to neither party until someone says so.
- Indirect plumbing drains, water heaters, water boosters and hydronic water systems are excluded by
  the mechanical contractor as a matter of course.

Watch for the same company bidding both packages. It happens frequently, and when it does the bidder
may offer a combined deduction for awarding both together, which means the two packages cannot be
levelled independently of each other.

### Appliances and the in-unit exhaust cluster

Three in-unit exhaust runs sit exactly on the appliance boundary, and the appliance package records
the other half of each:

- Kitchen hoods. The hood is commonly inside an appliance allowance. The duct from the hood to the
  exterior wall is commonly the mechanical contractor's, and at least one bidder says so explicitly
  while another excludes all exhaust hoods. Confirm both halves exist somewhere.
- Dryer venting. The dryer vent kit typically rides with the appliance as an accessory. The duct
  run, the dryer wall box and the exterior cap are the mechanical contractor's, and are counted
  roughly one per dwelling unit. Booster fans are priced separately per unit when required by run
  length. One bidder was recorded as carrying dryer venting for only a fraction of the units in its
  base, so check the count against the unit count rather than assuming coverage.
- Bath exhaust. Usually inline fans with dampers, carried here, and the register count follows the
  bath count rather than the unit count.

Also note that mechanical bidders commonly exclude unloading, uncrating, moving and storing any
appliance or equipment, so owner-furnished or allowance-purchased equipment arriving on site has no
receiving party unless one is named.

### Drywall and framing

- Architectural access panels. Furnished by the mechanical contractor and installed by drywall is
  the dominant shape, and it is stated that way on nearly every proposal that mentions them. Fire
  rated panels where required. The count is the leveling row, not the panel itself.
- Fire-rated access doors in ductwork are the mechanical contractor's to furnish and install, which
  is a different item from an architectural access panel in a wall or ceiling, and the two get
  conflated.
- Blocking of walls for equipment fastening and support. Excluded by some mechanical bidders and
  belonging to framing, and this is the same implausible-exclusion shape that shows up elsewhere:
  framing that exists only to carry mechanical equipment can end up in neither package.
- Nail shields at concealed refrigerant line sets, where framing or drywall fasteners could reach
  the line. Carried by one recurring bidder as an explicit inclusion, which tells you it has been
  bitten by it.
- Ceiling removal and replacement for access to the work on renovation projects.

### Structure and miscellaneous metals

Supplemental steel for hanging ceiling-mounted equipment, steel dunnage supporting rooftop
equipment, equipment pads and housekeeping pads, and structural reinforcement for a fuel oil tank
are all commonly excluded here. So is beam penetration and, on renovation work, slab scanning before
coring. Coring itself is usually carried but bounded by hole diameter, so a larger penetration falls
out of the inclusion silently.

### Fire protection and firestopping

Through-penetration firestopping of the trade's own penetrations is carried by roughly half of
bidders and is one of the most commonly offered deduct alternates. Where it is deducted from every
mechanical trade, someone still has to seal the penetrations. Acoustical and sanitary sealants are a
separate matter and generally sit elsewhere.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

This is the section the drawings cannot cover, and it is the reason a scope line has to carry more
than a mark and a count.

What mechanical contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The equipment schedules. This is the primary document and it is what the tag counts come from.
   Nearly every proposal keys its scope to schedule marks, and the sheets carrying those schedules
   are cited by number.
2. The mechanical plans, for routing, duct sizes, damper locations and penetration conditions.
3. The unit count. Every estimator sheet normalizes every bid to a cost per dwelling unit and ranks
   on it. Unit count is the leveling currency of this trade exactly as it is in the finish trades.
   If the set does not make the unit count unambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable.
4. Specific details, cited by number, for roof penetrations, equipment support and curbs. Bidders
   quote detail references back at the general contractor more often here than in any other trade.
5. The specification, for coating, insulation ratings, control requirements, basis-of-design
   manufacturers and the balancing standard.
6. The architectural sheets, reluctantly. Wall caps, dryer vents and hood ducts often appear only
   there, and at least one bidder excludes anything mechanical shown on architectural drawings by
   that description alone.

They do not price from a measured duct takeoff. Sheet metal is described and not measured in
essentially every proposal seen.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Union versus open shop labor. The largest single driver by a wide margin, and it stratifies a bid
  list cleanly. On one project every open shop bidder ranked below every union bidder with no
  overlap at all, and the cheapest union bidder sat about thirty percent above the cheapest open
  shop bidder. Within either band the spread is far narrower than the gap between the bands.
- Whether the project buys a building automation system or stand-alone thermostats. Worth roughly
  fifteen percent of the base bid, and no mechanical proposal prices the automation side.
- Whether balancing must be certified and third party.
- The ventilation architecture: central dedicated outside air with common risers, or individual
  energy recovery ventilators per apartment. On a large building the deduct for switching from
  central to individual has been worth close to a third of the entire base bid, and it is the
  standing value engineering item in the trade. On a small building the direction can reverse, with
  the individual route costing more. It is not a rule, it is a calculation, and the drawings will
  show one option only.
- Whether heat recovery is required in the common area system, or a simpler heat pump system will
  serve. The branch selector boxes that heat recovery requires are a specific, nameable cost.
- The equipment manufacturer actually bought, and whether the named basis of design can be furnished
  at all. One brand substitution was withdrawn mid-negotiation because the substitute could not meet
  the specification. Another bidder pointed out that one manufacturer's larger liquid line diameter
  permits longer line set runs than a competitor's, which changes the installation and not just the
  equipment price. Two bidders on the same project gave opposite advice about which of two brands
  was cheaper.
- Which manufacturer's representative holds the territory for the specified brand, and whether the
  bidder can submit through them.
- The domestic hot water system type, which is a plumbing decision that lands in this package. In-unit
  heat pump water heaters discharge cool air and need a louvered room or ducted exhaust, and that
  exhaust is the mechanical contractor's.
- Bond rate and insurance basis. Both quoted as a percentage of contract value, both only on request,
  and the bond percentages seen vary by more than a factor of two between bidders on the same project.
- Payment portal fees, quoted as a small percentage of contract value plus a per-sub-tier charge.
- Workforce compliance obligations. Municipal residents, minority and women participation goals, and
  business-enterprise certification, all of which are bid list facts the general contractor tracks
  per bidder and none of which appear on a drawing.
- Experience modification rate, tracked per bidder.
- Whether the energy standard applied to the building is a conventional code compliance or a
  high-performance certification, which changes both equipment selection and sealing requirements.
- Whether a proprietary duct-sealing process is required. On one project a bidder offered it as an
  add alternate with its own note that it is called for in neither the specification nor the
  drawings, and on another the same process was in the base and worth about two percent of it.
- Whether the project uses a bid portal, and which one. Bids get logged against invitations sent
  through a portal, and at least one bidder missed the invitation to spam.
- Rigging mobilization count, phasing, and any adjacent tenancy requiring off-hours work.
- Whether temporary heat or temporary conditioning will be expected, and whether permanent equipment
  may be used for it.
- Long lead equipment timing and whether stored material will be billed.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders openly price around missing information and say
so. One wrote directly on the face of its proposal that the plans are incomplete. Others carry
quantities as allowances rather than counts, or state that a rooftop unit quantity is a budget
figure with the final count to be set by airflow. Treat any row marked as an allowance at design
development as an uncommitted quantity, not an inclusion. The dispersion is also visibly wider at
design development: on the small project bid at that stage the field spread about two and a half to
one, roughly twice the spread the same trade shows on a conformed set. By construction documents and
conformed sets, bidders switch to citing mark ranges with real counts.

Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its folder's set stage frequently disagree. Documents
in the corpus cite two different drawing dates in the same file, carry a date a full year off on
every header line, cite an attached qualifications page dated two months earlier than the proposal
itself, or cite no drawing set at all. A proposal that names no set date is the strongest single
signal that its number is not priced against the same documents as the rest of the field, and in
this corpus the two bidders that never cited a set date were also the two lowest and least
comparable numbers.

### Demolition and make safe on renovation work

This deserves its own treatment because it behaves unlike the rest of the trade.

- It is frequently a separate contract, a separately priced breakout, or bid by a different company
  than the new work, and it must be normalized before any comparison. On one project the estimator
  added a bidder's separately quoted demolition breakout to its base so that a make-safe-inclusive
  comparison could be made.
- The scope is almost always bounded to cut, cap and make safe. Dropping to the floor and removal
  are somebody else's, and each of those is its own row.
- Refrigerant reclaim is an environmental compliance operation priced in labor hours with a hard
  cap, with time and material beyond it and a per-day rate for extension. It is the only scope in
  the trade whose unit is time rather than equipment, and the only one where the bid can be exceeded
  by conditions rather than by scope.
- Uniformed fire watch for welding, brazing and soldering is commonly carried on renovation work and
  excluded on new work.
- Rework and repair of existing systems not specifically shown, relocation of existing systems not
  shown, tie-ins to existing piping, repairs to existing equipment, and slab scanning are all
  routinely excluded, which means the concealed-conditions risk sits with the general contractor.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, by equipment schedule mark. The dominant unit, and the one every proposal speaks in.
- Dwelling units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator sheet.
- Square feet, banded by space type, for the common and back-of-house portion. This is the
  estimator's basis rather than the bidder's. The rate ladder observed runs roughly: amenity space
  highest, then corridors at about two fifths of the amenity rate, then back of house at about a
  quarter, then garage at about a fifth, then a retail cool dark shell at about a twelfth of the
  amenity rate, with basement and parking ventilation lowest of all. Amenity space is the most
  expensive square foot in the building for this trade by a wide margin.
- Linear feet, for refrigerant piping, condensate piping and mechanical screen. Refrigerant line
  footage appears occasionally in a proposal and is one of the few duct-side or pipe-side quantities
  anyone states.
- Gallons, for fuel oil tank capacity and for the first fill, with the fill priced at a stated
  per-gallon rate explicitly subject to the price on delivery day.
- Labor hours, for refrigerant reclaim and drain down on demolition work, with a per-day extension
  rate.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, general liability insurance, payment portal fees, and
  materials contingency reserve.
- Cubic feet per minute, for fan and ventilation sizing, which appears in scope descriptions but
  never as a priced unit.

Per-dwelling-unit quantity ratios worth carrying as a sanity check on any HVAC quantity read of a
multifamily set. From a bidder's own counts on a large residential building, per dwelling unit:
roughly one energy recovery ventilator, roughly one dryer vent, a little over two heat pump split
systems, about thirteen registers, grilles and diffusers, about ten radiation dampers, about two
motorized dampers, and about four exterior wall caps. One estimator's own workbook carries a
simpler rule on a different project: one outdoor heat pump, one in-unit heat pump, one air handling
unit and one rooftop ventilation unit share per dwelling unit, plus two ceiling diffusers, two
grilles and two access panels. These two are not in perfect agreement, which is itself the point:
use them to notice when a count is implausible, not to generate one.

Cost shape. Where a general contractor forced a lump sum apart into its named scope rows, the
resulting picture was roughly: the central ventilation plant about a quarter of the base, the in-unit
equipment about a tenth, the common area system about a twentieth, the fuel oil system about a
twenty-fifth, certified balancing and the duct-sealing process a couple of percent each, and every
other named item under two percent apiece. The unnamed remainder, which is ductwork, insulation and
field labor, was over forty percent of the base. In other words the largest single cost in this
trade is the part nobody itemizes, and no proposal will decompose it for you.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Labor market, union versus open shop. Tracked on every bidder in every leveling, and on one
   project it stratified the ranking perfectly with no overlap between the two bands.
2. The ventilation architecture, central versus individual. Worth close to a third of the base on a
   large building.
3. Whether the building automation system is in this package. Worth roughly fifteen percent of a
   base bid when bought separately.
4. Equipment manufacturer and package. Whole-brand substitutions across the heat pump and
   ventilation equipment have been worth five to six percent of the base, and they can move the
   electrical package too.
5. Total equipment count, driven by unit count and by how much amenity and common area the project
   carries.
6. Whether heat recovery is required in the common system, and the branch selector boxes it needs.
7. Certified versus non-certified balancing, worth a couple of percent.
8. The fuel oil system, which is small in the scheme of the package but carries the widest bidder
   dispersion of any subsystem, by a factor of about three.
9. Warranty term, bond rate and insurance basis. Warranty extension has been worth about two percent
   and bond rates vary between bidders by more than a factor of two.
10. Damper scope, particularly motorized dampers, which also drives access panel counts.
11. Coordination and three-dimensional modeling, a standing deduct on every project.
12. Duct material and insulation choices in apartments, where a board substitution has been worth a
    stated amount per dwelling unit.
13. Phasing, rigging mobilization count and off-hours work.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per dwelling unit has run at about 1.7 times, about 2.0 times, about 2.5
times and about 3.1 times. The two widest were driven by a single low outlier that cited no drawing
set date, and by a design development set where the documents themselves were incomplete. The
narrowest was an all-open-shop field on a permit set. Bidder counts per project have run from five
to nineteen. The top of the range is usually a bidder who has priced a different job or left every
boundary open, not a bidder who is expensive.

One more pricing convention worth knowing: an estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base
bid plus adjustments, where adjustments normalize each bidder to a common scope basis rather than
merely adding a uniform plug. Real adjustments seen include reducing a bidder by the value of a
two-year warranty so it can be compared on a one-year basis, increasing another by the value of
certified balancing it had not carried, and moving a bidder's entire number into the adjustment
column when its bid was narrow or alternate-only so that a partial bid never sits at the bottom of
the ranking as if it were a complete one. The general contractor's own budget is sometimes carried
in the ranking as a pseudo-bidder, and on one project it landed mid-pack above four real bids.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly forty HVAC proposals and email bids, a specialty controls quote,
several mechanical screen and roof screen quotes, a separate demolition and make-safe package, an
engineer's system selection matrix carrying the estimator's own handwritten per-unit budgets, and
four estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional
market, all bid by one general contractor. The proposals span design development, permit,
construction documents, conformed sets and one bulletin round. Several companies appear on more than
one project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4
testable, and one bidder's template appears on all four, which is what makes its unchanging rows
readable as boilerplate.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity, back of house, garage and
  small retail space, in one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against
  commercial office, institutional, healthcare, laboratory, industrial or public prevailing-wage
  work, and those are exactly the sectors where hydronic systems, chillers, boilers, cooling towers
  and pumped water distribution dominate. This corpus is almost entirely electric heat pump and
  variable refrigerant flow work with electric resistance supplements. There is no chilled water,
  no boiler plant, no steam, and essentially no gas-fired heating anywhere in it, so nothing here
  describes how those systems are packaged, bid or levelled.
- Only one project involved renovation of an existing building, so the demolition, make safe and
  refrigerant reclaim material rests on that project alone, and no project involved an occupied
  building.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation and which were bought out. The trade's habit of grinding a number down across
  four to six rounds is visible; where it landed is not.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the flexible duct limits, working hour
  windows, escalation validity windows and storage limits convert into change orders is read out of
  the proposals' own language rather than observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- No submittal or coordination-phase record is in the corpus, so the claim that a named
  basis-of-design manufacturer can fail as a substitution rests on a single negotiation email.
- The building automation carve-out rests on one specialty controls quote on one project. The ratio
  it implies against a mechanical bidder's stand-alone controls line is striking and is corroborated
  by every proposal's exclusion language, but the magnitude itself is a single data point.
- The corpus has not shown kitchen exhaust for a commercial kitchen, grease duct, or hood
  suppression, which is a substantial and differently packaged body of work in any project with a
  restaurant or a full amenity kitchen.
- The corpus has not shown smoke control or atrium smoke evacuation systems, dedicated data centre
  or laboratory cooling, or clean-room work.
- The corpus has not shown how the package is split when a project runs a design-build or
  performance-specification mechanical delivery. Every project here is plan and spec, with a full
  engineered set and equipment schedules, and the design responsibility never sits with the bidder.
  Where MEP documents are thin and the design responsibility itself is part of the scope, none of
  the grain rules in section 3 have been tested.
- Per-unit quantity ratios in section 7 come from two projects and disagree with each other in
  detail. They are usable as an implausibility check and not as a takeoff.
