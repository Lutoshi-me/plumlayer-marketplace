# Roofing

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the roofing package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The entry
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real roofers can bid it and a real estimator can level it.

One warning belongs at the top because it governs everything below. Roofing looks like a
straightforward package and it is not. The membrane itself is the least contested part of the work.
Almost all of the money that moves at leveling moves at the boundary between the membrane and the
forty other things that sit on, through, or beside it, and at the boundary between what the
specification requires and what a bidder actually priced. A scope list that describes the roof
assembly well and the boundaries badly will produce a set of numbers that cannot be compared.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

There is no stable CSI section for this trade. Low-slope membrane roofing appears under 075300,
075400, 075200 and 075423 across projects, and the same bidder will be filed under two different
numbers on two jobs. The section number is a weak identifier. The strong identifier is the assembly:
a membrane over a coverboard over insulation over a vapor barrier, terminated in shop-formed metal.

The package as bid in the market is the roof assembly plus its terminations plus its accessories, not
the membrane alone. Three spec sections travel with it so reliably that a proposal citing sections
will usually name all of them: membrane roofing, sheet metal flashing and trim, and roof accessories
or roof specialties. Joint sealants come along as related to roofing operations. A bidder that
enumerates by spec section will explicitly claim these as included and then list the neighbouring
Division 07 sections it refuses, which is the most legible boundary statement any bidder in this trade
produces.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another section or another division:

- Edge metal, drip edge, fascia and coping cap. The default, and carried by nearly every bidder. But
  it is bounded: bidders will state that their metal exists solely to terminate the roof membrane,
  and that capping a trellis or flashing between two runs of siding is not their work even though it
  is the same gauge of the same material formed on the same brake.
- Roof hatches, safety rail kits at the hatch, and walkway pads. Commonly carried, though the hatch
  is sometimes an add rather than base scope.
- Scuppers, collector boxes and downspouts. Genuinely split. Some bidders carry the whole rainwater
  path including splash blocks, others carry the through-wall scupper and explicitly leave the
  collector box and downspout to someone else, which leaves the water landing nowhere.
- Rough carpentry for nailers and blocking related to roofing operations. A minority claim it. Most
  exclude blocking wholesale, and blocking is one of the most reliable estimator plugs in the trade.
- Roof demolition on renovation work. Usually priced by the roofer as its own line, and frequently
  bought instead with the demolition package, so it gets deducted at leveling. A roofing number that
  looks competitive may be carrying demolition that already has an owner.
- Gutters and downspouts, and pitched metal roofing including standing seam and snow guards. Carried
  where the project has them, excluded by name on flat-roof projects. On a project with both roof
  types, expect at least one bidder to lump membrane and metal into a single undifferentiated number,
  which is a leveling problem rather than a price problem.
- Temporary roofing and dry-in. Excluded by most bidders as a standing line. One bidder on a heavily
  penetrated renovation carried it explicitly along with multiple remobilizations, which is the
  correct answer and an uncommon one.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like roofing:

- The amenity deck or plaza assembly on structural slab, the one built from hot fluid applied
  rubberized asphalt with a drainage board and high-compressive rigid insulation under pedestal
  pavers. This is the single cleanest carve-out in the trade. Where a scope sheet enumerates it by
  assembly mark, roofing bidders answer no to the mark and to every one of its layers, without
  exception, and the work is bought from waterproofers instead. Do not put it in the roofing package
  and do not treat its absence from roofing bids as a gap. Do price it, because it is expensive: on
  the projects seen its unit rate runs one to one and a half times the main roof rate, and the two
  waterproofers quoting the same deck disagreed with each other by six percent on quantity and by
  over sixty percent on unit rate.
- The vegetated green roof. A landscape package, not a roofing one. It is bid by green roof
  installers who start at the root barrier laid over someone else's waterproofing, and whose
  proposals carry growing media, planters, plantings, drip irrigation and multi-year maintenance
  visits. Its warranty is written against the waterproofing spec section, not the roofing one.
- The pedestal paver system and its protection sheet, considered separately from the deck assembly
  underneath. On terrace assemblies where the membrane is answered yes, the pedestal pavers and the
  protection sheet above them are routinely answered no by the same bidder in the same breath, so the
  terrace splits horizontally at the top of the membrane.
- The rooftop mechanical equipment screen. Its frame is bid by a screen manufacturer or a misc metals
  fabricator, its base blocking is carpentry, its cladding may be in the siding package, and the
  flashing of every one of its posts is the roofer's. See section 5, because this one element split
  four ways is the clearest illustration of how this trade actually fails at leveling.
- Roof anchors, davits and permanent fall protection. Its own package with its own vendors. See
  section 5.
- Fixed and ballasted rooftop guard rail systems. No bidder seen carrying the product. Bidders answer
  install-only, or price the whole system as a large add, or answer no. Somebody has to buy the rail.

## 2. How the market bids

The default output is one number. Segmentation into two or three numbers happens, and when it does it
segments by assembly family or by building, essentially never by trade operation.

What varies enormously is not the number count but the enumeration grain behind it. Six distinct
shapes are common enough to recognise on sight, and knowing which one you are holding tells you what
question to ask next:

- The assembly component matrix. A row per assembly layer and accessory, each with independent
  columns for labor included, labor excluded, material included, material excluded. This is the most
  informative shape in the trade because it exposes furnish-versus-install per component rather than
  per package, and it makes the substrate-board and vapor-barrier boundaries visible without asking.
  Two unrelated bidders on one project used the identical template, so the form travels between
  competitors the way exclusion boilerplate does.
- The element count. Every flashed element carried with an explicit count and a note naming who does
  the adjacent work. The most enumerated shape seen ran to twenty distinct counted quantities and not
  one unit price, because the counts exist to bound scope rather than to price it.
- The flat list. Numbered assembly lines against one area figure, usually with a separate exclusion
  block carrying the real information.
- The assembly type. Scope organised by the architect's roof assembly marks, sometimes opening with
  the bidder's own read of the roof (area, parapet height, drainage method, deck slope) before any
  materials appear. This is the grain that matches how estimators actually level, and it is worth
  encouraging.
- The spec section. Boundary drawn as a list of sections in and a list of sections out, with the
  assembly description subordinate. Rare and excellent.
- The answered scope sheet. Where a general contractor issues an enumerated scope sheet, bidders
  return it filled in and the sheet becomes the proposal. Sheets seen have run to a couple of hundred
  rows and are the richest single artifact in the trade.

What a proposal will normally state:

- The basis document. A named set with a date, commonly with the architect's name and project number.
  This is reliable, and it is the field you should order revisions by. It is also where basis-set
  mismatch shows up: a bidder calling the same set design development that the general contractor
  calls a permit set, a bidder citing a set four months older than its competitors, and a proposal
  whose estimate half cites one set while the scope sheet bound into the same file cites another.
- Membrane thickness, colour, and attachment method. Nearly always.
- Insulation R-value, layer count and attachment method. Nearly always, and it is the field most
  likely to drift between rounds.
- Coverboard type and thickness. Commonly, including the fastening rate in plates per board on the
  more detailed bids.
- Edge metal gauge and finish, and whether colours are limited to a manufacturer standard range.
  Commonly, with premium colours as an extra.
- Warranty term, and increasingly whether it is a no-dollar-limit system warranty or material only,
  plus a separate workmanship term. Both numbers matter and they are not the same warranty.
- Sales tax treatment. Almost always stated, because material is the dominant cost.
- Labor type, union or open shop, in about half of proposals, though an estimator records it for
  every bidder regardless.

What a proposal will normally not state:

- Unit prices for the roof itself. Almost never. Where unit pricing exists it is for adders (pipe
  boots, pitch pockets, scuppers, curbs by size, walkway pads by the foot), not for the assembly.
- The labor and material split. Seen once, and the disclosure was startling: labor under a tenth of
  the number and material over four fifths. That ratio is why escalation and tariff clauses dominate
  the qualifications in this trade rather than wage rates.
- Crew size, duration or schedule, unless a scope sheet asks.
- Bond cost, unless asked. When asked it comes back as a percentage of contract value.
- Any quantity at all, in a meaningful minority of proposals.

On roof area, treat every stated quantity as an independent read rather than a fact. On one building
the four bidders who stated an area gave figures spanning about eleven percent, and the trade mixes
units freely: some quote squares, some square feet, and squares are sometimes rounded so hard that
back-converting them is misleading. On another building the ratio of a bidder's stated roof area to
the general contractor's footprint ran from just under one to just over one and a quarter. If your
scope list does not fix the area basis, every bid will be measured against a different roof.

Exclusion behavior splits the market the same way it does in painting, and the split matters more
here because the boundary count is higher. Most proposals carry ten to twenty-five exclusions. A
minority carry two. A bidder with two exclusions is not offering broader scope; it has left every one
of the seams in section 5 open, and each one will need a plug or a question. Two of the lowest bids
seen carried the shortest exclusion lists.

There is also a house-conditions species worth recognising. Some bidders attach a standing conditions
document, several times longer than the bid itself, covering escalation, mobilization minimums,
penetration protocol, damage policy, weather, winter conditions, insurance limits and hourly rates,
and asserting that the attachment supersedes any written clauses in the contract. Read it. It is
where the real qualifications live, it is reused verbatim across projects, and the assertion of
supersession is a contract term dressed as a bid attachment.

### Revision habits, and why they are the most dangerous part of this trade

Roofing revisions are negotiation artifacts far more often than document-change artifacts, and four
distinct mechanisms recur:

- The specification ladder. A bidder's base falls across rounds while the specification quietly
  leaves it. On the clearest case seen, a base dropped about fourteen percent between rounds while
  the membrane thinned, the insulation R-value fell, the warranty shortened from a long no-dollar-
  limit term to a shorter material-only one, and full coping became drip edge at sloped parapets only.
  None of the four changes was flagged as a deviation. Only a line-by-line diff against the earlier
  round exposed them. If a roofing base falls and the proposal level changes from bid to buyout, diff
  the assembly before believing the saving.
- The same ladder re-presented as a menu. The following round moved no money at all and was still the
  whole story: it added five add-alternates, each one restoring a specification item the base had
  dropped. Buying all five puts the number back above where the ladder started, so the apparent
  saving was negative. Worse, the warranty alternate was gated on buying the membrane alternate
  first, so the two could not be levelled independently.
- Comeback absorption after a scope sheet. A base rises while a technical value falls, because the
  round is an answer to the general contractor's enumerated sheet rather than a re-price: items the
  sheet asked about came into the number, and the insulation R-value came down to what the sheet
  actually specified. A price rise here is the bid getting more correct, not more expensive.
- The restructure that moves nothing. Two rounds byte-identical in scope and total, differing only by
  a new deduct line. That deduct was the winter-conditions workaround from the house conditions
  finally surfaced as a priced option, which is real information carried by a revision worth nothing.

Two mechanical warnings. Filename revision labels are unreliable: a file labelled as an initial round
carried a document calling itself a first revision, one revision label predated its own document by
six weeks, and one proposal was filed three times across two set-stage folders under two spellings of
the company name with one spurious revision suffix, all three files byte-identical. Order rounds by
the cited basis document and by internal scope content, never by a filename or a letterhead date.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Roofing scope sheets have run about 30, 37, 62 and 93 rows. Target roughly 50 to 90 scope lines for a
low-slope package on a multi-building or multi-assembly project, and expect the count to rise on
renovation work where the existing-conditions block alone can carry six to ten rows.

The primary organizing axis is the roof assembly mark, and this is the strongest single
recommendation in this entry. Where the architect names assemblies, build the scope list on those
marks and decompose each mark into its layers as separate answerable rows. The sheet that did this
produced usable answers from every bidder, exposed exactly where each one stopped inside a given
assembly, and made the amenity-deck carve-out visible as a block of consistent noes rather than as an
ambiguous silence. The alternative axes, area or building, are secondary and should nest inside the
assembly mark rather than replace it.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every roof assembly mark on the set, decomposed into its layers: vapor retarder with its type and
  thickness, base insulation with its R-value and attachment, tapered insulation and crickets,
  coverboard with its type and thickness, membrane with its thickness, colour and attachment, and any
  protection sheet or overburden above it. The layer rows are what let you see a bidder taking the
  membrane and declining the pavers, or taking the assembly and declining the vapor barrier.
- The R-value, twice: as a minimum and as an average. Sets specify both and bidders answer whichever
  is cheaper. R-value drift between rounds is the most common silent deviation in the trade, and a
  scope row naming both figures is what catches it.
- Membrane thickness and warranty term as separate rows from the membrane itself. They are priced
  separately as alternates and they move independently.
- Edge metal, coping and fascia as separate rows from each other, and separately again from
  counterflashing and through-wall flashing. Bidders routinely carry the first group and exclude the
  second, and a single sheet-metal row will always be ambiguous.
- Every rooftop penetration family with a count: pipe boots, pitch pockets, duct penetrations, vent
  pipes, roof hydrants, scuppers, drains, trash chute curbs, expansion joints. Counts diverge wildly
  between bidders reading the same set, so a count on the row is the only way to know whether two
  bidders priced the same roof.
- Every rooftop element owned by another trade, as its own flash-only row: mechanical curbs,
  equipment supports and sleepers, duct supports, screen posts, railing posts and privacy screens,
  davit and anchor bases, catwalk posts, skylights, solar provisions. State explicitly on each row
  whether the roofer sets, flashes, or both. The best proposals seen do exactly this on their own
  initiative and count each family.
- Drains, split into the plumber's part and the roofer's part. See section 5.
- Roof accessories individually: hatch, hatch safety rail, ladder or alternating tread stair, walkway
  pads with a stated length, slip sheets under condensers with a count, splash blocks with a
  material.
- The vapor barrier grade as its own row. The gap between a poly sheet and a self-adhered forty-mil
  barrier was worth about eleven percent of one base bid, and the bidder who carried the cheap one
  was the low bidder and the eventual selection.
- Electronic leak detection, as its own row and expected to be an alternate. See the gap list below.
- Temporary roofing, dry-in, and temporary covering of curb openings, each separately.
- Winter conditions, as a row with a stated assumption. Excluded by essentially every bidder seen.
- Mobilization count and phasing, as a row with a stated number of mobilizations or zones.
- Warranty, split into system warranty term, whether it is no-dollar-limit, wind speed rating, and
  workmanship term. Four separate facts that bidders present as one word.
- On renovation: existing roof removal, ballast removal, abatement interface, investigation and
  repair of damaged existing deck, removal and reinstatement of existing skylights, removal and
  replacement of existing anchors and davits, parapet modification framing and sheathing, and debris
  logistics including vertical transport. Each of these was answered differently by different bidders
  on the renovation seen, and each moved real money.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Adhesives, fasteners, plates, termination bars, cant strips, saddles, cover tape, cut-edge sealant,
  T-joint covers and preformed corners. These are attributes of the membrane line. No estimator sheet
  seen breaks them out and no bidder prices them separately.
- Individual roof areas of the same assembly. Main roof, elevator overruns, stair overruns and
  canopies collapse into their assembly mark unless the assembly actually changes. One bidder listed
  each area with a count, which is useful as a completeness check, not as a pricing axis.
- Membrane manufacturer and product name. An attribute, not a line, though it is worth recording
  because warranty terms follow the manufacturer.
- Seam welding procedure, seam testing frequency and quality control steps. Attributes of the
  membrane line, occasionally offered as a differentiator.
- Crickets, when they are part of the tapered insulation scope. They collapse into the tapered
  insulation row unless the set treats them as a separate design.
- Individual penetrations within a family. Ninety-four pipe boots is one row with a count, not
  ninety-four rows.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In roofing that
test fires hardest in two places: on every seam in section 5, and on every layer of every assembly
where the specification states a performance value the bidder can quietly under-deliver.

The second half is what makes this trade different from painting. A painting bidder that carries less
scope shows it by naming fewer surfaces. A roofing bidder that carries less scope often carries the
same surfaces at a lower specification, and the proposal reads as complete. Rows that name the
performance value, not just the component, are the only defence: membrane mils, insulation R-value as
both minimum and average, coverboard thickness, metal gauge, warranty term and type, and wind uplift
rating. On wind rating in particular, two bidders on different projects disclosed a system rated to a
speed well below what a coastal structural drawing would typically require, and in both cases the
disclosure was buried in a yes-or-no grid answer rather than stated as a deviation.

Silence is a finding here as much as in any trade. If a scope item is one that some bidders carry and
others exclude, and a given proposal says nothing about it, that silence is a scope gap to surface,
not an inclusion to assume. Beware in particular the bidder with almost no exclusions: its silence is
total, and it is usually near the bottom of the ranking.

### The structural gap list

These are the items the market systematically will not carry, that an estimator adds to every number
before comparing. A scope list naming all of them is doing the plugging in advance.

1. Wood blocking, nailers, sleepers and equipment supports. The most reliable plug in the trade,
   applied on nearly every column of the sheets seen.
2. Electronic leak detection. Where a specification calls for it, every bidder prices it as an
   alternate and none carry it in base. Its adds ranged from about seven to about twelve percent of
   the base bids they attached to, and one project resolved it simply by excluding it.
3. Pedestal pavers, protection sheets and paver sub-base at terraces and egress paths.
4. The hot fluid applied amenity deck assembly, which is not a plug so much as a separate buy.
5. Fixed or ballasted rooftop guard rail systems, and permanent fall protection where it is not its
   own package.
6. Winter conditions, temporary roofing and temporary curb covering.
7. Material escalation and tariff exposure. Some bidders quote a percentage allowance, some carry a
   contractual escalation clause instead and price nothing, and the general contractor typically
   records both and carries neither.
8. Bond, quoted as a percentage of contract value.
9. Rooftop mechanical wall and pipe chase housing, and its cap.
10. On renovation: pipe portals and similar penetration families that appear only in a later set,
    demolition where it may already sit in another package, and abatement sequencing.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate reproduced verbatim by repeat bidders across projects, and its
presence carries no project information. Read it for the trade map, not for the project. Bidders
appear on two, three and four of the projects seen and their exclusion lists are unchanged.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- An exclusion naming a specific assembly mark from the set. When a bidder writes out assembly marks
  and excludes them, it read the assembly schedule and found something it will not do. It is also
  where internal contradictions surface: one proposal included pavers at balconies naming an assembly
  mark, then excluded that same mark by name in its own exclusion list.
- An exclusion justified by a named drawing detail. Two bidders excluded anchor or davit work by
  pointing at a specific structural detail sheet, which tells you the detail exists and that they
  read it.
- An exclusion accompanied by a request for information. One bidder excluded the vapor barrier and
  filed it as a question rather than assuming either way, while every competitor priced one. When a
  bidder and the general contractor disagree about whether scope exists on the set, that is a
  documents problem and it is worth an RFI.
- An exclusion phrased as awaiting additional details. One bidder twice declined to price a condition
  on the stated ground that the drawings did not carry enough detail. That is a design-completeness
  flag pointed at the set, not a bidder preference.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: an existing structure, a specific
  penthouse, a post-tension deck, a courtyard, a neighbouring building.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Mobilization economics. Bidders state a minimum contiguous roof area that constitutes a major
  mobilization, and charge for smaller ones. They state how many mobilizations the price carries, in
  zones or in phases, and one bidder carried two crane mobilizations per zone across five zones. A
  phased project priced as a single continuous mobilization is a change order waiting to happen, and
  the highest bidder on one project was the only one whose price assumed a continuous unphased
  presence on a job everyone else described as five zones.
- Deck readiness. The deck must be broom clean, drains installed and secured at the low point,
  perimeter and penthouse framing and sheathing complete, and large equipment curbs already set,
  before the roofer will mobilize. Bidders also state they cannot roof over drains for insurance
  reasons. This is a sequencing constraint hidden inside a qualification and it belongs on the scope
  line.
- Penetration protocol. Notice periods of one to two days, a requirement that penetrations be ganged
  rather than trickled, and a window (sixty days was seen) inside which all other trades' penetrations
  must be complete or another mobilization is chargeable. Bidders also state their price is based on
  penetrations clearly shown across the architectural, electrical, plumbing, mechanical and fire
  protection sets, and that anything else is extra.
- Winter conditions and adhesive chemistry. Low-VOC bonding adhesives do not work below about forty
  degrees, so a fully adhered system specified for a winter installation either changes to a
  mechanically fastened or induction-welded system, which bidders offer at no cost or as a credit, or
  buys a cold-weather adhesive good to about twenty-five degrees at an extra cost. This is a real
  design decision driven by the schedule, and the drawings will not raise it.
- Weather authority. Bidders reserve sole judgement over whether conditions permit installation, and
  reimbursement for demobilizing and securing the roof ahead of high wind.
- Escalation and tariffs. Given that material dominates this trade's cost, escalation clauses are the
  dominant commercial risk. Shapes seen include a stated percentage allowance carried in the number,
  a contractual clause converting any increase above a threshold into a change order, a validity
  window of thirty to ninety days, an assertion that pricing holds only for material purchased and
  paid within thirty days of subcontract, and a reservation of the right to amend the proposal at
  time of delivery. That last one makes the number an estimate rather than a bid, and it should be
  read that way.
- Post-tension decks. Bidders require written notice of post-tension areas so they can adhere rather
  than pin the insulation, and one demands it by registered letter.
- Damage and protection policy. A common formulation: minor damage circled by the offending trade is
  repaired free, egregious damage is time and material or a full reskin to preserve the warranty, and
  rooftop chemical or solvent spills may not be cleaned by others. Overburden areas require
  re-inspection and a signed waiver before pavers or decks go on top.
- Perimeter safety. Bidders flag the roof perimeter while they are working and disclaim
  responsibility for flagging it for anyone else, and refuse to work above or below other trades.
- Insurance basis, and one specific trap. Standard limits are stated on the more detailed proposals.
  One carrier-driven exclusion recurs and is easy to miss: a policy that specifically excludes
  residential condominium work due to litigation exposure, with apartments explicitly not counted as
  residential. On a for-sale residential project that exclusion is disqualifying unless additional
  coverage is bought, and the proposal mentions it in passing.
- Owner-controlled insurance programs. Where a project runs a wrap, bidders assume it in base and
  price conventional general liability as an alternate. The adds for the same coverage change ranged
  by a factor of two across bidders on one project, so it is not a wash line.
- Site logistics as an explicit condition. A clear perimeter of about twenty feet for forklift and
  boom lift access, crane access to a specific adjacent area, a dedicated laydown area, a container
  footprint on site, and in one case dedicated power to roof level. Where those are unavailable the
  price is stated to change.
- Payment and retainage terms. Retainage refused on material, billing on material receipt at the
  jobsite, manufacturer warranties released only on full payment, and lien rights asserted at thirty
  days. The warranty release condition in particular is a commercial term with a technical
  consequence: the no-dollar-limit warranty the scope requires does not exist until the last payment
  clears.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section that decides whether a roofing package levels cleanly, and it deserves more room
than any other. The roofer's real product is a continuous waterproof plane, and almost every seam in
this section is a place where somebody else's object punctures that plane. The recurring pattern is
that the other trade supplies and sets the object, the carpenter supplies the blocking, and the
roofer flashes it, with each of the three excluding the other two.

### The mechanical equipment screen, split four ways

On one project a single rooftop screen was divided as follows. The screen manufacturer quoted the
frame and panels furnished and installed, with roof flashings by others in capital letters as its
first note, and base supports requiring minimum blocking by others subject to engineering review. A
misc metals fabricator quoted the same screen under a different division on the same project. A siding
bidder priced fibre cement cladding on one face of the screen wall in base with the second face as an
add alternate, with sheathing by others. And the roofers divided three ways among themselves: one
flashed five hundred and eighteen screen posts and said so with a count, one flashed all screen
support posts with pre-moulded boots, and one excluded the equipment screen entirely including its
post flashing.

That is four packages and one element. Nobody is wrong and no two bids are comparable. A scope list
that carries a single equipment screen line will produce exactly this outcome. The line has to be at
least four: frame and panels, base blocking, cladding by face, and post flashing with a count.

### Roof anchors, davits and permanent fall protection

Its own package, and one that attracts three unrelated vendor types who share almost no vocabulary: a
designed-access-systems specialist, a facade-access equipment manufacturer, and a structural steel and
ornamental iron fabricator, all bidding the same package on one project.

They do not price the same thing. One quoted a few dozen discrete tie-back anchors plus a lifeline
run. Another quoted several hundred anchors plus over a thousand feet of stainless cable and thirteen
termination sets. That is a ninefold divergence in anchor count on one roof, and the reason is that
the anchor layout design is stated as by others on the set. Neither bidder is wrong.

What makes this package a roofing concern rather than a curiosity is the seam, and every anchor vendor
states it the same way. Flashing, sealing, cutting and patching of deck, insulation, roofing and
waterproofing are all by others. One goes further and dedicates a clause to it: removal and
replacement of roofing to give clean access to the attachment point, and restoring the area watertight,
is by others, and because of insurance restrictions the vendor may not even direct that roofing work.
It then recommends using the roofer who holds the warranty, because anyone else jeopardises it.

So the anchor package cannot be levelled alone, and the practical consequences are these. Every anchor
bid needs its flashing plug priced from the roofing side, with a count. The engineering stamp and the
load test are separately priced by some vendors, offered as an alternate by others, and omitted by a
third, so three bids for the same anchor count are still not comparable. Installation labor can exceed
the anchor material cost, so a material-only anchor quote is roughly half a bid. All supplemental
blocking, gusset plates and stiffener plates are excluded by the vendor and land on the general
contractor. And on one system, all drilling was excluded by others, which on a thru-bolted anchor is
most of the installation labor.

On the roofing side the answer is usually flash-only, and the good proposals count it: thirty-five
tie-off anchors flashed, thirty-six flashed on another sheet. Roof davits are commonly excluded except
for flashing, and anchor load testing is excluded by name. The one exception seen was a renovation
bidder who removed the existing window-washing davits and furnished and installed new through-bolted
anchors itself, taking scope a separate vendor bids everywhere else.

### Roof drains

A split boundary with a consistent shape and it needs two rows, not one. The plumber supplies the
drain, sets and secures the bowl, and provides the clamping ring, bolts and basket. The roofer
determines the drain height to achieve a proper sump and installs the clamping ring and basket the
plumber provided. Downspout nozzles, piping and any cast iron are the plumber's. Bidders also require
that drains be installed and secured at the low point of the deck before roofing starts, because
insurance will not let them roof over an open drain.

Where a project drains through scuppers instead, the split moves outboard: the roofer provides the
through-wall scupper, and the collector box and downspout are contested. One bidder carried scuppers,
collector boxes, downspouts and concrete splash blocks and excluded metal splash blocks by name.
Another carried the scupper and excluded the collector boxes and downspouts entirely.

### Mechanical curbs, equipment supports and condensers

The most commonly plugged seam in the trade. Most roofers exclude curbs, blocking, sleepers and
equipment supports wholesale, then flash a stated number of them. One bidder set, levelled and flashed
exactly two curbs and flashed seven duct supports, with everything else by others in advance. One
published a per-size price schedule for curbs and their blocking, which is the most useful artifact in
seen for pricing this plug. One carried sleepers and full blocking in base, which is rare enough
to be worth checking rather than assuming.

Large curbs recur as a sequencing condition: curbs above about four by eight feet must be in place
before roofing to avoid wasted material, and temporary covering of curb openings is an extra.

Condensers produce a seam of their own. On one project the roofer furnished slip sheet membrane for
several hundred condenser units and the mechanical trades cut and installed it, and the same roofer
excluded boots and pockets for those condensers on the correct ground that they penetrate the
mechanical screen wall rather than the roof, which puts their flashing with the mechanical trades
entirely. That is a genuinely subtle boundary and no drawing states it.

### Terminations against masonry, siding and the wall assembly

The roofer's metal terminates the roof membrane and nothing else. Bidders state this directly, and
they exclude through-wall, sill and shelf flashings, reglets, counterflashing, and the flashing
between two runs of siding or the cap on a trellis where no membrane exists.

Where the membrane returns up a wall the substrate matters and the roofer will state its requirement:
a termination bar at masonry or concrete, butyl or zip tape at plywood or gypsum sheathing, and
plywood rather than gypsum sheathing on any vertical wall over about two feet so the membrane will
adhere properly. That is a carpentry requirement generated by the roofing system, and if the wall
sections do not carry it the roofer will not warrant the termination.

The air and vapor barrier tie-in is the other half. Scope sheets ask whether vapor barriers run behind
parapets, up and over the top to the outboard leading edge, and tie into the facade barrier. Bidders
answer yes on the sheet and separately disclaim liability for water infiltration where the wall
barrier was furnished or installed by others. Someone owns the lap and the drawings rarely say who.

### The terrace and amenity deck horizon

Terraces split horizontally. The membrane assembly beneath is the roofer's and the pedestal paver
system and protection sheet above it are not, and the same bidder will answer yes to the lower half
and no to the upper half of the same assembly mark. Where the deck sits on structural slab and uses a
hot fluid applied membrane, the whole assembly leaves the package as described in section 1.

Terraces are also expensive out of proportion to their area. On the one project where a bidder priced
terraces separately, the terrace assemblies came in at roughly double the main roof rate per square
foot. Do not let terrace area disappear into a main-roof quantity.

Pavers themselves are genuinely contested rather than cleanly excluded. Most bidders exclude pavers
and pedestals wholesale. One carried porcelain pavers in base, one carried wood pavers and pedestals
bounded to the locations of one named detail, and one carried concrete pavers at balconies while
excluding the assembly mark those balconies belong to. Expect to ask.

### Safety rails, guard rails and edge protection

Three different objects that scope sheets conflate. The hatch safety rail kit usually comes with the
hatch and is carried. Temporary perimeter edge protection during the roofer's own work is carried, and
disclaimed for everyone else's work. The permanent fixed or ballasted guard rail system is carried by
nobody: bidders offer install-only against a general contractor-furnished rail, or price the whole
system as a large add, or answer no and note there is no budget for it. Two proposals carried
install-only of a general contractor-furnished rail system, which is a mode change on a single line
worth watching for.

### Renovation seams

On existing buildings the seam set changes shape entirely. Existing skylights were handled three ways
by three bidders on one project: flash only, excluded from removal and disposal, and removed with a
temporary cover installed and then reinstated on new blocking accounting for the added insulation
thickness. Existing anchors and davits split the same way. Roof demolition may be priced by the roofer
and already bought in the demolition package. Abatement sequencing is a real cost driver, and the
scope sheet asked bidders how they would work with the abatement contractor broken down by parapets,
main roof and other locations, a question no proposal answers unprompted. And vertical logistics can
be the whole demolition scope: eleven floors, service elevators, bagged and bundled debris, none of it
visible on a roof plan.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What roofers actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The roof plan and the roof details sheet. The primary documents. Penetration locations, drainage
   method, parapet conditions and details come from here, and bidders cite specific detail numbers
   when they want to be precise about a boundary.
2. The assembly schedule. Where the architect names roof assembly marks and lists their layers, this
   becomes the organizing spine of both proposals and leveling sheets. It is the most valuable single
   document in the set for this trade.
3. The membrane roofing spec section, mostly for the membrane, the warranty requirement, the wind
   uplift requirement and the leak detection requirement. Bidders cite the section number and rarely
   quote from it, which is exactly where deviations hide.
4. The sheet metal flashing and trim section, for edge metal and coping.
5. The mechanical, plumbing, electrical and fire protection sets, for the penetration count. Bidders
   say explicitly that their price is based on penetrations clearly shown across all of those sets,
   which means the roofing number depends on the completeness of four other disciplines.
6. The structural drawings, for deck type, slope, wind requirements and anchor attachment details.

They do not price from a measured takeoff. Not one proposal seen contained a priced quantity survey of
the roof. The normal method is area plus assembly type plus counted penetrations plus judgement.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Labor type, union or open shop. Union bidders occupied the top of the range on both projects where
  they appeared, in one case the top three positions.
- Phasing, zone count and the number of mobilizations the schedule will actually require.
- Deck readiness sequencing, and whether the drains, curbs and perimeter framing will genuinely be
  complete before the roofer arrives.
- Whether the work falls in winter, which changes the attachment method of the whole system.
- Material escalation and tariff exposure over the project duration. Given that material dominates
  this trade's cost, this is arguably the largest single uncertainty and it is entirely commercial.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies, and what conventional coverage costs if not.
- Whether the project is a for-sale condominium, which some carriers exclude outright.
- Bond requirement.
- Crane access, laydown area, container footprint, and the perimeter clearance for lifts.
- Who holds the roof warranty when another vendor penetrates the membrane later, and whether the
  anchor and solar packages have been sequenced to protect it.
- The real penetration count, as opposed to what the drawings show. Bidders disclaim anything not
  clearly shown, and the divergence between two bidders' counted penetrations on one roof was large
  enough to be a scope difference rather than a rounding difference.
- Whether a leak detection system is required and who scans it. The primer, the testing and the
  testing agency survey are three separable things and bidders separate them differently.
- Lead times on anything engineered. A permanent fall protection package quoted five weeks for shop
  drawings, three for approvals, six for manufacturing and six to eight for installation, roughly
  twenty weeks from order to installed. Nothing on a drawing says that.
- On green roof scope, the seasonal installation window. One installer's price stands only for an
  April to October installation, and carries a foliage-cover performance standard conditional on the
  architect verifying minimum daily sunlight hours. No roofing proposal seen carries a
  seasonal limit, and no drawing implies one.

Set-stage behavior. Design development rounds come in well below the eventual permit-stage market, and
the gap is the set rather than the market: one design development bid landed at roughly two thirds of
what the same roof drew at permit stage. Expect quantities to be approximate and assemblies to be
described generically at design development, and expect assembly marks, counts and specific detail
references to appear at construction documents and conformed sets. Note also that a proposal's stated
basis and its folder's set stage frequently disagree, and that on one project a bidder called the same
set design development that the general contractor called a permit set. Order by the cited set.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Squares, meaning one hundred square feet, for the roof area. The trade's native unit and the one
  used in the scope descriptions of most proposals that state an area at all.
- Square feet for the roof area, which is how the general contractor asks for it and how every
  leveling sheet normalizes.
- Each for every penetration family: pipe boots, pitch pockets, scuppers, drains, duct supports,
  screen posts, curbs, hatches, anchors, splash blocks, condenser slip sheets. This is where all the
  counting in this trade happens.
- Linear feet for walkway pads, expansion joints, downspouts, coping perimeter, cable runs and screen
  runs.
- Each by size band for mechanical curbs, which is the only place a published price schedule was seen.
- Hours for time and material work, quoted by title with supervisor, foreman, roofer and laborer
  rates, plus premium and double time uplifts.
- Percent of contract value for bond, for general liability, and for escalation allowances.
- Weeks for lead times on engineered rooftop equipment.
- Years for warranty terms, and separately for workmanship terms and maintenance periods.

A warning about the normalizing denominator, because it is the most consequential quantity convention
in this trade and it is easy to get wrong. Estimator sheets normalize to cost per square foot, but the
quantity underneath that rate is not consistent. One project normalized to building footprint, another
to total roof area, another to a roofing area figure, and one sheet's own budget row used a quantity a
quarter larger than the quantity it normalized every bidder against. Where the denominator is
footprint, the published cost per square foot is not a roofing unit rate at all, because the bidders'
own stated roof areas were well below the footprint. Cost per square foot figures from two different
projects are almost never comparable, and inside a single project they are comparable only because
every column shares one denominator.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause. The first three are commercial rather than
physical, which is the distinguishing feature of this trade:

1. Material escalation and tariff exposure. Material dominates the cost, the one bidder that disclosed
   its split showed labor under a tenth of the number, and every serious proposal carries an
   escalation mechanism of some kind. Whether that mechanism is an allowance inside the number or a
   clause that fires later is the single largest difference between two otherwise identical bids.
2. Specification compliance. The gap between what a set requires and what a base bid actually carries
   is worth more than most physical variables. Observed as ratios of a base bid: upgrading the vapor
   barrier from a poly sheet to a self-adhered forty-mil barrier ran about eleven percent; adding five
   R-value points to the insulation ran about seven percent; restoring parapet coping ran about three
   and a half percent; upgrading the membrane one thickness step ran under two percent; and adding a
   no-dollar-limit warranty ran a little over one percent. A bid that quietly dropped four of those at
   once was about fourteen percent below its own compliant version.
3. Insurance and bond basis. Whether a wrap applies, whether the project is a condominium, and whether
   a bond is required. Bond quotes ran between two and a half and three percent of contract value, and
   the general liability adds for the same coverage change varied by a factor of two between bidders.
4. Roof area, and how much of it is terrace rather than main roof. Terrace assemblies ran roughly
   double the main roof rate per square foot.
5. Assembly complexity: insulation thickness and R-value, whether the membrane is adhered or
   mechanically fastened, coverboard type, and how much tapered insulation the roof needs.
6. Penetration count. Several hundred boots and pockets on one roof, and the counts diverge between
   bidders reading the same set.
7. Phasing, zone count and mobilization count. A phased job priced as a single mobilization is
   mispriced.
8. Winter work, which changes the attachment method and adds cold-weather adhesive or a system change.
9. Labor type, union or open shop.
10. Electronic leak detection, worth roughly seven to twelve percent of a base bid where it is
    required.
11. Metal upgrades. Copper coping in lieu of aluminum ran about five percent of one roofing base.
12. Crane access, laydown and hoisting conditions.

One more pricing convention worth knowing. An estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base
bid plus adjustments, where adjustments are plugs added to every bidder to cover what the market
systematically will not carry, and the recurring adjustment on one project ran to roughly eleven
percent of a typical base. The plugs to expect are the ten items in the gap list at the end of section
3, and a scope line set that names all ten explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging for it.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per unit ran at about 1.6 times, 1.7 times, 2.2 times and 3.4 times. The
widest spread was on the renovation project, which is the existing-conditions effect: those rows are
answered inconsistently and each one moves real money. Bidder counts per project ran from eight to
fifteen columns, but column counts overstate the list: several columns are the same firm at different
set stages, and on one sheet three columns belonged to one company. Count firms, not columns.

Finally, watch the top of an ascending ranking as carefully as the bottom. On one project the lowest
row by cost per square foot sat at roughly one fifteenth of the next bidder, because it was a partial
or placeholder number, and it sat exactly where a reader stops looking. On another the lowest bidder
was roughly half the next one because it had omitted an entire roof type, and the plug for that one
missing scope family was worth about three quarters of the bid it was added to. In this trade the
lowest number is more often incomplete than cheap.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about two dozen roofing proposals plus their specialty siblings, drawn
from four projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor, together with the four
estimator leveling sheets for those projects. The specialty siblings included permanent fall
protection and roof anchor quotes from three unrelated vendor types, a rooftop equipment screen quote,
a vegetated green roof quote, and a siding quote opened only to resolve where the screen wall cladding
lives. Three of the projects are new multifamily or mixed-use construction and one is a renovation of
an existing building with an added rooftop structure. The proposals span design development, permit,
construction document and conformed sets, and several bidders appear on more than one project, which
is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Every project here is a single-ply thermoplastic membrane roof, overwhelmingly one membrane type,
  fully adhered, on polyiso over a coverboard. Nothing here is tested against modified bitumen,
  built-up roofing, single-ply EPDM as the primary system, sprayed polyurethane foam, or liquid
  applied roof systems, and the seam and exclusion map may differ for those.
- Steep-slope roofing is barely represented. One project carried a standing seam metal roof and one
  bidder carried asphalt shingle capability without pricing it. Shingle, slate, tile and architectural
  metal roofing conventions are not covered here at all.
- Only one project is a renovation, so the existing-conditions material in sections 3 and 5 rests on
  that project alone, and it happens to be a high-rise with service-elevator debris logistics that
  will not generalize to a low-rise reroof.
- Only one project carried a green roof and one carried an equipment screen, so those carve-outs are
  described from a small number of documents each.
- The anchor and fall protection package is well represented in vendor proposals but appears in only
  one leveling sheet, so how a general contractor actually levels three incommensurable anchor bids is
  not directly observed.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, whether the specification ladder described in section 2 was ever caught, or
  what the selected bidders were finally held to.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the mobilization, penetration and deck
  readiness qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language
  rather than observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- No warranty claim or leak history is in the corpus, so the practical difference between a
  no-dollar-limit system warranty and a material-only warranty is described as the market prices it,
  not as it performs.
- The corpus has not shown a roofing package on institutional, healthcare, industrial, or public
  prevailing-wage work, and prevailing wage appears only as a negative in bidder qualifications.
- The corpus has not shown solar-ready or solar-integrated roofing beyond bidders excluding future
  solar penetrations, and does not reveal who buys that work or how the warranty is protected.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the roofer also held the wall air barrier scope, which is a
  packaging shape that exists in the market and would collapse several of the section 5 seams.
