# Plumbing

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the plumbing package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The entry
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real plumbers can bid it and a real estimator can
level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI division 22 is the anchor, and it is a division rather than a section: proposals head themselves
"division 22 00 00" or "22 0000" and estimator sheets key the whole package to that code. Individual
sections get cited only when a bidder is disputing something, and then usually by a subsection
number pulled out of a general-requirements section rather than by a product section.

The package as bid is plumbing and gas together where gas exists. Proposals title themselves "plumbing
and gas" as one thing, carry gas piping in the same number, and take the gas permit alongside the
plumbing permit. But gas is not merged, it is attached: gas service, the gas meter, and the gas
company's own fees leave the package nearly always, and gas connections at ranges and stoves are
excluded about as often as they are carried. The result is a package that owns the gas pipe in the
building and owns nothing at either end of it. On a project with no gas at all the same exclusions
still appear, printed on the form, and mean nothing.

The single largest packaging question is whether the fixtures are in this package or beside it.
Plumbers carry fixtures by default and price them as a separable block, so a fixture package can be
lifted out of a bid without disturbing the rest. Expect to see it lifted out in at least three
different ways on any given project: a distributor quoted directly for material only, a bidder
splitting its own number into a fixtures half and a piping half, and a bidder furnishing some fixture
types while installing all of them. Never assume the fixture line means the same thing across two
proposals until you have read what each one furnishes.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits
elsewhere in the documents:

- Firestopping of the plumber's own penetrations. Carried by nearly every bidder, always bounded to
  its own penetrations, and offered back as a deduct by nearly every bidder as well. It behaves as an
  add-or-delete block rather than as integral scope.
- Pipe insulation. Almost always carried, and almost always carried with a location qualifier that
  turns it into several separate answers rather than one.
- Coring and sleeving. Carried nearly always, and bounded by hole diameter, by whether the element is
  structural, and by whether the material is concrete, wood or steel sheathing.
- Coordination and modeling. Carried by most, priced as a large separable line by some, excluded
  outright by others, and offered as a credit back to the general contractor when a project decides to
  consolidate modeling under one entity.
- Storm and roof drainage, including overflow drains, leaders and sometimes exterior downspouts. Almost
  always carried, but bounded to drains that carry a tag, which leaves untagged and scupper drains
  contested.
- Roof scuppers and metal downspouts. Occasionally carried, more often silently nobody's.
- Radon and soil gas vent piping. Excluded by most, occasionally carried and volunteered by a bidder
  noting it is not specified, and frequently unassigned.
- Elevator sump pumps and their oil separators. Nearly always carried, while the pit that holds them
  is nearly always excluded.
- The interior half of bathroom accessory installation. One bidder in the corpus carries grab bars,
  recessed mirrors, paper holders, soap dispensers in pre-drilled counters, shower curtains and shower
  poles, which is division 10 work in a plumbing proposal. Most bidders exclude toilet accessories
  outright. This is a genuine boundary, not a formality, and it is worth its own scope row.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like plumbing:

- Fire protection and sprinkler work. A separate trade and a separate division everywhere. Only one
  proposal seen bothers to exclude it, which is a sign of how settled the boundary is. The one place
  it re-enters is heat trace: a specialty heat trace vendor may quote plumbing and fire protection
  piping together in one document under a two-division heading, so a heat trace quote is not
  automatically all yours.
- Heat trace, discussed at length below because it is the most reliably unassigned scope in the trade.
- Water booster packages, sewage and storm ejectors, and central hot water plants, when a manufacturer's
  representative quotes them directly. These arrive as equipment supply quotes sitting in the plumbing
  folder looking like competing bids. They are not bids. They are price checks on one line inside every
  plumber's number, and they are a plug source.
- Underslab and foundation drainage. The site contractor's work by market default, even where the
  geotechnical drawings are the ones that show it, and even where the specification puts it on the
  plumber. Half the field excludes it citing normal trade practice.
- Excavation, backfill and compaction. The same pattern: a specification can assign it to the plumber
  and the plumber will still exclude it, sometimes saying so in exactly those words.

## 2. How the market bids

Plumbing is a one-number trade with a document problem. The base bid is a lump sum and always has
been. What varies enormously is how much apparatus arrives around that number, and the apparatus is
where all the information is.

Expect four proposal shapes, and expect all four on the same bid list:

- A one-page lump sum with a run-on inclusion sentence and a short exclusion list. Common. On a
  package worth thousands of dollars per unit this leaves every boundary in the trade open, and at
  leveling it needs a plug or a question on nearly every one. A short exclusion list is not narrower
  risk, it is unmeasured risk.
- A two or three page letter with a work-included list, a work-not-included list, and a value
  engineering block. The most common shape by a wide margin. In this shape the value engineering block
  is usually longer and more specific than the base scope statement, and it is where the bidder tells
  you what it actually read.
- The same letter with the general contractor's own scope sheet completed and bound into the PDF.
  Common once a project is at construction documents. The sheet can run to hundreds of rows and its
  answers are worth more than the letter.
- A standing qualifications matrix, alphabetized, roughly one hundred and fifty rows, with four
  checkbox columns: furnished and included, excluded, not applicable, and questionable. Used by some
  bidders on every project unchanged.

A proposal will nearly always state the drawing set it priced, usually by drawing number range and
date, sometimes by listing every sheet individually, occasionally with a sheet count so the basis is
checkable. It will state the specification date less reliably, and roughly half the time it cites
drawings only.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Piping material by system and by area. This is the discriminating fact of the trade and nearly every
  proposal supplies it, sometimes as its only real content. Expect it in the form of a material named
  per system (domestic water, sanitary waste and vent, storm, condensate, gas), split again by
  residential versus commercial or by above ground versus below ground, and qualified with "where
  allowed by code".
- The fixture basis, meaning which schedule the fixtures came off. Stated commonly, and it matters more
  than almost anything else in the document.
- Labor type, union or open shop. Stated by most, and an estimator records it for every bidder without
  exception because it is the largest price driver in the trade.
- Whether the warranty is one year or two.
- Permits, and whether the plumbing and gas permits are both included.
- Sales tax.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Quantities. Measured quantities are almost never present. Counts by fixture mark appear rarely, and
  when they do, they are the most valuable artifact in the round because they give every other bid a
  denominator.
- Labor hours. Stated occasionally, and where two bidders on the same project both state them, the
  hour counts sit far closer together than the prices do, which locates the price spread in labor rate
  rather than in labor content.
- A schedule of values, unless the general contractor's sheet asked for one.
- An exhaustive account of what is insulated. Insulation is nearly always stated as included and almost
  never bounded completely, and the qualifier that follows it usually carves out more than the reader
  expects.
- Bond, which is quoted as a percentage of contract value if at all, and which no bidder carries in the
  base.
- Whether it is carrying the specified product or an equal. Silence here is not compliance, for reasons
  set out in section 4.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs:

- The most common revision moves the price and nothing else, most often because the basis document date
  changed with a conformed or addendum issue. Movements of a few percent in either direction are
  routine.
- The second most common revision moves no money at all and restructures the presentation: breakouts
  re-keyed to the general contractor's own leveling sheet line numbers, each labelled furnish versus
  provide, with two or three ambiguous seams converted into an explicit include-plus-exclude pair.
  This revision is pure information and it is the most useful document in the pair. Treating a
  no-price-change revision as a no-op discards exactly the content that was added.
- A revision may drop the price while quietly changing the base condition. Watch for the case where a
  material substitution that was a deduct alternate in the earlier round has vanished from the
  alternate list, because it vanished by becoming the base. The price fell because scope left, and a
  line will usually have appeared in the exclusions saying the piping material no longer matches the
  specification. That price drop is not a price drop.
- A revision responding to an addendum or bulletin may price the addendum outside the base bid and say
  so in one line. Compared against a bidder who folded the same bulletin in, the ranking is false.
- On the supply side, a revision commonly keeps the price identical to the dollar and adds exclusions
  and a product deviation. The exceptions paragraph is the whole content. Diffing totals finds nothing.
- A revision is frequently not a document. It may be one sentence in an email giving a new number, and
  a general contractor re-soliciting on a conformed set will accept a written confirmation of no change
  as the revised bid. Expect to find a general contractor's own filing instruction inside the file
  telling somebody to log the email as a revision.

Two structural warnings about revision labels. Filename revision labels are unreliable, and a document
whose filename says one revision number can carry a different one in its own subject line. And a file
filed as a revision may not be a revision at all: general contractor internal correspondence about a
bid comparison gets filed under the bidder's name, and no delta should be computed against it.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Plumbing scope sheets in the corpus have run 14, 34, 87 and 192 rows, and the row count tracks
document maturity almost perfectly. At design development the sheet is 14 rows, five of which are cost
options rather than scope, and no bidder returns it. At construction documents and permit stage it is
34 to 192 rows and bidders return it completed inside their own proposals. Target roughly eighty to
one hundred and fifty scope lines for a full multifamily plumbing package at construction documents.
Below about thirty rows the sheet stops distinguishing bidders, because the trade's whole variance
lives in seams and a thin sheet has no row for any of them.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Piping system, split by material and by area. Domestic cold water, domestic hot water, hot water
  recirculation, sanitary waste, vent, storm, overflow, condensate, laundry waste, greasy waste,
  indirect waste, trap primer, and gas each earn a row, and each splits again where the material
  changes between residential and commercial or between above and below ground. This is the axis every
  proposal adopts on its own, so scope written this way is scope bidders can answer.
- Every equipment mark on the schedule. Water booster, sewage ejector, storm ejector, elevator sump
  pumps, unit water heaters, common area water heaters, circulation pumps, mixing valves, pressure
  reducing valve stations, backflow preventers, interceptors and separators, storm filtration vaults.
  Estimator sheets key these by mark and by named manufacturer, because the leveling axis on equipment
  is product identity rather than quantity, and a substitution is worth real money on every one.
- Every fixture mark on the schedule, separately, with its count. Every estimator sheet at permit stage
  or later enumerates fixtures by mark, and the counts come from a bidder rather than from the sheet
  more often than not. Toilets and accessible toilets are separate marks with different products.
  Lavatories, lavatory faucets, tubs, tub trim, shower bases, shower trim, shower drains, kitchen
  sinks, kitchen faucets, garbage disposals, ice maker boxes, washing machine boxes, hose bibbs, wall
  hydrants, mop receptors, pet wash fixtures, water dispensers and specialty amenity fixtures each earn
  a row.
- The fixture schedule itself, as a row asking which one the bidder priced. See section 6; this is the
  most valuable single row on a plumbing scope sheet.
- Water meters, split three ways: main meter supply and install, submeter furnish, and submeter
  install. These three answers differ within a single bidder, and treating meters as one line loses the
  trade's most consistently contested seam. Add a fourth row for meter programming where the project
  has a data collection system, because it is assigned away separately from the meter itself.
- Insulation, broken by what is insulated: mains, risers, in-unit piping, tempered water, horizontal
  storm and cold waste including drain bodies, piping in exterior furred spaces and overhangs, piping
  collecting cold condensate, piping at top floor ceilings, acoustic lagging, plenum rated wrap, and
  direct buried pipe. Bidders answer these differently from each other and sometimes differently from
  themselves between a proposal and a scope sheet.
- Heat trace, split three ways: cable, installation, and the insulation over it. All three are assigned
  differently and the commissioning readings have to be taken after the insulation goes on, which makes
  it a sequencing row as well as a scope row.
- Drainage by element type: garage and trench drains, floor drains, floor sinks, roof drains, overflow
  drains, area drains, receptor drains, backwater valves, trap primers, and cleanouts. Split trench
  drains out separately because a bidder may carry a stated short length and exclude every other one.
- Pits, basins, vaults and their covers, as their own block separate from the equipment that sits in
  them. Elevator pits, ejector basins, separator vaults, storm filtration vaults, and the covers,
  hatches, ladders and grates that come with them.
- Appliance connections, one row each: dishwasher, refrigerator and ice maker, washing machine, garbage
  disposal, and gas range. These are answered inconsistently across bidders on the same project, with
  the washing machine connection the one most often answered no or unknown while the others are
  answered yes.
- Demolition and existing conditions on renovation work: drop and make safe, cut and cap, disconnect
  only versus removal from site, keeping an occupied tenant in service, and connection to existing
  service. On a renovation this block alone can carry an entire separate contract.
- Permits, inspections, utility fees, tap and access charges, and utility back charges, as separate
  rows. Permits are included by nearly everyone and excluded by the occasional bidder, and utility fees
  are excluded by nearly everyone.
- Coordination and modeling, layout from control points, as-builts, record model files, and attendance
  at meetings, as a block. A bidder may exclude the modeling and still attend the meetings.
- Testing, chlorination, disinfection, start-up, commissioning, training, operation manuals and
  closeout, as a block.
- Firestopping, split from fire caulking of others' penetrations and split again from cast-in-place
  pipe sleeves, because a bidder carrying firestopping may specifically exclude the sleeves.
- Access panels, as furnish and install separately. Nearly every bidder furnishes and does not install,
  and the access panel type differs by area on some projects.
- Temporary plumbing systems, temporary water and drains, and their construction period maintenance, as
  their own rows. Excluded by nearly every bidder and rarely assigned to anyone.
- The commercial block as scope rows: bond percentage, insurance basis, warranty term, escalation
  window, validity period, retainage schedule, storage window, tariff exposure, and any indemnity or
  waiver language the bidder attaches. See section 4 on why these belong on the sheet.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual pipe runs, fittings, hangers, supports, sleeves and escutcheons collapse into the piping
  system line. No document in the corpus prices these separately.
- Rooms and units collapse into unit type and area. Nobody prices a room.
- Levels collapse into a level range unless the piping system or the fixture group actually changes.
- Pipe size collapses into the system line, except where a bidder has stated a size band with a
  different material on each side of it, in which case the band is the enumeration.
- Individual valve, gauge and specialty counts collapse into the system they serve.
- Coat count equivalents do not exist in this trade; the analogue is joint method, and joint method
  collapses into the material line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In plumbing that
test fires hardest on seams rather than on surfaces, which is why section 5 is the long one. It also
fires on every item that has a furnish half and an install half, because in this trade those two
halves land with different parties more often than they land together.

Silence is a finding, and in this trade it is a stronger finding than in most. Because the market
default is that the plumber furnishes and installs, an item the plumber is silent about reads as
included, and a total that quietly excludes it looks competitive. The three-state problem is worse than
usual here: a line can be included, excluded, or listed at no value, and a no-value line reads as
included to anyone skimming. Heat trace shows up as a dash on a schedule of values on at least one
proposal precisely because it went to another trade.

The trade's structural gap list, meaning the plugs an estimator adds to every bidder before comparing:

1. Heat trace, in some combination of cable, installation and insulation.
2. Submeter furnishing, and meter programming where it exists.
3. Underslab and foundation drainage.
4. Underground pipe hangers, which specifications assign to the plumber and the market excludes.
5. Excavation, backfill and compaction, same pattern.
6. Concrete pits, basins, vaults, covers and grates at every pump, ejector and separator.
7. Utility tap fees, access charges and utility back charges.
8. Bond.
9. Engineered and stamped drawings, expansion loop design, and seismic calculations.
10. Temporary plumbing systems.
11. Shower pans and waterproofing below showers.
12. Bathroom accessory installation.

A scope line set that names all twelve explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging for it.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### Deviations first

In this trade the substitution disclosure outranks the exclusion list, because a plumbing number is
mostly product and the products are scheduled by manufacturer and model. Read the deviation paragraph
before anything else.

The forms it takes, in descending order of how often it is seen:

- A blanket reservation to provide equal products by alternative manufacturers meeting the same
  performance and aesthetic criteria, subject to submittal approval. Common, and it means the bid is
  not a specified-product bid at all.
- A per-category disclaimer: drains, carriers, hangers and specialties by a named manufacturer or other,
  not necessarily the one specified. Common in the matrix-form proposals, one line per category.
- Piping material stated as plastic where the documents call metal, sometimes disclosed plainly and
  sometimes buried in a materials bullet that reads like a description rather than a departure.
- A size or rating departure on a specific scheduled item, disclosed in a revision and nowhere else.
  Meter sizes and control panel enclosure ratings both show up this way.
- A fabrication method assumed to be acceptable to the engineer without confirmation.
- A quantity departure on engineering grounds: a bidder judging a scheduled quantity redundant and
  carrying fewer, citing the sheet and detail it read.
- A specification error found and corrected by the bidder. A scheduled accessible fixture that is not
  actually accessibility compliant, with a compliant substitute carried in the base, is the clearest
  case seen. When a bidder tells you the specified product does not meet the code the drawing invokes,
  that is a documents problem and it is worth an RFI, not a leveling note.
- A domestic-content exception, stated as a category not committed to full compliance.

The lesson underneath all of these: two plumbing numbers built on different product baskets are not
comparable, and the product basket is disclosed in the deviation paragraph, not in the scope list.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate. Repeat bidders reproduce the same exclusion list verbatim
across projects, and one bidder's standing qualification matrix is comparable line for line between
two projects, which confirms it carries no project information at all. Read boilerplate for the trade
map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- An exclusion citing a specific sheet, detail or schedule remark number. When a bidder excludes
  ejector basins by citing the remark number on each mark's schedule row, it read the schedule.
- An exclusion citing a plan note that assigns the item to somebody else. Submeters, garbage disposals
  and heat trace all get excluded this way, and the citation tells you the documents already made the
  call.
- An observation that scope referenced in one place does not exist in another: a fixture on a schedule
  with no floor plan location, an equipment quantity on a riser diagram the bidder judges redundant, a
  scheduled sink shown as drop-in in one document and undermount in another. Bidders find these and
  general contractor scope sheets often have not. Every one is an RFI.
- A statement about what the bidder looked for and did not find. A specialty vendor stating it searched
  the mechanical drawings for heat trace and found none is doing scope-gap work for you.
- A constraint on somebody else's work stated inside a plumbing proposal: a maximum point load per
  hanger, a required pit dimension at the base of each elevator group, a demand that control lines be
  set and the trench sanded to within an inch of pitch before underground layout. These are
  coordination requirements that will otherwise surface as a change order.

### Contract terms as scope

More than in most trades, a plumbing bidder's commercial addendum changes what its number is worth,
and several bidders make it a formal part of the offer. Watch for a bidder that requires its proposal
to be incorporated into any contract, requires to be indemnified rather than indemnifying, offers its
standard insurance limits rather than the specified limits, refuses the warranty flow-down language
that matches manufacturer warranties, will execute only lien waivers, dictates a retainage schedule,
reserves the right to bill delay and lost revenue, or excludes tariffs and reserves a price adjustment
for any future duty or surcharge. Tariff exclusions appear on most recent proposals and are effectively
an open price. Give these lines words on the scope sheet, because a bidder that has rewritten the risk
allocation has not bid the same job as one that has not.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

Violating one of these converts the lump sum into a change order.

- Working hours stated as a specific daily window, with everything outside it chargeable. Stated by
  most.
- Escalation held only through a stated date, or only if released to buy within a stated number of days.
  Common, and the window is often shorter than the procurement schedule.
- Validity of the offer stated in days, and shorter here than in most trades, sometimes ten.
- Free use of the general contractor's hoist, tower crane or freight elevator assumed. Stated by several,
  and it is a real cost if the assumption fails.
- Storage included for a stated number of months after equipment receipt, then chargeable per month per
  stated area.
- A single construction phase assumed, to limit mobilizations.
- Underground installation assumed to follow a mutually agreed sequence.
- A stated slab thickness assumed for all penetrations.
- Warranty stated as one year against a specification requiring two. Common enough that you should
  treat the warranty term as an open field on every bidder rather than as a given.
- A minimum number of dedicated temporary power circuits demanded for coring, with a stated generator
  rental add if not provided.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section that carries the trade. Plumbing scope variance is almost entirely seam variance,
and the drawings will tell you almost none of it.

### The three-way seams

Three items in this trade are routinely claimed by nobody, and each is claimed differently on each
project.

Heat trace. Expect at least four different answers on a single project. The plumbers may all exclude
it citing a plan note that assigns it to the electrical contractor, while the general contractor's
electrical scope sheet carries it as a plumbing item. One plumber may carry it in its base with a
stated value. A specialty thermal vendor may bid it directly, and that vendor may bid material only on
one project and supply-and-install on another, so the vendor's mode has to be read off each quote
rather than assumed. A plumber may carry the wire only with terminations by others. And a plumber may
include heat trace in one location and exclude it in another on adjacent lines of the same document,
distinguished by a single location qualifier. Whatever the answer, the insulation over the cable is a
separate question again, and the vendor's commissioning readings must be taken after the insulation is
installed, which makes this a sequencing dependency between two or three parties. Split heat trace into
cable, installation and insulation as three rows and ask every bidder all three.

Shower pans and waterproofing below showers. Contested three ways between the plumber, the tile setter
and a cast marble or specialty pan supplier, and the corpus shows it unresolved in writing. On one
project the general contractor asked bidders to break out a shower pan price precisely because which
trade would carry it was still to be determined, and a bidder answered with a price per shower base
mark and per count. On another the pan is furnished by a cast marble supplier with no walls, and the
plumber connects to it. Some bidders exclude copper shower pans and waterproofing below showers by
name. Give the pan its own row, give the waterproofing its own row, and give the surround a third,
because the pan, the membrane and the tile wall land with three different parties.

Submeters. The furnish half and the install half separate almost universally. Nearly every bidder
installs, most exclude furnishing, and the add to furnish differs across bidders on the same project
by more than four times, which is a wider spread than anything else in the trade. Where the project has
a meter data collection system, programming is assigned away separately again. Where a plan note says
meters are furnished by others, bidders cite the note and the note is the answer. Where the
specification calls for meters and no note exists, expect nobody to have carried them.

### Fixture seams

The plumber's default is furnish and install. Every departure from that default is worth a row.

- Kitchen sinks and lavatory bowls, furnish only. Seen on more than one project, stated in exactly those
  words. The installation follows the countertop, so a furnish-only sink is set by whoever fabricates or
  installs the counter.
- Undermount sinks, install excluded while furnish is included. The mirror image of the above, and both
  arrangements appear in the same market.
- Countertops, vanities and integral bowls, excluded by the plumber. The sink and the counter are one
  assembly and two trades.
- The sink dimension is constrained by the countertop cutout. A fixture substitution that saves money
  can fail because its interior dimension does not match the opening the fabricator has already
  templated. Treat a fixture substitution as a cross-trade change, not a plumbing change.
- Garbage disposals, furnished by the appliance vendor and installed by the plumber. The most common
  arrangement. A plumber may cite the fixture schedule notes by mark to prove it. The furnish half is
  worth breaking out because a general contractor may want it moved into the appliance allowance
  entirely, which takes it out of this trade's number.
- Appliance connections stop at the appliance. The plumber runs the water line and connects the ice
  maker at the refrigerator's connection, sets and fastens the dishwasher to the counter or cabinet, and
  provides the washing machine box. It does not unbox, place or mount the appliance, and on some
  projects it does not connect the washing machine at all. Anti-tip devices are excluded.
- Owner-supplied equipment, in two distinct variants that are separately excluded: install and start-up
  only, and receive, store, deliver, install and start-up. Ask which one the project intends.
- Bathroom accessories. Most plumbers exclude toilet accessories outright. One carries installation of
  the whole accessory package including grab bars, mirrors, paper holders and shower poles. Do not
  assume either way.
- Blocking for fixture and accessory support. Excluded by some, and installed by one who has it furnished
  loose by the carpenter, with a stated fallback of cutting its own from the carpenter's material. The
  blocking has to be in before the wall closes, so this is a sequencing seam as well.
- Shower and tub surrounds, one-piece and tiled. Excluded by nearly everyone. Shower doors excluded.
  Shimming of shower bases for lightweight concrete floors by others.
- Finish caulking at tubs, showers and toilets. Carried by most as caulking of all fixtures, excluded
  outright by at least one. A single-word difference between two proposals.

### Equipment and structure seams

- The pump is the plumber's and the pit is not. Elevator sump pumps, sewage ejectors and storm ejectors
  are nearly always carried while the concrete basin, the precast structure, the cover, the grate and
  the access hatch are nearly always excluded. On elevator pits a bidder may state the exact pit
  dimension it needs formed at the base of each elevator group, which is a requirement on the concrete
  trade issued through a plumbing proposal.
- Separator and filtration vaults decompose further. One bidder itemizes the vault seam component by
  component: excavation and setting, the precast vault, the internal separation components furnished by
  the vault's own supplier, the covers and ladders that come with the vault, and the slab or paving over
  it, keeping only the piping connections. Every one of those is a potential gap.
- Housekeeping pads and inertia bases, excluded by most, and a bidder may still count how many pads it
  needs and tell you.
- Roof curbs under rooftop water heating equipment. A plumber may furnish and install the curb while
  excluding the structural steel under it.
- Supplemental steel and dunnage for ceiling-hung equipment, excluded by most.
- Electrical wiring of everything plumbing furnishes. Universally excluded, and the list of what needs
  wiring is longer than the drawings suggest: float switches, leak detection shutoffs, pump controls,
  aquastats, booster pump controls, control panels and building management communication. A plumbing
  equipment schedule that does not enumerate its own control points hands the gap straight to the
  electrical package.
- Central hot water plant and booster packages arrive from manufacturer representatives with their own
  exclusion lists: no piping, no valves unless listed, inertia base by others, thermal purge valves and
  bypass by others, start-up priced separately with warranty preservation stated to depend on it, and
  covers marked non-returnable so a late schedule change is a real cost. Lead times on these are the
  long poles in the trade, quoted from a couple of months to most of a year from approved submittals.

### Adjacent-trade overlaps

- HVAC. Condensate is the boundary and it is drawn on both disciplines' sheets. Several bidders exclude
  condensate piping from HVAC equipment unless it is shown on the plumbing drawings, which makes the
  answer depend on which engineer drew it. On at least one project the piping appeared only on a
  mechanical sheet and the plumber therefore excluded it. Hot water supply and return for HVAC equipment
  is another: the plumber provides the outlet only. Flue and intake piping is excluded. And where the
  same bidder is bidding both plumbing and HVAC, expect a deduct for being awarded both, quoted by
  multiple bidders at values differing by more than two and a half times.
- Electrical, beyond wiring. Heat trace panel mounting, transformers and all conduit belong to
  electrical even when a specialty vendor supplies the cable.
- Site and civil. The water service pick-up point is the seam and it moves: some bidders stop at a blind
  flange inside the building, some at the interior face of the foundation, some at five feet out, some
  at ten feet out. Domestic water service, sewer service, gas main extension and their permit fees each
  have their own distance, and the fees split between the plumber's permit and the owner's access
  charges. Trench drains shown on architectural or civil sheets rather than plumbing sheets are
  routinely excluded by the plumber and belong to nobody.
- Concrete. Every pit, basin, pad and encasement, plus the coring the plumber will not do through
  structural elements.
- Tile. Shower pans, waterproofing, surrounds, and the linear drain that a tile-integrated shower base
  brings with it.
- Casework and countertops. Sinks, cutouts, and undermount installation.
- Roofing. Every penetration, its flashing, and the scuppers and downspouts that sit between the storm
  system and the cladding.
- Fire protection. A separate trade with a clean boundary, except that heat trace and firestopping both
  cross it, and a specialty vendor may quote both divisions' piping in one document.
- Insulation. Usually the plumber's, occasionally sub-subcontracted by the plumber, and worth knowing
  which because a sub-subcontracted line prices differently.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

### The fixture schedule problem

This is the trade's defining documents hazard and it deserves to lead.

Fixtures are scheduled in more than one place, and the schedules disagree. On a single project you may
find the plumbing fixture schedule at its original issue, the same schedule at a later issue with
different products against the same marks, an architectural finish or unit schedule listing the same
fixtures again, and an owner-issued fixture schedule listing them a third time. The same mark carries a
different manufacturer and model in each. A leveling sheet that handles this properly enumerates all
three schedules in full and asks each bidder which one it priced, and the bidders do not all answer the
same one. On another project two adjacent scope rows ask separately whether the bidder priced fixtures
per the plumbing schedule and per the architectural schedule, and record for one bidder that it appears
not to have seen the second at all.

Two consequences. First, a plumbing scope line for fixtures must name the governing schedule by
document and by date, not just by mark. Second, if a set contains two fixture schedules that disagree,
that is a documents problem to surface before bidding, not a leveling problem to solve after.

### What plumbers actually price from, in descending order of what they cite

1. The plumbing drawing series, cited by number range and date, and occasionally by an exact sheet
   count. This is the primary document and nearly every proposal names it.
2. The fixture and equipment schedules, by mark. This is where the money is and where the ambiguity is.
3. Plan keynotes and scope notes on the cover sheet. Several of the trade's largest seams are settled by
   a single note, and bidders cite the note number: heat trace by others, submeters by others, garbage
   disposals by others. A note that assigns scope away is worth more than a paragraph of specification.
4. Schedule remarks columns. Bidders exclude items by citing the remark number attached to a specific
   equipment mark.
5. Riser diagrams and details, for expansion joints, sleeve details, water entrance details, drain pan
   details and thrust joints.
6. The geotechnical drawings, for underslab and foundation drainage.
7. The specification, cited less than half the time and usually only to dispute something or to name a
   general-requirements clause.
8. The unit count, as the leveling denominator. Bidders state it as the basis of their number more often
   in this trade than in most, and every estimator sheet normalizes every bid to cost per unit and ranks
   on it. If the set does not make the unit count unambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable.

They do not price from a measured takeoff. Counts by fixture mark appear rarely. The normal method is
system and area and equipment schedule plus unit count plus judgment.

### Not deducible from any drawing

Each of these moves the number, and none of them is on a sheet.

- Union versus open shop labor. The largest single driver by an enormous margin. On one project a bidder
  quoted a deduct for open shop in lieu of prevailing wage worth about fifteen percent of its own base.
  Across every project in the corpus the union bidders occupy the top of the cost-per-unit ranking and
  the open shop bidders the bottom, with essentially no overlap. Where two bidders on the same project
  both state labor hours, the hour counts sit within about ten percent of each other while the prices
  differ by half again as much, which puts the difference in rate rather than in content.
- Prevailing wage applicability, stated separately from union status and usually as a negative.
- The insurance basis. Whether an owner-controlled program applies changes the base bid, and the
  conventional general liability alternate is quoted by most bidders at a percentage of contract value.
- Bond, and whether it must cover through the warranty period.
- The warranty term the specification actually requires, and whether the manufacturer-match flow-down
  language will be enforced. Multiple bidders refuse that language explicitly while carrying a two year
  labor and material warranty, and several carry only one year against a two year requirement.
- Whether the local authority having jurisdiction will accept plastic piping. This is the single largest
  material swing in the trade and it is not an engineer's decision. Bidders quote copper-throughout and
  cast-iron-throughout adds against a plastic base precisely because the answer is unknown at bid time,
  and on a renovation the substitution may need approval from a state board rather than from the design
  team. Any scope line naming a piping material has to say who decides and by when.
- Whether a substitute fixture holds state plumbing product approval. A substitution can be killed
  outright on this ground, and it is invisible on drawings and in specifications alike.
- Whether the specified accessible fixtures are actually compliant. Assume nothing; a bidder found a
  scheduled accessible fixture that was not.
- The tariff and commodity environment. Most recent proposals exclude future tariffs and reserve a price
  adjustment, so a lump sum in this trade is conditionally lump sum.
- The escalation window, which is frequently shorter than the time between bid and award.
- Site access, staging, storage, parking, and whether the hoist, tower crane or freight elevator is free
  to the plumber.
- The construction schedule and phasing. A bidder may decline to price demolition on the stated ground
  that no schedule was issued.
- On renovation: whether an existing tenant stays operational, how work will be handled in the occupied
  space, whether live vents must remain until new ones are in, and where an adjacent tenant's own
  systems intersect the project. These questions appear on estimator scope sheets addressed to the
  estimator itself, unanswered, which is a fair indication of how hard they are.
- Whether the general contractor intends to consolidate coordination modeling under one entity, which
  turns a large plumbing line into a credit.

### Set-stage behavior

At design development the trade is barely bid. Scope sheets run a handful of rows, most of them cost
options rather than scope. No bidder returns the sheet. Proposals state fixture types as assumptions
rather than reading marks, and a bidder's stated scope and its priced scope are uncorrelated: on one
design development round the bidder with the thinnest scope statement carried the highest number among
its peers. The estimator's own budget at this stage is built from quantity rules of thumb, an area rate
for the parking and basement plus a per-unit rate for the residential portion, and that budget can sit
below every actual bidder.

By permit stage and construction documents the picture inverts. Scope sheets grow by an order of
magnitude, bidders return them completed, fixtures are answered mark by mark, and the value engineering
lists become itemized by sheet and by mark. Treat a design development plumbing number as a rate times
a count, and a construction document plumbing number as a scope answer.

Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its folder's set stage frequently disagree. Bids priced
off a drawing issue months older than the round they are filed in are common, and at least one proposal
carries a letterhead date months earlier than the drawings it cites, which is a template carry-over. The
document's own date cannot be trusted to order the rounds; the cited basis document can.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Units, meaning dwelling units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator
  sheet, without exception. This is the leveling currency of the trade.
- Each, by fixture and equipment mark. The counts come from a bidder more often than from the drawings,
  and a bidder that enumerates every mark with a count while pricing one lump sum has given the round
  its denominator whether or not it wins.
- Linear feet, for heat trace cable and for trench drain. Both are quoted as a stated length carried
  with everything beyond it excluded, so the length is the scope.
- Square feet, for area-based budget rates at early stage on parking and basement space. An estimator
  convention, not a bidder one.
- Hours, for projected manhours on the package. Stated by a minority of bidders and recorded by
  estimators where available. Manhours per dwelling unit runs around one hundred and fifty on a
  mid-rise multifamily building.
- Hourly rates by classification, foreman, journeyman and apprentice. Published by a minority, and the
  spread between the top and bottom classification runs about a quarter.
- Percent of contract value, for bond and for conventional general liability insurance. Bond runs
  roughly one to two percent.
- Weeks and months, for submittal turnaround, fabrication and equipment lead time. Long lead equipment
  in this trade is measured in months, not weeks, and the central hot water plant is the pole.
- Months, for the free equipment storage window, and a monthly rate per stated floor area beyond it.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Labor type, union versus open shop or prevailing wage versus not. Larger than everything below it
   combined. A deduct for open shop in lieu of prevailing wage has been quoted at about fifteen percent
   of a base bid, and across projects the union-to-open-shop cost-per-unit gap runs between about one
   and a half and more than two times.
2. Piping material, and whether the authority having jurisdiction will accept plastic. Copper-throughout
   and cast-iron-throughout adds against a plastic base have each been quoted at roughly a tenth of a
   whole package.
3. The central hot water plant and its manufacturer. Alternate plant deducts run to several percent of
   a package, and one bidder volunteered unprompted that the plant alone was worth about an eighth of
   its whole number. Beware that a plant substitution deduct quoted at bid time can shrink by a third
   or more once the submittal reveals the alternate needs more heat pumps, exchangers, sensors and
   wiring, and lacks components the specified plant includes.
4. The fixture package. Worth roughly a tenth to a quarter of the whole number, and bidders' own stated
   values for the same fixture scope on the same project differ by more than two times. Fixture
   substitution savings are real but per-mark: no single manufacturer wins across a whole schedule, so
   a manufacturer-level substitution cannot be evaluated as one decision.
5. Unit count and the mix of amenity, commercial and back of house space against residential.
6. Whether submeters are furnished, and by whom.
7. Whether in-unit hot water return lines and circulation pumps exist. Eliminating them has been quoted
   at a few percent of a package.
8. Leak detection and flood protection systems at water heaters and laundries. Deleting or downgrading
   them has been quoted at a couple of percent.
9. Insulation extent, especially whether in-unit piping and plastic cold water piping are insulated.
10. Firestopping and coordination modeling, both of which behave as detachable blocks.
11. Heat trace.
12. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.
13. Escalation and tariff exposure, which is genuinely open on most current proposals.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per unit has been seen at about 1.46, 1.61, 1.62 and 2.23 times, with the
widest spread on the earliest-stage set and the tightest on the most mature. Bidder counts per project
run from six to fourteen. The top of a plumbing range is nearly always a union bidder on an open shop
board rather than a bidder who has priced more scope, and the scope-narrowest bidder in a round is
sometimes the most expensive.

One further convention, and it is the most important one in the section. Bidders agree on the total far
more than they agree on what is in it. On a project where the totals spread by a little over half,
component values recorded against the same schedule of values rows spread by more than two times on
fixtures, nearly half again on the central plant, and more than three times on both the drainage line
and the insulation line. Do not read a component value off one bidder as a market price. The
convergence is on the package, not on the parts.

And as in every trade, an estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus adjustments,
where adjustments are the plugs listed at the end of section 3 plus each bidder's alternates and value
engineering. Two further wrinkles specific to this trade: an estimator may aggregate substitution ideas
across the whole bid list and offer the winner somebody else's value engineering, so the leveling sheet
becomes a shared idea pool; and the number the sheet ranks may be lower than any number in the winning
bidder's own documents, because it is tracking a post-interview figure that exists nowhere else.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly thirty plumbing-package proposals, several equipment supplier and
fixture distributor quotes, two specialty heat trace quotes, and four estimator leveling sheets, drawn
from four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor.
The proposals span design development, permit, construction documents and conformed sets. One project
is a renovation with an occupied commercial tenant remaining in service; the other three are new
construction. Several bidders appear on more than one project, which is what makes the
boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable, and one appears under three
different name tokens across the filing.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back of house space, in one
  market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors, institutional,
  healthcare, laboratory, industrial or public prevailing-wage work. Medical gas appears once, as a
  single excluded certification line, and nothing here describes how that scope is actually packaged.
- Only one project is a renovation, so the demolition, make-safe and occupied-tenant material rests on
  that project alone.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation and which were bought out. Where a leveling sheet carries a number lower than any
  proposal, the negotiation that produced it is not visible.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the working-hours, sequencing and access
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed in the field.
- Gas is thin. One project has substantial gas scope, one has none, and the rest sit between, so the gas
  boundary described in section 1 rests on a small base. Nothing here covers gas booster systems,
  medium-pressure service, or how gas is packaged where it is a large scope in its own right.
- The corpus has not shown a project where fixtures were bid as a genuinely separate package with its own
  bid list. The fixture distributor quotes present are price checks taken by the general contractor, not
  competing bids, so the fully separated fixture package is described here by inference.
- The corpus has not shown irrigation, pool or water feature systems bid as plumbing scope, only excluded
  from it, and it does not reveal where they are actually bought.
- The corpus has not shown rainwater harvesting or greywater reuse carried by anyone; it appears only as
  an exclusion, and on one project as a storm filtration vault whose ownership is unresolved.
- The shower pan seam is documented here as open because the corpus caught it open. No project in the
  corpus shows it resolved, so this entry can say who claims it and cannot say who ends up with it.
