# Support of excavation

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the temporary support
of excavation package, including underpinning, bracing design and the monitoring that goes with them.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The entry
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The one thing to carry in: this is a design-build package
priced against assumptions the drawings do not contain. The bidder chooses the method, the extent,
the depth and whether the wall is braced, and then prices its own choice. A scope line that names an
excavation and asks for a number is not a scope line here.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The package is bid under a division 31 earthwork section, usually an excavation support and
protection specification, and lands under a division 31 tab on the estimator's board. It travels
under several trade tokens: support of excavation, shoring, underpinning, bracing design and earth
retention. They are one family and the same bidders answer all of them.

The package boundary is unstable across projects, and this is the first thing to establish. On one
project support of excavation had its own leveling tab with its own bidders and its own denominator.
On another it was a requested breakout row inside the earthwork tab, alongside excavation and
dewatering, civil site work and asphalt, with the instruction to plug the breakouts into the
alternates section. Same trade, same market, two package shapes. A rate carried across projects
without checking which shape it came from is measuring different things.

What sits inside the package as bid:

- The retention wall itself: piles or minipiles or micropiles, plus lagging.
- Internal bracing, where the bidder's design needs it. Walers, corner braces and rakers.
- The PE-stamped design submittal. Every bidder carries its own, which is one reason a separately
  bought bracing design can duplicate work already inside the wall price.
- Mobilization, priced as a countable event.
- Sometimes the safety railing at the top of the wall. Sometimes an add, sometimes in the base,
  never assumed.

What is bought separately, and from whom:

- Underpinning of an abutting structure. Usually the same bidder, on a companion proposal, and priced
  on the assumption that it shares the support of excavation mobilization. Buying it from someone
  else loses that assumption and adds a whole mobilization.
- Geotechnical instrumentation and monitoring, and the pre-construction condition survey. A
  consultant buy, not a subcontractor buy, and it is what makes every retention bidder's shared
  exclusion of monitoring of existing structures survivable.
- Bracing design as a standalone consultant engagement, seen once and worth reconciling against the
  bidders' own design submittals before paying for both.
- Ground improvement, which one bidder offered alongside its wall as a separate duration and a
  separate number.
- Dewatering. Excluded by every retention bidder seen.

## 2. How the market bids

The proposal is long, narrative and dense with conditions. Three to nine pages is normal and most of
it is not price.

The shape is consistent:

- A scope narrative naming the chosen method and stating every governing quantity as an assumption.
  The phrase to look for is "up to". Length up to, depth up to, exposed face up to, surcharge assumed
  at, deflection limited to.
- A short price block, typically three lines: design submittal as a lump sum, mobilization priced as
  each, and the wall itself as a lump sum.
- An add and deduct block of unit prices that govern outside the assumed envelope: additional lagging
  per square foot, additional piles per each, additional mobilization per each, reduced scope after
  mobilization.
- An exclusions list of twenty or more items, most of which assign real work to the earthwork
  contractor.
- A conditions block carrying an hourly rate card: extended hours per hour per crew, weekend shift
  per crew, delay and standby per hour per crew, obstruction drilling per hour per crew.

That structure is the trade's defining instrument, and it should be read as one thing rather than
three. The lump sum is a price for a stated quantity envelope, and the unit prices are what happens
in either direction outside it. A lump sum captured without its envelope is not a price. A bidder
assuming a shorter wall and a shallower cut will always look cheaper and is not.

What a proposal will state:

- The method, named precisely: drilled micropiles or minipiles with timber lagging, drilled soldier
  pile and lagging, driven soldier pile with pre-augering, and so on. Always stated.
- The assumed length, in plan view linear feet.
- The assumed exposed face, in square feet, or enough to derive it.
- The assumed maximum or range of cut depth.
- Whether internal bracing is included, and how many levels.
- The assumed surcharge, and whether building or construction surcharges were considered at all.
- The deflection limit, and whether it came from the specification or the bidder's judgement.
- Durations, by operation, in working days or weeks, and whether they are concurrent.

What a proposal will not state, unless asked:

- How the square footage was computed. On one project the estimator proposed a figure from length
  times maximum cut and the bidder corrected it to an average cut depth, by email, after the bid.
  Ask every time, and pin the answer to the round.
- Whether the assumed extent covers the whole excavation. One bid covered a single corner of the
  building while competitors priced the full perimeter, and nothing on the face of the proposal
  flagged it.
- What happens where a neighbouring structure could substitute for the wall. See section 6.

Revision habits. Round pairs in this trade tend to be near-identical documents that reprice silently:
two files extracting to within a hundred characters of each other with different numbers. Diff the
scope sentences and the assumed quantities, not the totals, because the quantity reduction ladder is
the live mechanism here. A bid that wins by assuming a shorter wall and a shallower cut is winning on
a design decision, not on production.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Leveling sheets in this trade have run around twenty five rows, and unusually many of them are
questions rather than items. Target twenty to thirty lines and accept that half of them will be
interrogation.

### What enumerates

- The method, as a row that the bidder fills in. This is the single most valuable row on the sheet
  because it is the axis every other quantity depends on.
- Whether bracing is needed, as a row, with room for the bidder's explanation. On one sheet the
  answer recorded that the system has internal bracing and that it interferes with the concrete work,
  which is a coordination fact worth more than the price beside it.
- Why the bidder chose this method, as free text. Answers naming high rock and construction surcharge
  are scope-defining information no drawing carries.
- Linear feet of retention, in plan view.
- Square feet of exposed face, with the derivation asked for explicitly.
- Cut depth, as a maximum and as a range.
- Assumed surcharge, split between traffic or vehicle surcharge and construction or building
  surcharge. Bidders differ on whether either was considered at all.
- Deflection limit, and whose it is.
- Design submittal and PE stamp, its own line.
- Mobilization count, its own line, with the per-mobilization rate captured.
- Internal bracing, split into setting and removal, because a bidder may include one and not the
  other and because removal is a second mobilization.
- Pile cut-off, its own line, with the elevation and who provides it.
- Safety railing at the top of the wall, its own line, and see section 5 before assuming what it is.
- Whether the wall is abandoned in place or removed, its own line, with removal priced.
- Underpinning, as its own block: length, height, depth, volume, and whether it shares the retention
  mobilization.
- Monitoring and pre-construction survey, as their own block, since they are a different buy.
- Durations by operation, as rows: submittal, mobilization, pile installation, bracing installation,
  lagging installation, and which of them overlap.

### What collapses

- Individual pile locations. The count is derived from the length and the spacing and no bidder
  prices by location.
- Lagging board detail, species and treatment. An attribute, though see section 4 for what the
  attribute discloses.
- Grouting, drilling spoils and casing as separate operations. They are inside the wall price
  everywhere, and their handling is an exclusion rather than a line.
- The rate card. Capture it wholesale as a block rather than as rows.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

Almost nothing here is a genuine include-or-exclude question, because the bidders all carry the same
physical work and exclude the same surrounding work. The test that actually discriminates is a
different one: could two reasonable bidders assume different quantities for this item, and would the
difference be invisible at the total. If yes, it gets a row with the bidder's own number in it. That
is why this trade's sheet is a quantity questionnaire rather than a scope checklist.

### The structural gap list

The plugs to expect on every number:

1. All excavation, including excavating just past the pile face so the lagging can be placed flush,
   and excavating trenches and heel blocks for rakers. Excluded by every bidder seen.
2. Containment, collection and disposal of drilling and grouting spoils, continuously. Excluded by
   every bidder seen, and it is a continuous obligation rather than a one-time cost.
3. Dewatering. Excluded universally.
4. Survey and layout, including stakes at every pile location. Excluded universally.
5. Manned hoisting to set and hold walers and bracing sections. Excluded, and it means a crane and an
   operator standing by inside someone else's package.
6. A level, stable working bench of a stated width along the whole alignment, plus access built and
   maintained for a tracked rig of stated dimensions and weight. Excluded, and it is real earthwork.
7. Preconstruction survey, monitoring, and protection of adjacent structures including against the
   bidder's own vibration. Excluded by every retention bidder and only partly picked up by the
   monitoring consultant, who measures but does not protect.
8. Utility locating, relocation and de-energizing, and removal of obstructions. Excluded, and
   obstruction drilling is separately charged by the hour on top.
9. Traffic control, police detail and barricades.
10. Wall removal and pile cutoffs, where they are not in the base.
11. The safety railing at the excavation edge, where it is an add.
12. Testing and inspection of the installed work.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

The exclusion lists here are long, near-identical across bidders, and almost entirely a map of what
the earthwork contractor must do. Read them for the interface, not for the project.

The recurring exclusions and their counterparty are the gap list in section 3. What is worth
separating out is the qualification set, because in this trade the qualifications are the scope.

The quantity envelope. Every governing number is prefaced with an assumption. When the wall turns
out longer, deeper or larger in face area, the unit prices govern and the lump sum was never the
price. Capture the envelope with the number or the number means nothing.

The design criteria the bidder chose. Surcharge, deflection limit and bracing are all bidder
decisions dressed as facts. One bidder assumed a vehicle surcharge across the entire system; another
stated that no building surcharges were taken into consideration at all and set a minimum standoff
distance for construction loads. One bidder accepted the specification's deflection limit and, in the
same paragraph, reserved allowable deflection to its own engineering judgement and design submittal,
which converts a project requirement into a bidder variable. Read the deflection clause carefully:
downstream packages assume the wall holds its line, and the concrete package inherits the consequence
when it does not.

Continuity. Every bidder prices continuous, uninterrupted work under a single mobilization on
regular day shifts, and every bidder attaches a delay rate to the assumption. The lagging crew works
inside the earthwork contractor's excavation sequence, so the two crews are coupled and neither
controls the pace. One bidder states outright that a break between the pile phase and the lagging
phase could add cost. This is a schedule risk priced by the hour and it belongs on the scope line.

Material and workmanship disclosures that read as boilerplate and are not. Pile casing quoted as
non-domestic new mill secondary pipe with no mill test certificates is a grade floor, not a
formality, and two proposals from one bidder carry it verbatim on two projects. Untreated rough sawn
timber lagging that will naturally decay over time is a statement about a permanent condition behind
a permanent wall.

Commercial terms that change what the number is worth. One bidder rejects pay-if-paid and any
contingent payment clause explicitly, and requires no retainage be withheld. Another excludes
liquidated and consequential damages. Bond is offered as a percentage of contract value. These
materially change the risk position and no leveling column captures them.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

Excavation. The retention contractor installs the piles, then lags progressively as the earthwork
contractor excavates in lifts, then installs bracing when the cut reaches a couple of feet below each
support level, then lags on to subgrade. Two crews, interleaved, in one hole, with neither owning the
sequence. Every retention bidder excludes all excavation and every one attaches a delay rate to the
interleaving. This is the highest-friction seam in the package and it should be written as a
sequencing note on the scope line, not as a boundary.

Internal bracing and the concrete package. Where the design includes internal bracing, walers,
corner braces and rakers occupy the excavation for as long as the foundation walls are being built.
The earthwork entry records this seam from its side, and an estimator's own note on one leveling sheet
records that the bracing interferes with the concrete work. It changes formwork access, placement
sequence and often the wall pour strategy, and it is a cost that lands in another package with no
line item anywhere naming it. The waterproofing entry records the matching consequence at the
membrane: a pre-applied membrane run against the retention wall, and form ties as the leak path.

The one-sided wall. Where the retention wall is close enough to serve as one face of the form, the
concrete package absorbs an assumption it never saw. This is the assumption pair to hunt for: two
packages each relying on the other's behavior, with no document connecting them.

Voids behind lagging. One bidder assumes the site contractor will help backpack lagging boards if
the soil sloughs. Nobody prices it.

Spoils. Drilling and grouting spoils are a continuous stream requiring pits or tanks, pumping and
disposal, all by others. The earthwork or demolition package carries it and the disposal
classification question belongs there.

The safety railing. The rail at the top of the wall is furnished and installed by the retention
contractor when it is bought, and two bidders state explicitly that the cable rail is not designed to
be tied off to by workers and that maintaining it after installation is by others. So it is a
guardrail and not an anchorage, and treating it as fall protection is an error the proposals
anticipate and disclaim in advance. On projects where it is an add rather than a base item, OSHA
compliance at the excavation edge is unassigned by default.

Underpinning. Priced on the assumption that it runs in conjunction with the retention mobilization,
by the same crew. Its own seams are the preconstruction baseline survey by others, groundwater held
below the lowest level of underpinning by others, spoils stockpiled for disposal by others, and
periodic excavation support by others for the approach pits.

Monitoring. The consultant installs and initializes instruments and crack gauges and runs a
reporting platform. Reading the gauges afterwards and removing them is by others, and protection of
the structures being monitored is excluded by everyone. So monitoring proves damage happened and
nobody is contracted to prevent it.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

The drawings show an excavation. They do not show a retention system, and on the evidence here they
often cannot.

What bidders price from:

1. The geotechnical engineering report. Named by nearly every bidder and by the monitoring consultant,
   and on one project it is what required the piles to be drilled rather than driven, which turned a
   bidder choice into a document requirement.
2. The excavation support and protection specification section, mainly for the deflection limit.
3. The drawing set, for the excavation footprint and the depth, and for the structural sheets keying
   bracing of an existing wall and underpinning of an abutting wall.
4. A site visit. One bidder's method rationale names high rock and a construction surcharge, neither
   of which is on a drawing.

Not deducible from any drawing:

- The extent. On one excavation, with one document set, bidders assumed retention lengths ranging
  from roughly a hundred and fifty to roughly four hundred and fifty linear feet, a factor of three.
  Exposed face ranged from about two thousand to about five and a half thousand square feet. Cut
  depth ranged from ten to fifteen feet. None of that divergence is visible in a total.
- Whether a neighbour's existing foundation wall substitutes for the retention system. One bidder
  assumed exactly that over roughly a third of its own perimeter, and priced internal bracing to hold
  the existing wall instead of building a new one. That is the single largest silent assumption in
  the corpus and it is invisible unless the scope narrative is read.
- Whether the wall is braced. The same bidder assumed one level of internal bracing on one project
  and none on another. Two bidders on one excavation split on it. Bracing is a design decision, not
  a site fact, and it propagates into the concrete package.
- Whether building and construction surcharges are in the design. Explicitly excluded by one
  bidder and explicitly included by another on comparable sites.
- Whether the wall is abandoned in place or removed, and whether the piles are cut off and at what
  elevation.
- Whether the underpinning scope exists at all. On one project the condition of the wall to be
  underpinned had not been provided, so the bidder wrote that the requirements, if any, were
  currently undefined, and priced a budget envelope anyway. That is the honest form of an unpriceable
  scope and the scope line should carry it as a stated envelope, not as a number.
- Obstructions. Excluded by everyone, charged by the hour, and unknowable in advance. Penetration
  rates are described as very unpredictable in the bidders' own words.
- Site access for a tracked rig of stated dimensions and weight, laydown area, water at a stated flow
  and pressure per rig, and three-phase power.
- Whether the work is union or open shop, which the leveling sheet tracks for every bidder.

Set stage behavior. Every proposal in this corpus is a design development or permit round document,
and each one describes itself as budgetary in effect if not in word. This trade prices early because
it is on the critical path, and it prices on assumptions because the information to do otherwise does
not exist yet. Expect the assumptions to be the deliverable.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Linear feet of retention, in plan view. The primary extent measure and the one bidders state.
- Square feet of exposed face or lagging. The derived measure, and the one estimators normalize on.
  Its derivation is contested; see below.
- Feet of cut depth, as a maximum and as a range.
- Pounds per square foot, for surcharge.
- Inches, for the deflection limit.
- Each, for mobilizations and for additional piles.
- Cubic yards, for underpinning volume.
- Working days or weeks, by operation, with concurrency stated.
- Per hour per crew, for delay, standby, obstruction drilling and extended hours.
- Percent of contract value, for bond.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. The assumed extent, which is the bidder's own design decision.
2. The method, which follows from the geotechnical conditions and the bidder's fleet.
3. Whether internal bracing is in the design, and how many levels.
4. Cut depth and the surcharge assumed against it.
5. Whether an adjacent existing structure is assumed to carry part of the retention.
6. Continuity of work, priced through the delay rate and the mobilization count.
7. Obstructions, priced by the hour.
8. Whether the wall is removed or abandoned.
9. Union or open shop.
10. Bond, and the commercial terms the bidder refuses.

On spread, and what it does not prove. On one excavation the ranked totals spanned roughly 3.75
times from the general contractor's own budget to the highest bid, and about 2.7 times between the
two real bids. That spread is not a market. It is one bidder pricing the full perimeter segmented
into three building zones, another pricing a shorter wall, and a general contractor budget computed
off a square footage roughly 1.8 times the selected bidder's own stated lagging area for essentially
the same wall length. Three columns, three different walls. The general contractor's budget was the
low column and would have won the ranking, which is the clearest possible sign that the low end of a
ranking in this trade is not a purchasable number.

The mirror also holds. Convergence proves nothing either: two bidders can agree on a total while
assuming different methods, different bracing and different depths, because the compensating
variables run in opposite directions. Normalize the extent, the depth and the bracing before
comparing anything, and treat any leveling sheet without those three rows as unable to distinguish
its bidders.

One denominator caution. Estimators normalize this package on whatever the project's overall
denominator is, which on one sheet was cost per residential unit and on another was cost per site
square foot. Neither has any physical relationship to retained wall face. A rate lifted from one of
those sheets and applied to another project is meaningless unless the denominator is re-derived from
the wall itself.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly a dozen distinct proposals covering support of excavation,
underpinning, shoring, bracing design and geotechnical monitoring, from seven or eight bidders, plus
one dedicated estimator leveling tab and one earthwork tab where the scope appears as a breakout row.
The material comes from two projects in one regional market, both bid by one general contractor: an
urban infill mid-rise excavating against streets and an abutting property, and a smaller site where
rock is high. The proposals span design development and permit rounds.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Only one project produced a dedicated leveling tab, so everything in section 7 about denominators,
  ranking behavior and the general contractor budget column rests on that one sheet.
- Every proposal here is a pre-construction document. Nothing shows what the wall actually cost, what
  the obstruction hours came to, how many mobilizations were really needed, or whether the assumed
  extents held. The unit price envelope is the mechanism by which the real number is discovered after
  award, and the corpus contains none of that discovery.
- Both excavations are shallow by the standards of this trade, at ten to fifteen feet with at most one
  level of internal bracing. Nothing here is tested against a deep excavation with multiple bracing
  levels, tiebacks, slurry walls or secant piles, and the tieback regime in particular brings an
  entirely separate seam family around adjacent property easements that this corpus does not touch.
- Tiebacks appear only in one bidder's boilerplate exclusion of tieback remediation, so the whole
  external-anchor regime is unobserved.
- No project in the corpus shows the concrete package's own accounting of what the bracing cost it,
  so the internal bracing seam in section 5 is read out of the retention bidders' language and one
  estimator's marginal note rather than measured. Treat the magnitude as unknown.
- Only one geotechnical monitoring proposal is present and only one bracing design engagement, so the
  consultant side of this package is described from a single document each.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the drawings issued a retention design for bidders to
  price against. Every observation about bidder-authored extent rests on projects where the design
  was left open, and the entry cannot say how this trade behaves when it is not a design-build buy.
- No dispute, claim or change order record is present, so the delay and obstruction rate cards are
  recorded as priced instruments and not as observed outcomes.
