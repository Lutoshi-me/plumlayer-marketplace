# Signage

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the signage package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The entry
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point of this trade is unusual and worth stating first:
there is usually no sign schedule in the documents. Every bidder builds its own sign list off the
plans, and those lists disagree. The scope you produce is therefore not a summary of a schedule, it
is the schedule, and it is the only thing that makes the bids comparable.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 10 1400 is the anchor and it holds firmly. Every estimator sheet keys the package to it,
and where a proposal names a section at all it names that one. Bidders who cross-sell nothing else in
Division 10 still appear under it, so the section number is a strong identifier here in a way it is
not in most trades.

Establish what kind of company is bidding before comparing anything, because four distinct kinds sell
into this package and they are not selling the same thing:

- Retail sign franchises. Consumer-facing estimating systems, cash or deposit payment terms, online
  bill-pay links, charges for proof revisions beyond the first two, validity windows of thirty days.
  They price panels competently and treat submittals, samples and surveys as things that do not exist.
- Architectural sign manufacturers and sign system houses. These mint their own sign-type vocabulary
  or their own location-class matrix, publish a sign count as the deliverable, sell shop drawings and
  a message schedule as priced work, and quote a design-level ladder rather than a single product.
- Sign and awning shops. These carry exterior, illuminated and dimensional work alongside interior
  panels, which is where the exterior scope actually gets bought.
- Graphic solutions houses. These price project management, shop drawings, survey and travel as hours
  at a blended rate, and price installation per sign type rather than as one lump.

The package as bid splits along one line more often than any other, and it splits harder than the
drawings suggest: interior code and wayfinding signage on one side, exterior building identification
on the other. Bidders routinely file them as physically separate documents with separate round
histories, and the exterior side is commonly allowance-only. Treat interior and exterior as two
packages that happen to share a bidder, not one package with two parts.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package:

- Parking, traffic and garage signage. Frequently carried, frequently priced as its own separate
  estimate by the same bidder, and just as frequently excluded outright by the interior-focused
  fabricators. It is genuinely contested and belongs on its own lines.
- Garage clearance bars and crash indicators. Almost always priced by whoever gets the parking
  signage, at a unit price an order of magnitude above a panel sign.
- Dimensional letter sets at mail rooms, elevator lobbies and amenity walls. Commonly carried, cited
  to an interior elevation or a casework detail, and priced per word set rather than per letter. These
  are cast or fabricated metal and they cost multiples of a panel sign.
- Evacuation and egress maps. Nearly always carried by the sign package.
- Vinyl applied to glass, whether address numerals, no-smoking legends or elevator regulatory copy.
  Carried by nearly everyone, priced as a set rather than as a sign.
- Desktop and countertop signs at leasing areas. Carried where anyone enumerates that far.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves the package even when it looks like signage:

- Illuminated unit entry signage. See section 5. Where it exists it can exceed the entire rest of the
  package, and it commonly leaves this trade altogether.
- Branding and specialty graphics as a class. Excluded by name in most fabricators' standard lists.
  If the owner has a branding vendor, its art files arrive as an outside document and nobody in this
  package will originate the design.
- Large format graphics and wall murals. Excluded by name wherever the exclusion list is real.
- Epoxy floor and wall wayfinding graphics. Excluded here, and the painting market excludes floor
  coatings too, so this is a recurring genuine gap.
- Digital signage and display hardware. Excluded by name, and no bidder seen carrying it.
- Site monuments shown on civil drawings. Excluded by drawing reference where the exterior package
  goes to a fabricator rather than to a sign and awning shop.
- Room numbering and stenciling. Frequently unassigned. The painting market excludes it as a matter
  of routine, so if the signage package does not carry it, nobody does.

## 2. How the market bids

The organizing fact of this trade is that the bidder produces the quantity. In every project seen the
enumerating bidders built their own sign list from the architectural plans and said so in writing,
usually with the same caveat: the count is estimated from the plans and will change once a sign
location map is produced. That document does not exist at bid time. Two bidders on the same building
counted about three hundred signs and about five hundred, and both were reading honestly.

So a signage proposal is nearly always itemized, and the itemization is the whole content. Expect:

- One number per sign type, with a count, a unit price and an extension. This is the dominant shape
  and it holds across every kind of company.
- Between seven and fifty priced rows. The low end is a franchise collapsing whole families into
  single rows; the high end is a system house pricing a location-class matrix.
- Fabrication and installation separated, either as parallel columns per sign type or as one
  installation lump at the bottom. Both are common.
- A commercial block that the drawings will never imply: samples, surveys, shop drawings and
  submittals, project management, general conditions, freight, contract administration, sales tax.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The count, the size and the material of every sign type it carries. This is reliable.
- The mount method, at least where the bidder prices by mount. Mount is chosen by substrate, so the
  same sign appears twice at different prices, once for a glass surface and once for a non-glass one.
- Whether installation is union or open shop. Commonly stated, and an estimator records it for every
  bidder regardless.
- Its own assumptions about quantity derivation, sometimes as arithmetic written into the line
  description. A bidder that publishes its derivation is auditable in a way its competitors are not.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Sign copy. Nearly every priced row carries the message as to be determined. Nobody prices copy
  because nobody has the message schedule yet.
- Colors, type sizes and typefaces, which are quoted back verbatim as selected by others.
- Which of its signs are code-required and which are discretionary. One bidder made that split and it
  was the most useful sentence any bidder produced. It arrived by email, not in the priced document.
- Warranty duration. Stated rarely, and usually only after an estimator asks by email.
- Whether it will accept the general contractor's subcontract terms without a legal review fee.

Exclusion behavior is thinner here than in most trades. A short printed list of four to seven items
is common among the architectural fabricators, franchises often carry none beyond damage disclaimers,
and the sign and awning shops bury the important boundaries in a house-terms paragraph rather than in
an exclusion list. The consequence is that exclusion-list leveling yields almost nothing in this
trade. The priced breakout is where the scope lives.

Revision habits, and this trade revises heavily:

- Recounting against a newer set is the most common cause of a revised number, and it moves counts by
  a few percent without moving a single unit price.
- Product substitution is the second, and it is the one that moves money. A unit sign repriced from an
  illuminated fixture to a flat plaque, or from a rendering-based custom panel to a standard one, can
  halve a total on its own. It is almost never labelled as a scope change.
- Configuration selection. Where a bidder quoted two design levels, a later round promotes the cheaper
  one to the base bid and demotes the dearer one to an add alternate. The headline falls and nothing
  has been conceded.
- Descope calls. Told plainly that its number was high, a bidder will reclassify sign families as
  optional and zero their quantities. That is real scope leaving, and the rows stay visible at zero.
- Commercial answers to a general contractor checklist: hold pricing to a date, confirm tariffs are
  included, put a two year warranty in the base with a deduct for one year, list value engineering
  separately, price an insurance credit, add bond above a contract threshold. These answers frequently
  live only as email sentences and never enter the priced document.

Two practical consequences. First, a revision here is usually a document artifact or a product
artifact rather than a negotiation artifact, so read the cited basis document before assuming an
escalation. Second, order rounds by the set the bidder cites, never by the letterhead date. Estimating
systems in this trade reprint the original created date on every regeneration, so two documents eleven
months and one entire rescope apart can carry the same date on their face.

## 3. Scope grain rules

The natural grain is one line per sign type, and the natural count of lines is between thirty and
fifty for a residential building. Fewer than about fifteen and the sheet cannot distinguish bidders at
all, because the divergence in this trade lives in counts and in product, and a collapsed row hides
both. The thinnest estimator sheets seen carried two and nine rows, and on both the general
contractor ended up levelling numbers that did not cover the same work.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Sign type by function. Unit identification, room identification, restroom, stair identification,
  stair exit access, stair landing regulatory, floor and level identification, exit, elevator notice,
  two-way communication, evacuation map, area of rescue, maximum occupancy, rules and regulations,
  fire department regulatory, fire alarm panel, fire department connection, sprinkler control valve,
  shutoff valve, roof authorized access, storage, no smoking, fair housing, accessibility notice,
  hallway directional. This is the primary axis in every itemized proposal.
- Location class, crossed with function. Front of house, back of house, leasing area, amenity,
  exterior, garage, pool. The same functional sign carries a different price in each, because the
  design level differs, and a package priced as one class throughout is not the package that gets
  built.
- Substrate, because it decides the mount and the mount is priced. A glass surface and a non-glass
  surface are separate rows of the same sign in the sheets that are built well. Adhesive mounting,
  keyhole backers, screws through the face, ceiling hanging, bracket hanging, posts set in landscape
  and zip ties are all distinct and all priced differently.
- Photoluminescent stairwell signage as its own line, separate from ordinary stair signs. It costs
  several times a plain panel and it is code-driven, so it is neither optional nor comparable to the
  rest of the stair family.
- Unit identification as its own line, always, and never folded into room identification. Section 5
  explains why.
- Dimensional letters and letter sets, one line per word set or per location group, separate from
  panel signage entirely. Different material, different mounting, different order of magnitude.
- Exterior building identification, one line per sign, with illumination stated. A single illuminated
  blade or wall sign can approach the value of an entire interior package.
- Exterior monuments and site directionals, separate from building-mounted identification, because
  they carry foundations and the foundation is somebody else's.
- Parking and traffic signage as its own block: accessible parking, van accessible, reserved, electric
  vehicle charging, future resident, surveillance notices, clearance bars.
- Vinyl and applied graphics, priced per set.
- Every commercial line the trade actually sells: shop drawings and submittals, samples of each sign
  type, a technical survey, project management, general conditions, freight, installation
  mobilizations, permits, contract administration.
- Sales tax, explicitly. It is material here, it applies to fabrication and usually not to
  installation, and bidders disagree about whether it is in the number.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual signs within a type. A count is the right grain; a sign is not.
- Copy, color, typeface and type size. These are attributes of a line, and they are unknown at bid
  time anyway.
- Braille and tactile text. An attribute of a compliant panel, not a separate scope.
- Floors and buildings, unless the design level or the access changes. A repeating floor prices as a
  count, not as a row per level.
- Panel thickness and print method. These are the design-level variable and belong as an alternate
  across the whole package rather than as a per-line split.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be silent
about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied here the test
fires hardest on the unit sign, on exterior identification, on parking and traffic, on the commercial
block, and on anything illuminated. It fires almost not at all on the ordinary interior panel
families, which is why a sheet that enumerates only those families cannot separate bidders.

Silence is a finding in this trade more than in most. Installation is the clearest case. A proposal
with no installation line is not a cheap furnish-and-install bid, it is a furnish bid, and it will
rank first on any leveling sheet that does not notice. Look for the installation line explicitly on
every proposal and treat its absence as a scope gap rather than an inclusion.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs to expect, meaning scope no bidder reliably carries that has to be added to every
number before comparison:

1. Installation, where a bidder priced fabrication only.
2. Shop drawings, submittals and samples, absent from every franchise-style quote.
3. A technical survey of existing conditions.
4. Permits for exterior signage, and the acquisition labor as distinct from the fee.
5. Blocking and structural support for anything wall-mounted or dimensional.
6. Primary electrical and the dedicated circuit for anything illuminated.
7. Sales tax, where a bidder excluded it while its competitors printed it inside their totals.
8. The exterior building identification package, where the interior bidder was silent.
9. Room numbering and stenciling, which the painting package routinely excludes.
10. Sign copy development and the message schedule itself.

A scope line set that names all ten is doing the estimator's plugging in advance.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

| Excluded by the sign contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Blocking and structural modifications to support signs (commonly, in house terms rather than in an exclusion list) | Rough carpentry, or misc metals for exterior letters and cabinets |
| Provision and connection of primary electrical, and access for secondary wiring (nearly always, whenever anything is illuminated) | Electrical, and it needs a dedicated circuit and a junction box with a disconnect at the sign |
| Foundations for monuments and posts (commonly) | Site, concrete or landscape |
| Permit fees, as distinct from the permit application (commonly) | General contractor or owner |
| Police details and lane closures for exterior installation (commonly) | General contractor, unless the exterior tab prices it |
| Fire department approvals of the code signage submittal (occasionally, stated as the customer's responsibility) | General contractor |
| Engineering of exterior signs, where required (occasionally) | Unassigned, and a real gap on anything large or projecting |
| Exterior parking and traffic signage (about half the interior-focused fabricators) | A separate parking sign supplier, or unassigned |
| Branding and specialty signage (most architectural fabricators) | Owner's branding vendor, or unassigned |
| Large format graphics (most) | Unassigned |
| Epoxy floor and wall wayfinding graphics (commonly) | Frequently unassigned; the painting package excludes floor coatings too |
| Digital signage (occasionally) | Audiovisual or owner |
| Commercial and retail tenant signage including its exterior identification (occasionally) | Tenant, under its own lease |
| Sitework and site monument signage shown on civil drawings (occasionally, cited by drawing) | Site, or a sign and awning shop |
| Artwork, fonts and copyright permissions for supplied logos (some bidders) | Owner or branding vendor |
| Damage to walls during installation, and painting or patching afterward (nearly every franchise) | General contractor, or back-charged |
| Owner controlled insurance participation, payroll reporting, prevailing wage, lift rental, local storage, parking (one house-terms paragraph, several bidders) | General contractor |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table is boilerplate and reads identically across unrelated competitors, so its presence
carries no project information. Read it for the trade map rather than for the project.

What is project signal:

- An exclusion that cites a drawing. When a bidder excludes scope by naming the sheet it appears on,
  it read that sheet and decided not to fabricate what is on it. This is the highest-value exclusion
  shape in the trade and it points straight at an RFI.
- An exclusion of a basis-of-design product paired with an add alternate for the bidder's own version.
  That is a disagreement about the design, not about the scope, and it has to be resolved before any
  ranking means anything.
- A stated inability to price. A bidder writing that it did not feel comfortable pricing the blade,
  canopy and monument signs with the information available is reporting a documents defect, not
  hedging.
- A quantity annotated with a question about who decides. A bidder writing that the architect has to
  say which basement doors are hold-open before the count can settle has named a real open question
  and priced its guess anyway.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

- The count is an estimate from the plans and will change when a sign location map is produced. Nearly
  universal among the enumerating bidders, and it means the number is provisional by the bidder's own
  statement.
- Final pricing pends sign design and quantity approval by the client. Common, and it is the same
  point from the design side.
- Exterior signage is allowance-only until municipal permit research is complete. Common wherever an
  exterior scope exists at all. This is a real regulatory gate, not a hedge, and it is treated in
  section 6.
- Fabrication validity of thirty to ninety days, with a contingency line offered to hold a price
  longer. Universal. Sign fabrication prices are not held through a long precon.
- Fifty percent deposit to lock pricing and enter production. Near universal, and it is a cash-flow
  fact the general contractor's payment terms have to absorb.
- Installation assumes normal working hours, optimum site conditions and readiness of the surfaces.
  Common, and out-of-sequence or after-hours work is chargeable.
- Multiple finished sizes for one sign type voids the unit price and requires requoting. Stated by
  some. It matters because the sign schedule that eventually appears usually does have size variants.
- A legal review fee for the general contractor's subcontract, banded by contract value. Stated by
  some, waived by the same bidders when their own agreement is used instead. It is a real line and it
  is invisible on the drawings.
- Production begins only after signed acceptance, signed drawing approval, deposit and permit
  approval, with six to eight weeks to manufacture and install after that. Where stated, that clock
  is the buyout deadline for the whole package.
- The approved shop drawing, not the contract documents, is the document of record. Stated by some
  fabricators, and it inverts the document hierarchy. Where a bidder's terms say this, pricing to the
  specification scopes nothing.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The unit sign: the seam that decides the package

On a residential project the unit sign is one row with a large count, and it is the single most
divergent line in the trade. Bidders reading one set have priced it as:

1. A flat tactile plaque with braille, adhesive mounted. The default, and the cheapest by a wide
   margin.
2. A thicker panel at an elevated design level, sometimes citing an owner's standard from a
   comparable building rather than anything in the contract documents.
3. A custom panel priced from an interior rendering, at several times the plaque price.
4. An illuminated sconce with an integral unit number, at roughly nine times the plaque price, sold
   furnish-only with installation by others.
5. Brushed metal numerals with no braille and no backer, offered as the shop's recommendation.
6. Excluded entirely, because the interior designer's basis-of-design version is one the shop will not
   fabricate, with the shop's own alternative offered as an add.
7. Absent, because the general contractor routed it to the electrician as a lighting fixture.

Every one of those is a defensible read of a drawing set. The consequence is that the unit sign row
alone can swing a signage number by a multiple, and that the cheapest bid and the dearest bid on a
project frequently differ on this row and nowhere else. Establish which of these seven the project
intends before soliciting, put the answer on its own scope line with the product named, and if the
sign is illuminated, decide in writing whether it is signage scope or electrical scope. When it goes
to electrical, the signage package still has to say so, or two packages will both assume the other
has it.

The same shape, smaller, appears at the water heater closet and storage signs on projects that have
hundreds of them. Those are high-count, low-value rows where the size is set by what an inspector
will accept rather than by any drawing, and a bidder will value engineer them to the smallest panel
that passes.

### Exterior identification

Exterior building identification is a different trade wearing the same section number. Its seams:

- Structural support. A wall sign or a cabinet needs steel tubes, mounting plates and through-bolts,
  and the sign shop's own shop drawings say those tubes are provided by others. On monuments the base
  is a concrete or grouted masonry element, also by others. Neither shows on an architectural
  elevation, and neither is in the sign shop's number.
- Blocking behind dimensional letters. Every dimensional letter set is stud-mounted into something
  that has to be there.
- Primary power. An illuminated sign wants a dedicated circuit and a junction box with a disconnect
  switch delivered to the sign location by the electrician. The sign shop wires everything inside the
  sign and nothing outside it. This boundary is stated identically by every kind of bidder and it is
  still routinely unassigned.
- Permits. Exterior signage is a distinct regulatory gate that interior signage does not have. The
  municipal ordinance governs how many signs a building may carry, their total area, whether they may
  project past a sidewalk or property line, and which illumination types are permitted. Until someone
  reads that ordinance against the elevations, exterior scope cannot be priced, and bidders say so
  plainly by carrying the whole exterior tab as an allowance. Expect the allowance to drift between
  rounds with no scope statement attached to explain it.
- Lane closures and lift access for high wall signs, priced with the permit rather than with the sign.

### Other seams

- Glazing. Signs mounted on glass need a different adhesive and often a backer panel behind the glass,
  which appears as its own priced row. Where a bid does not distinguish glass from non-glass mounting
  it has not read the elevations.
- Millwork and specialties. Mail room dimensional letters are cited to interior elevations and to
  casework details, and they mount to a panel or wall furnished by another package. Mailbox
  identification panels sit at the same wall and can be bid by either trade. Confirm which package
  furnishes the substrate before either one prices the letters.
- Elevators. Floor designation and hall lantern signage sits at the elevator entrance, and elevator
  jamb plates are the elevator contractor's. Elevator regulatory copy applied as vinyl to the cab or
  trim is the sign package's. Both exist on the same door.
- Painting. Room numbering and stenciling is excluded by the painting market as routine. It lands here
  or nowhere.
- Fire protection and life safety. Fire department connection, fire alarm panel, sprinkler control
  valve and shutoff valve signs are signage scope but their locations and their required text come
  from other trades' drawings and from the fire department's own review, not from the architectural
  set.
- Flooring. Epoxy floor wayfinding graphics fall between this package and flooring and are commonly
  excluded by both.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

Start from the strongest fact: on the projects seen there was no sign schedule in the documents. What
exists instead, in descending order of what bidders actually cite:

1. A single architectural detail sheet carrying typical code sign details, sometimes with a partial
   type list. This is the closest thing to a schedule and it is where a bidder that prices only code
   signage stops.
2. Interior design drawings. Where an interior design package exists it governs sign design intent,
   and bidders cite its sheet numbers when excluding scope or when pricing a design level. It is the
   primary document for anything the resident sees, in the same way it is the primary document for
   millwork.
3. Renderings. A bidder will price a unit sign off a rendering and say so on the priced row. A
   rendering is doing the work a specification would do, and it is expensive.
4. Exterior elevations, for building identification, plus a site plan for monuments and directionals.
5. Floor plans, for counting doors and rooms.
6. The specification section, which is usually a short paragraph naming a material family and leaving
   color, type size and typeface as selected. It rarely settles anything.

What the drawings will not settle, and each of these moves the number:

- The sign count itself. This is the leveling denominator of the trade and it is the one number no
  document supplies. Expect roughly two and a half to three and a half signs per residential unit
  across the enumerating bidders, and expect that range to be wider than any price difference between
  them.
- The message schedule. Every sign's copy is to be determined at bid time. Someone has to author it,
  and a system house will sell it as a priced deliverable inside general conditions. If nobody buys
  it, nobody writes it.
- The sign location map. Every enumerating bidder conditions its count on this document, which does
  not exist yet and which is normally produced after award, in a review with the architect. The
  buyout number is provisional until that review happens.
- Which signs are code-required for occupancy and which are discretionary. Hallway directionals,
  interior and exterior rules, and leasing-area courtesy signs are commonly discretionary, and they
  are a meaningful fraction of the count. No document makes this split.
- Design level. Panel thickness, print method and whether the panel is back-painted or direct-printed
  are the value-engineering axis of the whole package, and the usual outcome is a mix: elevated at
  front of house, value engineered at back of house. That mix is not decided by any drawing and is
  normally settled in a call.
- Whether the unit sign is illuminated, and whose scope it is if it is.
- Whether the general contractor will accept the fabricator's terms or require its own subcontract
  with a legal review fee attached.
- Union or open shop installation. Some bidders quote a priced union adder; most quote one basis and
  state it.
- Whether an owner controlled insurance program applies, and whether the credit is a real number.
- Warranty term. A one year term is the market default; an owner asking for two is a specification
  requirement invisible on the drawings, and it may or may not be free.
- Whether the owner has set a signage allowance. Where an owner does, it is commonly split interior
  and exterior, and it is commonly well above what the market actually bids.
- Site conditions that change materials. Coastal salt exposure drives exterior letters to painted
  aluminum. Nothing on a drawing says so.
- Access, staging, parking and lift availability, which some bidders price at zero on a stated
  assumption with the rate shown if the assumption fails.

Set-stage behavior is mild in this trade. Counts drift by a few percent between design development,
permit and construction documents, and the drift is genuine recounting rather than repricing. What
does move between stages is the product and the commercial terms, and those move because of
conversations rather than because of drawings. A bidder re-reading a construction set will typically
find a handful of signs it missed, such as an electric vehicle charging station called out in an
architect's note, and add them.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, per sign type. The universal priced unit for fabrication.
- Each, per sign type, for installation as well, in the shops that price installation per row.
- Full-day multi-tech mobilizations for installation, in the shops that price it as visits. Counts run
  from five to eleven on residential projects, with a stated rate for each additional mobilization.
  This is a production basis, not a quantity, and two bidders on the same building will assume
  different mobilization counts.
- Hours at a blended rate for project management, shop drawings, survey and travel.
- Sets, for dimensional letter word groups, vinyl legends and address numerals.
- Lots, for whole sign families, in the fabricators that price by family. When a bidder prices the lot
  it usually still enumerates the contents, so the counts are recoverable and comparable even when the
  prices are not.
- Percent of contract value, for a legal review fee banded by contract size.
- Square feet, but only in the municipal ordinance, which caps total sign area per building. This is
  the only place square footage matters in the trade and it constrains the exterior design rather than
  pricing it.
- Cost per residential unit, which is what every estimator sheet normalizes to. It is a poor
  denominator for this trade, because the cost driver is sign count and sign count per unit is itself
  the contested variable, but it is the currency in use.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. The sign count, which the bidder invents. Nothing else in the trade moves a number as much.
2. Whether the unit sign is a flat plaque, an elevated panel, a rendering-derived custom, or an
   illuminated fixture. On a project with hundreds of units this single row can be most of the bid.
3. Whether exterior building identification is in scope, and whether any of it is illuminated. One
   illuminated blade or wall sign can approach the value of an entire interior package.
4. Whether installation is in the number at all, and whether it is union or open shop.
5. Design level across the package, meaning panel thickness and print method. The spread between an
   elevated and a value engineered version of the same list runs around twenty to twenty five percent
   of fabrication.
6. The commercial block: shop drawings, submittals, samples, survey, project management, general
   conditions. In the fabricators that price it properly this can approach a third of the product
   value, and in the franchises it is simply missing.
7. Mobilization count and site access.
8. Sales tax treatment, which is not a rounding error here because fabrication is taxable and
   installation generally is not.
9. Permit and lane closure costs on the exterior package.
10. Photoluminescent and specialty code signage, which costs several times a plain panel.
11. Warranty term, insurance basis and bond.
12. Freight, which appears only on the exterior package.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. Within a single project the spread between the lowest
and highest cost per residential unit has run at roughly two and a half to one, six to one, and
seventeen and a half to one. Those are among the widest spreads in any trade, and almost none of it is
price competition. Read them carefully, because the spread in this trade proves less than in any other
covered so far:

- A spread of six to one collapsed to under two to one once the low bidder's missing installation line
  and the high row's folded-in exterior package were corrected. The ranking was measuring package
  boundaries, not prices.
- A spread of seventeen and a half to one was almost entirely a sign count difference: eighty signs
  priced off one detail sheet against eight hundred priced off the whole set. The two bidders who
  priced comparable scope landed within about seven percent of each other.
- Conversely, a tight spread proves nothing about agreement. Two bidders can land within a few percent
  while assuming sign counts that differ by a fifth, because a lower count at a higher design level
  and a higher count at a lower one arrive at the same total. Count convergence and price convergence
  are independent in this trade, and the count is the thing that gets built.
- The top of a signage ranking is usually a bidder that priced something illuminated. It is not an
  expensive bidder, it is a bidder answering a different question about the same drawing.

One more convention worth knowing. An estimator does not compare base bids here, and often cannot: the
ranking rows are frequently rounds rather than companies, with one bidder appearing three times as its
count and product changed, and one row sometimes being the general contractor's own internal budget or
the owner's allowance carried as a competing column. Where a general contractor budgets signage as a
flat rate per residential unit, expect the real market to come in well above it, by a factor of around
four in the one case where a budget and a real bid met. Before ranking anything, normalize every column
to the same sign count, the same unit sign product, the same installation inclusion, and the same tax
treatment. Very little else in the number matters by comparison.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly two dozen signage proposals from ten bidders, spanning four
projects and four estimator leveling sheets, all in one regional market and all bid by one general
contractor. The proposals span design development, permit, construction document and conformed rounds,
and several bidders appear on more than one project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-signal
distinction in section 4 testable. Three of the four projects are residential buildings of between
forty and three hundred and twenty five units; one is an office-to-residential conversion.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from residential work with amenity and back of house space. Nothing is tested
  against commercial interiors, healthcare, education, hospitality or transit, where wayfinding is a
  designed system with its own consultant and the packaging is likely to be entirely different.
- No project carried a real sign schedule or a sign location map at bid time, so the entry cannot say
  how the market bids when one exists. The claim that bidders build their own counts may be a property
  of these documents rather than of the trade.
- No wayfinding design consultant appears anywhere. Where a designed wayfinding system exists, the
  design-assist versus fabricate-from-design question that this entry treats as a product dispute
  becomes a contracting question, and nothing here speaks to it.
- Exterior signage is present but thin. Every exterior scope seen was an allowance pending permit
  research, so the entry can describe the regulatory gate but has never seen a priced, permitted
  exterior package with real numbers behind it. One bidder's comparable-project worksheet is the only
  evidence of what exterior identification actually costs, and it is that bidder's own record from
  other jobs.
- No monument sign was ever bought. Foundation and structural seams for monuments rest on shop
  drawings from unrelated projects rather than on anything priced here.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is present, so nothing shows which exclusions survived
  negotiation, whether the sign location map review actually settled the counts, or how far the final
  count moved from the bid count.
- No change order history. The claim that the count is provisional until the location map review is
  read out of the bidders' own language rather than observed, so treat it as untested here.
- Tenant and retail signage is excluded by name in several proposals and never appears priced, so the
  entry cannot say how the tenant boundary is actually drawn.
- Prevailing wage work is named only as an exclusion. No prevailing wage signage bid exists here.
