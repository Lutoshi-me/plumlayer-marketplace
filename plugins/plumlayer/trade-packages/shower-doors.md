# Shower Doors and Enclosures

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for shower doors,
enclosures and shower surrounds. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and
estimator leveling sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is,
is carried by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. This is a package that four different markets claim, whose
supply half and install half are usually sold by different companies, and whose price swings by more
than a factor of two on a product decision the drawings rarely settle.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Start with the kind of company, because packaging follows from it and nothing else in this trade
makes sense until it is established. Five kinds bid this work and they sell different things:

- Specialist glass enclosure fabricator-installers. They template each opening in the field,
  fabricate glass to the measured condition, install with their own crews, and warrant the result.
  They sell doors and enclosures only, never surrounds or pans. Their argument is that field
  templating removes hold-to conditions and that managing the whole scope removes the lead time
  problem.
- Domestic cast marble manufacturers. They sell surround panels, shower pans and tub surrounds made
  to order in a domestic plant, and they resell a catalogue glass enclosure alongside. They ship;
  they do not install.
- Offshore-sourced bundled interiors suppliers. They sell shower doors, surrounds, pans and
  accessories alongside countertops, cabinets and sinks, sourced overseas, with a named certified
  third-party installer quoted separately in the same document.
- Certified cast marble installation contractors. Install only, at published per-element rates, with
  the general contractor furnishing product, backing and hardware.
- Glaziers and Division 10 specialties houses, who appear on bid lists and, where their numbers are
  a fraction of everyone else's, are pricing a partial scope.

Three consequences follow.

First, the package is normally bought as three separately awardable pieces, and the corpus shows a
leveling sheet doing exactly that: a supply row for doors, a supply row for surrounds and pans, and
an install row, with different companies winning different rows. A bid conditioning its price on the
whole package selling together, which the bundled suppliers do by requiring separate purchase orders
per division and the specialty houses do by reserving a re-quote if sections are broken out,
conflicts structurally with how the package actually gets assembled.

Second, this scope is levelled inside the Division 10 specialties tab, not in glazing, on every
project in the corpus. But the finish schedule mark that names it is often a glazing mark, which is
exactly why glazing claims it. The glazing entry in this knowledge base records shower doors as
contested four ways and routed to Division 10 on three projects, and from this side that matches: on
one project the shower door figure was carried as an other-sub value plugged uniformly into every
Division 10 bidder's column, because no specialties bidder carried it. The mark says glass; the
package says specialties; nobody owns it by default.

Third, the surround, the pan and the door are three separable scopes that come in almost every
combination. One supplier bid doors only on one project while an installer on the same project quoted
four hundred and forty-one surrounds nobody in that document supplied. Never write a shower
enclosures line.

Section identity is weak and mostly irrelevant here. The corpus routes the work by trade token and by
finish schedule mark, not by section number.

## 2. How the market bids

Proposal shape follows bidder kind almost perfectly.

The specialist enclosure fabricators write one page: a product specification block naming glass
thickness and grade, hinge type, handle, channel and header configuration, an opening size, a count,
and one number stated as including template, fabricate, install, warranty and tax. Upgrades are
offered as named adds. This is the cleanest document in the trade and the count is always stated.

The cast marble manufacturers write a line-item quote with unit prices: one row per panel type per
fixture size, one row per pan type, one row per door size, plus crating, plus freight, plus tax.
Quantities and unit prices are both readable. The catch is that freight and tax are frequently quoted
as to be determined, so the printed total omits two of the largest cost categories.

The bundled suppliers write a divisional matrix: rows for product families, columns for material,
mold fee, freight and tax, with a separate certified third-party installation total, and a set of
line-item detail pages behind it carrying unit prices and counts by unit type. The shower block is
one of three divisions on the page and the printed project total covers all of them.

The install-only contractors write two lines with a count and a rate each. This is the most useful
document in the trade for normalization purposes and the only place the labor half is visible as a
rate.

What a proposal will normally state:

- A count, and usually several counts, decomposed by fixture type.
- Glass thickness, and for the specialists, glass grade, hardware finish, hinge type and handle.
- For cast marble, panel dimensions per wall and pan dimensions with drain type.
- Freight and crating treatment.
- Validity, commonly thirty days.

What a proposal will normally not state:

- Whether the pan is set by this trade or by someone else. It is excluded, but frequently as a note
  buried in a tax or install column rather than in an exclusion list.
- Fire rating of molded product. One quote header carries a fire rated field marked no, and one
  states that molded product is not fire rated unless specifically requested and so indicated. Nobody
  else raises it.
- Tariff exposure, except from the offshore suppliers, who name the specific duty categories they
  exclude and pin the rates to a date.
- Blocking requirements, except as a note code on a line item.
- Warranty, which is one year from the specialists and unstated by the suppliers.

Revision habits:

- A count correction with rates held. Common and clean.
- A product substitution that moves the price without moving the count. The largest one in the corpus
  moved a door and glass line from domestic to offshore sourcing, roughly halving the material rate
  while the mold fee and the freight both rose sharply. Read as a price drop, it is a supply chain
  change.
- A specification correction against a rising price. One supplier's revision three days later raised
  its material total by about a fifth, and the change was pans moving from a centre drain to a trench
  drain on the two largest fixture types. Nothing labelled it a scope change. The drain type is set
  by another trade's detail and is invisible in a total.
- A negotiation ladder with a product-detail change. One specialist's number fell by about fifteen
  percent between rounds while a header appeared and the glass grade moved out of the base into a
  priced upgrade.
- One half of a bundled package revising while the other half does not. A supplier's material total
  stayed byte-identical between rounds while its third-party install total fell, which means the
  supply count and the install count no longer agree and nothing in either document flags it.
- Scope entering and leaving inside one document. One proposal carries its own revision log recording
  a new bid, then add shower doors, then remove shower doors, inside a fortnight.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets here are rows inside a much larger specialties tab. The corpus shows three shapes: a
single plugged row, three rows splitting doors, surrounds and pans, and two rows splitting supply from
install. The three-row and the supply-install shapes are both defensible; the single row is not.

### What enumerates

- Fixture type, with a count each. This is the primary axis and it is the one the market answers
  natively. The vocabulary the corpus uses: standard shower at each drawn size, roll-in or accessible
  shower, and tub. These are different products at different rates and they carry different
  accessories.
- Door count, separately from surround count, always. They are not the same number. On one project
  the counts were one hundred and twenty-eight surrounds against one hundred and thirteen doors; on
  another, four hundred and forty-one surrounds against three hundred and fifty-four doors. Tubs and
  roll-in showers take a surround and no hinged door. A single line covering both hides the entire
  difference.
- Pan count and pan type, its own rows, with the drain type named. Centre drain, trench drain and
  custom roll-in pans are separately priced and the drain type alone has been worth about a fifth of
  a package.
- Enclosure configuration, its own row: door only, door and panel, panel-door-panel, with or without
  a header, and with the channel condition named. Configuration and rate move together and neither is
  readable from a total.
- Glass thickness and grade, its own attributes. The corpus spans eight millimetre, ten millimetre
  and three eighths of an inch, and clear versus low iron ultra clear as a priced upgrade.
- Hardware finish and pull style, its own row. Where more than one finish is called out for the same
  scope, this is a documents conflict, not a selection to defer.
- Clear opening dimension, its own attribute where accessibility governs. Only one bidder in the
  corpus states the clear opening its door produces, and it is the number that determines compliance.
- Accessories, each their own row with its own count: corner soap dish, foot rest, accessory ledge,
  trim sticks. Their counts differ from the room count and from each other, and the installer prices
  them per item with a stated upcharge if they arrive late in the revision cycle.
- Installation, its own row, with the counterparty named. See the seam section.
- Pan protection during construction. One supplier prices an acrylic pan protector per pan as an
  optional line and nobody else mentions it. The pan is installed early and lives through the rest of
  the build; the protection is real work whoever does it.
- Blocking and backing, its own row, named to the framing or drywall package.
- Caulking, its own row, distinguishing stone-to-stone caulking, which the installer includes, from
  caulking at the pan-to-floor and enclosure-to-finish joints, which is contested.

### What collapses

- Individual bathrooms collapse into a fixture-type count. No document prices a single bathroom.
- Panel counts collapse into the fixture set they belong to, for scope purposes. One supplier prices
  a surround as a back panel plus two side panels, so its panel count is about three times the room
  count. Comparing a panel count against a room count is meaningless, and this is a real trap.
- Colour, edge profile, finish gloss and pattern are attributes.
- Freight and crating collapse into the line for scope purposes even though they are separately
  priced, because no bidder competes on them.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

The test fires on the pan, on the installation, on the accessories, on the door-versus-surround count
difference, and on the glass grade. Each can be carried, excluded, or passed over, and each is worth
real money.

Silence here is dangerous in a specific way. A bundled supplier's matrix shows zeros against product
families it is not offering, and those zeros read as included to anyone skimming. On one project the
surround, pan, soap dish and foot rest lines were all zero on a project carrying four hundred and
forty-one surrounds. Read as included, that would leave the largest fixture quantity on the project
with no supplier. Zeros in this trade mean not offered far more often than they mean no charge.

### The structural gap list

Scope no bidder carries, which must be added to every number:

1. Shower pan setting and the drain assembly. Excluded universally, assigned to a licensed plumber,
   and the pan is fabricated for a specific drain assembly that is not provided.
2. Blocking and backing behind surround panels, doors and grab bars.
3. Installation, where the supplier is a manufacturer. The cast marble houses ship and do not
   install, and the install can land with a certified installer, with the supplier's named third
   party, or inside the finish carpentry package. All three appear.
4. Receiving, unloading and site storage. The offshore suppliers make the client responsible for
   unload including equipment and a licensed operator, and note that surround crates need extended
   forks.
5. Protection of installed pans and finished glass.
6. Freight and tax, on the manufacturer quotes that leave both as to be determined.
7. Tariffs and duties, excluded by name on the offshore quotes.
8. Waterproofing behind and beneath the assembly, which belongs to another package entirely and
   which no document in this corpus mentions.
9. Mock-up, attic stock, hardware samples, bonding and hoisting, excluded by the specialists.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

The specialists carry a short, near-identical exclusion list. The manufacturers carry almost none and
express their boundaries as column headings instead, which is why the exclusion list is a poor
leveling instrument here and the priced matrix is the authoritative document.

| Excluded by the shower door or surround contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Shower pan setting and drain assembly (every supplier seen) | Licensed plumber |
| Blocking and backing (universally, by note code or by silence) | Framing or drywall |
| Installation (the manufacturers, universally) | Certified installer, the supplier's third party, or finish carpentry |
| Receiving, unload and site storage (the offshore suppliers, explicitly) | General contractor |
| Freight and sales tax, quoted as to be determined (the domestic manufacturers) | Added at order |
| Tariffs, duties and import fees (the offshore suppliers, named by category) | General contractor, unquantified |
| Mock up, attic stock, hardware samples, bonding, hoisting and lifts (the specialists) | General contractor |
| Off-hours install and deliveries (the specialists) | General contractor |
| Structural integrity of the wall, base or mounting surface (the specialists) | Whoever built it |
| Cracked tile, marble, base or tub caused by required hole drilling (the specialists) | Disclaimed entirely |
| Cabinet or flat-pack assembly, where the installer's package spans other scopes (the installers) | Upcharge and change order |

Qualifications that are really pricing bases:

- Hole drilling and its consequences. Every enclosure requires drilling into tile, cast marble, a pan
  or sometimes a tub to secure the unit, and the specialists disclaim responsibility for cracking the
  surface material. That risk sits with the general contractor and it is real on a project with
  hundreds of bathrooms.
- Field template versus fixed dimension. The two supply models are different risk instruments. The
  specialist templates each opening after the walls and pans are in, and argues that this removes
  hold-to conditions. The offshore supplier states that field measurement will not be required
  because dimensions are produced as quoted from approved shop drawings. The first absorbs field
  variance and needs the finishes complete first; the second does not need field access and pushes
  all variance risk onto the accuracy of the framing. Neither is wrong, and they are not comparable
  as prices.
- Production release gates. Release into production requires deposit, executed contract, executed
  change orders and signed shop drawings. Everything downstream of that is the schedule.
- Mold and tooling fees. A one-time charge that does not scale with quantity and can run to half the
  material value on an offshore door line. It must never be spread across a per-unit rate.
- Mobilization minimums. The installers price a minimum number of units per trip, to be negotiated
  before contract, with a per-trip charge beyond it. On a phased turnover this is a live cost.
- Award conditions. Separate purchase orders per division are required by the bundled suppliers, and
  the specialty houses reserve a re-quote if sections are broken out.
- Payment terms. Half of material and shipment plus all sales tax before production release is seen,
  with intermediate payments and a net term after delivery, and deposits explicitly non-refundable.
- Overseas production calendar. One quote names a factory shutdown period of roughly a month and
  warns that lead times depend on when production is released relative to it.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is a seam-dominant trade and the seams determine the package more than the products do.

- Supply and install. The defining seam. It has at least four resolutions in the corpus: the
  specialist who does both, the manufacturer who ships and a certified installer who installs, the
  bundled supplier who quotes both halves in one document under separate purchase orders, and the
  case where install is carried inside the finish carpentry package. One leveling sheet records six
  different answers to a single install row. Name the resolution on the scope line; do not assume one.
- Plumbing. The pan is fabricated for a specific drain assembly which the supplier does not provide,
  and the pan setting is assigned to a licensed plumber by every supplier seen. The pan-to-drain
  connection is the seam and it is unambiguous in the documents, which is unusual.
- Tile and stone. Where the surround is tile rather than cast marble, this package shrinks to a door
  and the tiling package grows. Where the surround is cast marble, the tiling package stops at the
  bathroom floor. Which one applies is a finish decision that moves a large quantity between two
  packages, and it is readable from the finish schedule.
- Framing and drywall. Blocking for panels, doors and any grab bar. A note code on a line item
  reading blocking required is the only place this appears in a supplier document.
- Waterproofing. Nothing in this corpus addresses what is behind the surround or under the pan. That
  is a genuine hole in the evidence, not a genuine hole in the work.
- Finish carpentry. Install has been recorded as carried in finish carpentry on at least one project,
  which makes the two packages overlap on exactly the same physical work.
- Glazing. The finish schedule mark for a shower enclosure is frequently a glazing mark, and glaziers
  bid these lists. The corpus routes the award to Division 10, but the mark keeps pulling it back.
  Whichever way a project resolves it, the resolution should be stated, because both packages will
  otherwise assume the other has it.
- Countertops, cabinets and accessories. The bundled suppliers and the installers both sell across
  all of these, so a shower door number may be extractable from a much larger interiors package or
  may be buried in one. Check whether the block is separately subtotalled before assuming a number
  exists.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What these contractors price from:

1. Enlarged bathroom plans and unit plans, for fixture counts and opening dimensions. The count is a
   takeoff off the unit mix, and one supplier states its counts explicitly by unit type.
2. The finish schedule, for the enclosure mark, the surround material and the hardware finish.
3. Interior elevations and details at the shower, for the enclosure configuration, the header
   condition and the clear opening.
4. The plumbing fixture schedule and shower drain details, for pan type and drain configuration.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each moves the number:

- The unit mix, which is what turns a bathroom count into a fixture-type count.
- Whether the surround is cast marble or tile. This decides whether the package exists at all in this
  form.
- Whether the drain is a centre drain or a trench drain. Worth about a fifth of a package and set by
  a detail in another discipline.
- Whether the glass is standard clear or low iron, and at what thickness. The upgrade to low iron has
  been priced at close to a fifth of a base bid.
- Whether the enclosure is field-templated or fixed-dimension, which is a procurement decision, not a
  design one, and which determines when the trade can start.
- Whether the product is domestic or offshore. This is the single largest cost variable in the trade
  and it carries a lead time, a tooling fee, a tariff exposure and a replacement-speed consequence
  with it. The domestic manufacturers argue the point explicitly in their own marketing, contrasting
  lead time, breakage, replacement speed and time zones against imports, and the argument is a real
  scope difference rather than a sales pitch.
- Tariff exposure at the time of order.
- Who installs, and whether that party is certified for the product.
- When the walls, pans and finishes will be ready, and how many mobilizations the turnover sequence
  implies.
- Whether the molded product needs to be fire rated, which it is not unless specifically requested.

Set-stage behavior. Counts in this trade converge better than in the other trades in this cluster,
because the fixture count follows the unit mix and the unit mix is settled early. On one project three
bidders carried door counts within one percent of each other. On another they carried one hundred and
twelve, one hundred and thirteen and one hundred and fourteen. Where a count does diverge sharply it
is usually a bidder pricing a different scope, such as a door count from a company whose document is
a cabinet schedule of values.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, per enclosure or per door. The specialists' unit.
- Each, per panel, per pan and per accessory. The cast marble manufacturers' unit, and note that the
  panel count is roughly three times the room count.
- Each, per element installed. The installers' unit, and the only place the labor half is exposed as
  a rate.
- Square feet per surround set. Stated by one manufacturer alongside its per-set price, and the only
  area basis in the corpus.
- Sets, per unit type. The offshore supplier's basis, tied directly to the unit mix.
- Lump sum for mold and tooling fees, crating, and freight allowances by shipment count.
- Apartment units, as the leveling sheet's normalizing denominator for the whole specialties package.

Cost drivers, ranked:

1. Product class of the door. A custom field-templated heavy frameless enclosure with brass hardware
   has run to roughly two and a half times the delivered cost of an offshore fixed-dimension swing
   door with a fixed panel on the same project, and to roughly five times the unit rate of a
   catalogue frameless hinged door resold by a cast marble house. This dwarfs every other variable.
2. Domestic versus offshore sourcing on the surround and door supply.
3. Whether installation is inside the number.
4. Drain type on the pans.
5. Glass grade and thickness.
6. Fixture-type mix, since roll-in and tub fixtures carry different panels and no hinged door.
7. Mold and tooling fees, which do not scale with quantity.
8. Freight and crating, which the manufacturers quote as allowances or leave open.
9. Tariff exposure.
10. Hardware finish and pull style.
11. Mobilization count and minimum units per trip.
12. Labor basis, where a union option is offered explicitly.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. On the supply row of one leveling sheet three offers
spanned about 1.6 times. Across the whole corpus, delivered cost per bathroom spans well beyond a
factor of two, and the span is product class rather than competitiveness.

What the spread does not prove, and this trade has the sharpest example in the whole cluster. On one
project two cast marble manufacturers' printed totals landed within about one tenth of one percent of
each other. That looks like a market clearing at a price. It is not: neither total included freight,
neither included sales tax, one carried a tariff allowance marked to be determined, and neither
included any installation at all. The two numbers agree because they omit the same four cost
categories, and a third bidder on the same project whose comparable number was roughly a half higher
had installation inside it. On the same project a specialist's number for doors alone exceeded either
manufacturer's whole material package including surrounds and pans, because the products are not the
same product. Two totals agreeing in this trade is evidence about what was left out, not evidence
about price. Always reconstruct delivered-and-installed cost per fixture type before comparing
anything, and check what the printed total contains before you believe it.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about twenty shower door, enclosure and surround proposals from eight
companies, plus three estimator specialties leveling sheets, drawn from four mid-rise residential
projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor. Proposals span design
development, permit, construction document and conformed sets, and most companies appear on more than
one project.

Roughly a third of the files in this trade are not what their names say. Two are byte-identical pairs
filed under two different trade tokens, two are same-text pairs with different byte hashes filed
under a doors token and a surrounds token, one is filed under a barn doors token and contains no barn
doors and prices a different project than the folder it sits in, one is an image-only email screenshot
whose entire quote is four lines of message body, and one is a cabinet payment application filed under
a shower doors token that nonetheless contains the project's lowest door count in the thinnest glass.
Hash first, render anything thin, and open every file.

What the entry cannot yet claim:

- Everything here is apartment bathrooms in mid-rise residential work in one market. Nothing is
  tested against hotel, dormitory, healthcare, assisted living or single-family work, and hotel work
  in particular is the volume market for this product with different procurement entirely.
- The corpus contains no tiled shower project, so the boundary in section 5 between this package and
  the tiling package is described from one side only. Where the surround is tile, this entry
  describes a package that mostly does not exist.
- Nothing in the corpus addresses waterproofing, pan liners, curbs or the substrate behind the
  surround. That work is real and lives in another package, and this entry cannot say where.
- Accessibility is thinly covered. Roll-in fixtures appear as a counted type and one bidder states a
  clear opening dimension, but no document in the corpus addresses grab bar coordination, seat
  mounting, threshold requirements or the compliance review a clear opening actually needs.
- The domestic-versus-offshore claim in section 7 rests on one supplier quoting both ways across two
  rounds and on two domestic manufacturers competing with it. It is a strong signal from a narrow
  base.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing shows how the supply, install
  and pan-setting rows were finally assigned on any project.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The drilling and cracked-surface disclaimer, the
  mobilization minimums and the hold-to dimension risk are read out of proposal language rather than
  observed converting.
- Only one document prices pan protection during construction, and only one addresses fire rating of
  molded product, so both of those scope lines rest on a single source each.
- Labor basis is almost invisible here. One specialist offers a union option explicitly and nobody
  else states a basis, so nothing can be said about a labor delta.
