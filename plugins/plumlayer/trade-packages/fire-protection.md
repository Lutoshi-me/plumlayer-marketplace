# Fire Protection

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the fire protection
and sprinkler package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator
leveling sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried
by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real sprinkler contractors can bid it and a real
estimator can level it.

One thing sets this trade apart from almost every other package and colors every section below. The
fire protection drawings are not the design. They are a performance intent, and the winning bidder
finishes the design: it runs the hydraulic calculations, sets the head count, sizes the pipe, and
submits stamped shop drawings to an authority that can reject them. The bid is a promise to complete
a design nobody has completed yet. Everything about how this trade prices, qualifies, revises and
argues follows from that.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Division 21, section 210000, is the anchor and is nearly always the section a proposal or a leveling
sheet names. Fire suppression and fire protection are the same package under two labels, and both
labels appear. Fire alarm is Division 28 and is a different bidder entirely, which matters because
the most contested seam in this trade runs straight along that division line.

Establish what kind of company is bidding before comparing any two numbers. Three kinds appear and
they are not peers:

- The installing sprinkler contractor. This is the default and the great majority of the market.
  It designs, fabricates, installs and commissions, and it holds the license the work is permitted
  under.
- The full-line mechanical contractor that sells plumbing, HVAC and fire protection off one
  letterhead. It bids the package as one of several divisions it is chasing on the same job. Its
  scope tends to be shaped by what its own shop does rather than by what the trade normally carries,
  and one such bidder excluded the residential piping material the specification and every competitor
  assumed, without flagging it as a substitution.
- The inspection, testing and service company bidding installation work. It appears on bid lists
  because it is already in the fire protection business, and it lands two to three times the
  installing contractors. Counting it as a peer makes a bid list look wider and more dispersed than
  it is.

The package has two grain regimes under one division, and which one applies changes the entire scope
list. Say which one you are in before anything else.

- New construction. The scope is systems and equipment: service entry, riser assemblies, valves,
  pumps, standpipes, distribution, heads by area. The document set is complete enough that bidders
  argue about coverage extent rather than about existing conditions.
- Occupied or phased renovation. Everything above still applies, and a whole second scope block
  arrives on top of it: demolition of the existing system, make-safe, maintaining protection for the
  parts of the building that stay occupied and sprinklered throughout, temporary standpipes,
  salvaging owner equipment, and tying new work into piping that stays. On the renovation projects
  seen, that block is where the bidders diverge most violently and where the estimator does the most
  plugging.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another
division:

- Firestopping and fire caulking at this trade's own penetrations. Commonly carried, and it should
  be, because the penetrations are the sprinkler contractor's own. Where a general contractor has
  ruled that each trade firestops its own work, expect this to be in. Where a dedicated firestopping
  subcontractor is carried, expect a bidder to exclude it anyway and leave a small gap. Ask for it
  as a breakout: bidders who priced it separately came back roughly three times apart.
- Coring, sleeves and seals for its own piping. Usually carried. A minority exclude core drilling
  outright, which is a real gap because nobody else drills for sprinkler pipe.
- Underground fire service for the last few feet. Almost always split at a stated distance rather
  than carried or excluded whole. See section 5, because the split point moves.
- Hoisting and rigging of its own equipment. Carried by about half; the rest push it to the general
  contractor. The fire pump and controller are heavy and this is not a nominal line.
- Hydrant flow test. Sometimes carried with the fee, sometimes offered as a small priced add,
  occasionally excluded. It is a prerequisite to the hydraulic design, so if nobody carries it,
  nobody can design.
- Standpipe and system acceptance testing with the authority. Usually carried. Off-hours testing
  is a separate question and usually is not.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like sprinklers:

- Fire alarm, detection, and all wiring. Excluded by every bidder seen, without exception. See
  section 5; this is the defining seam of the trade.
- Fire extinguishers and cabinets. Excluded by every bidder seen. They belong to the specialties
  package, and the exclusion is printed on the form rather than considered.
- Fireproofing and firestopping on existing pipe in renovation work. Excluded where it comes up.
- Clean-agent, deluge, water-curtain and other special hazard systems. Excluded where they are
  named. A bidder will not carry these on a residential job unless they are drawn.
- Water storage tanks. Excluded as a class, and usually excluded with the note that none is shown.
  If the hydraulic calculations later demand storage, no package carries it.
- Underground fire main from the street, trenching, backfill, thrust blocks and street tie-in fees.
  Excluded by nearly every bidder.
- Concrete housekeeping pads for the pump and controller. Excluded almost universally and rarely
  picked up anywhere, because it is a small concrete item nobody remembers to route.
- The state or local fire protection narrative report. One bidder excluded it by name; nobody
  carried it; nothing else in the corpus mentions it. It is a code deliverable with no obvious home.

## 2. How the market bids

The default output is one number and two lists. Segmentation is rare and shallow.

- Single lump sum with no internal breakout: the great majority, on every project and in every set
  stage.
- Segmented into two or three numbers: occasionally, and when it happens the segmentation is almost
  always commercial rather than physical. It splits out a bond, an insurance basis, a training
  requirement, or a wiring allowance, not a building or an area.
- Never seen segmented by area, by floor, or by system. A bidder that carries wet, dry, pre-action
  and standpipe systems will still quote one number for all four. Ask for a per-system breakout and
  expect not to get one.
- Priced quantities, unit prices and measured takeoffs are essentially absent from the face of a
  proposal. One bidder disclosed that its earlier number was a budget square-foot rate and its
  revised one a hard takeoff, which is the only place in the corpus where the estimating method is
  stated at all.

So the realistic expectation is a lump sum against a sheet list. The proposal will not decompose,
and asking it to is how most of the useful information in this trade gets extracted.

Proposal length varies enormously and does not track price. The shortest proposals seen are a single
page with one includes sentence and one not-included sentence; the longest enumerate roughly
thirty-five components each keyed to a drawing location, plus a fifty-item exclusion paragraph. On
one renovation the one-page bidder was the low bid, beating component enumerations by a comfortable
margin. Document length measures the bidder's writing habits, not its scope.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The basis document, by date, and very often by enumerated sheet number. This trade cites its basis
  more reliably than any other. Some proposals list every fire protection sheet individually, forty
  or more of them, and one uses that list as its entire scope statement. Order rounds by cited basis
  and not by letterhead date, because both bidder templates and folder placement lie.
- The design standard, by name and edition, and the standpipe standard separately from the sprinkler
  standard. Nearly always.
- That all areas will be hydraulically calculated, that a professional engineer will review and
  stamp, and that shop drawings and calculations go to the authority for approval. Commonly.
- The pipe schedule and material by area: black steel of a stated schedule for wet mains and
  standpipes, galvanized for dry systems, and a plastic piping system in residential units. Nearly
  always, and it is the single most consistently stated technical fact in the trade.
- The head type by area: concealed pendent in finished ceilings, upright in exposed areas, dry
  pendent or dry sidewall in unheated space and at balconies. Commonly.
- The fire pump, by type and often by capacity. Commonly.
- Where its work starts relative to the building line. Nearly always, and the answers differ from
  each other. See section 5.
- Labor market. Stated by roughly half, usually as a negative that prevailing wage does not apply.
  An estimator records it for every bidder regardless, and treats it as a first-class comparison
  field.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Head counts. Almost never volunteered. When a general contractor asks for them on a scope sheet,
  bidders answer, and the answers are where the trade's real divergence lives. See section 7.
- Labor hours or crew size. Almost never volunteered, sometimes answered when asked.
- Lead times. Almost never volunteered, and always material. See section 6.
- Anything about the go-forward design risk it is accepting. A bidder will say it will calculate
  hydraulically; it will not say what happens if the calculation comes back worse than assumed.
  One bidder is the exception and states the threshold explicitly.

Exclusion behavior is uniform in this trade and that is exactly what makes it uninformative. Nearly
every proposal carries an exclusion list, usually ten to thirty lines, occasionally fifty. The lists
overlap so heavily between competitors that leveling off them yields almost no differentiation.
Several are demonstrably pasted templates: one document excludes a valve type on its exclusion list
that it has already priced as an inclusion two paragraphs above, and excludes a pipe coating that
its own dry system requires. Where a proposal contradicts itself, the priced inclusion line is the
authoritative read and the exclusion list is boilerplate.

Revision habits are the richest part of this trade and the most dangerous. Same-bidder round pairs
show, in rough order of frequency:

- Price moves, scope text does not. Very common, and the movement can be enormous. Two documents one
  month apart citing the identical basis, the identical enumerated sheet list, the identical scope
  bullets and the identical exclusions differed by more than half the number. Nothing but the total
  distinguishes the rounds.
- The number does not move at all while the basis document does. One bidder quoted the identical
  figure to the dollar against a design development set, a permit set and a construction set spanning
  most of a year, restating the citation each time under explicit pressure from the general
  contractor to submit an updated price. An estimator's ranking will show these as three separate
  competitive rows.
- Scope leaves the number through a sentence, not through the exclusion list. One bidder's round
  pair changed two phrases: demolition became demolition and removal by others, and disposal of the
  material it drops became disposal of all material. The price fell and nothing appeared in any
  exclusion list. Diff the inclusion sentences.
- Scope leaves and only part of it is disclosed. Another round pair carved out make-safe explicitly,
  at a value covering less than a tenth of the price drop, while a firestopping line silently vanished
  from the inclusion list in the same revision.
- Exclusions are added while the price rises. Seen where a bidder moved onto a later set and used the
  occasion to narrow scope and raise the number at once, disclosing neither as such.
- Previously silent or excluded items become named add alternates while the base drifts down slightly.
  The base looks like a small saving; the adds total more than a tenth of it.
- Labor concessions with no scope change at all. Union market recovery is available on some projects,
  and once one bidder discloses it, the general contractor asks everyone and every union bidder finds
  it. A separate bidder dropped its number substantially by reclassifying from a commercial to a
  residential labor rate, which is a third labor basis distinct from union and open shop.
- A negotiation ladder. One bidder moved through four rounds in nine days, roughly a third of the
  movement being scope departure and the rest being that labor rate reclassification, opened by the
  general contractor naming its normalization basis out loud and telling the bidder how far off the
  pack it was.

Two consequences. First, a revision in this trade is usually a negotiation artifact and only
occasionally a document-change artifact, so do not assume a later round reflects an addendum. Second,
the revision frequently is not a document: it may be an email sentence, and a written confirmation of
no change on a reissued set is treated as a valid revised bid. One ranking row in the corpus is a
price a bidder stated in an email while declining to submit at all.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Fire protection scope sheets have run 9, 27, 39, 143 and 258 rows. Target roughly one hundred to one
hundred fifty scope lines for a full building package, and expect a meaningful fraction of those to
be commercial and contractual rather than physical. The thinnest sheet seen, at nine rows, is the one
where the single largest piece of equipment in the package went unpriced by two of three bidders and
nobody noticed until leveling. Sheet thinness and scope divergence track together tightly here.

The largest sheet is instructive in the other direction. Of its rows, fewer than a hundred describe
physical work; the rest cover contract terms, submittals, testing, safety, hoisting, coordination and
schedule. That is not padding. In a design-build trade, the schedule rows and the submittal rows are
scope, because the deliverable is a design and the design has a review cycle.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every system type as its own row: wet, dry, pre-action, standpipe, and any special hazard system.
  These are separate systems with separate valves, separate pipe materials and separate air or
  nitrogen equipment, and bidders answer them independently. A single sprinkler system line is
  unbiddable.
- Every dry valve location separately. Dry systems appear wherever the space is unheated or exposed:
  parking garages, trash chutes, roof and attic interstitial spaces, balconies, first floors adjacent
  to openings. A leveling sheet that enumerated the dry valve locations one per row got clean answers;
  the ones that did not, did not.
- Every named piece of equipment by its drawing tag: fire pump, jockey pump, controllers, nitrogen
  generator, air compressors, alarm check valve, dry pipe valve, double check backflow, pre-action
  valve assemblies, floor control valve assemblies both pressure reducing and not, fire department
  connections, pumper connection check valve, test header, roof manifolds and roof hydrants,
  corrosion detectors. These are individually specified, individually priced internally, and
  individually the subject of value-engineering attempts. Every estimator sheet that got useful
  answers keyed these rows to the drawing tag.
- Pipe type by system and by area, as its own block: wet piping, dry piping, standpipe piping,
  residential piping. The schedule and the coating are the content of these lines, and both are
  routinely deviated from. A row that says piping is not a row.
- Head type by area, each area its own row: exposed garage, ceilings at garage, corridors, amenity,
  within units, balconies and decks, stairwells and intermediate landings, ceiling-to-roof spaces,
  interstitial and concealed combustible spaces, trash chutes, skylight wells, exterior. Coverage
  extent varies by area more than anything else in the trade, and the interstitial and concealed
  combustible rows are where the biggest single dollar swing lives.
- Head count, as an explicit answer, broken at minimum into residential, common and amenity, and
  garage. Bidders will supply these when asked and will not otherwise. They are the leveling
  denominator this trade actually needs. See section 7 for why.
- Head finish, escutcheon type and coordination with the architect, as its own line. Concealed
  versus semi-recessed is the most frequently quoted head substitution in the corpus, and it is an
  architectural decision priced in Division 21.
- Heads centered in ceiling tile, as its own line. Nearly every bidder states it as a qualification
  hedged with where possible. It is a coordination commitment against the ceiling package and it is
  worth its own row so that where possible does not silently become wherever it lands.
- The service entry point, stated as a distance and a physical object, as its own row. See section 5.
- Freeze protection as two rows, not one: heat trace, and insulation of the heat-traced pipe. Both
  are excluded by nearly every bidder, and where drawings show none, bidders answer that none is
  shown. That answer does not survive first cold weather in a garage or an unheated stair.
- Seismic bracing and expansion joints or loops as their own rows. Seismic is a recurring
  drawings-versus-specification conflict; expansion loops appear where seismic separations do, and
  bidders guess at their locations.
- Firestopping, coring and sleeves as their own block, separate from the piping.
- Temporary protection as its own block with several rows: temporary standpipe, whether permanent
  standpipes may be used temporarily, temporary sprinkler coverage during construction, whether
  existing heads must be turned up, and off-hours access for any of it. Bidders answer these
  differently from each other and from themselves round to round.
- Testing, commissioning, acceptance with the authority, and off-hours testing as separate rows.
  Off-hours is nearly always excluded even where testing is included.
- Training per specification, as its own line. Where the specification requires it, no bidder seen
  carried it in base and the estimator plugged it against everyone.
- Shop drawings, hydraulic calculations, professional engineer review and stamp, and as-builts as a
  block. Nearly always included, but the review cycle behind them is a schedule item, not a cost item,
  and belongs on the schedule rows too.
- Bond, insurance basis, and the owner-controlled insurance question as separate commercial rows.
  Bidders quote a bond as a percentage add and the percentages seen differ from each other and from
  the project's own stated rate.
- On renovation work, an entire demolition block: cut and cap, make safe, drop to floor, removal from
  the building, disposal, salvage of owner equipment, and maintaining protection to occupied areas.
  Six rows minimum, and each is answered differently by different bidders. See section 5.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Hangers, supports, sleeves and escutcheons collapse into the piping lines. Every bidder carries
  them and none prices them separately.
- Individual head counts by room or by floor collapse to the area totals. No document in the corpus
  prices a room, and no estimator sheet asks for one.
- Fittings, couplings and grooving method collapse into the pipe line as attributes.
- Valve tags, signage and tag charts collapse into one identification line.
- Levels collapse into a level range unless the system type or the head type actually changes.
- Low point drains, auxiliary drains and inspector test connections collapse into the drain riser
  line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied here,
the test fires hardest on the coverage-extent questions in section 1, the seams in section 5, and
every row where the answer depends on a hydraulic calculation nobody has run yet.

This trade adds a fourth state that the three-way test does not catch, and it matters more here than
almost anywhere. A bidder can exclude an item as a design conclusion rather than as a commercial
boundary. Excluded because none are shown, excluded because not necessary, excluded because the
insulation in the truss makes it unrequired: these read like exclusions and are actually the bidder
telling you what it thinks the code requires. They are unpriceable by the estimator because there is
nothing to price until somebody decides whether the item exists. On one project, five bidders produced
five different answers to whether concealed combustible space protection was in scope, three of them
phrased as reasons rather than as prices, while the general contractor's own sheet carried the same
item as a pending request for information. Nobody priced it. That is not a leveling problem to absorb;
it is a request for information the scope list should have raised before the bids went out.

Silence is a finding here as it is everywhere, but in this trade the higher-value finding is the
disagreement. Where two bidders read the same drawing note two irreconcilable ways, surface it
upstream. Two of the sharpest examples in the corpus: one bidder priced a diesel fire pump while
citing a set in which the pump had already changed to electric, and another bidder refused to price
a conditional piping requirement on the stated ground that the affected areas would have to be
identified first, while a competitor priced that same condition as a named add worth about a tenth of
its base.

### The structural gap list

These are the recurring plugs: scope no bidder carries that a general contractor must add to every
number before comparing. A scope line set naming all of them is doing the plugging in advance.

1. All wiring and detection for pre-action and air-sampling systems. Nobody carries it, and it is
   the single largest recurring plug in the trade.
2. Temporary protection during construction, including turning up existing heads on renovation work.
3. Training per specification.
4. Freeze protection, both heat trace and its insulation.
5. Concrete housekeeping pads for the pump and controller.
6. Concealed combustible and interstitial space coverage, where the documents leave it ambiguous.
7. Off-hours work: testing, shutdowns, and access to occupied areas.
8. The underground fire service between the street and wherever the sprinkler contractor starts.
9. Fire watch, impairment plans, and shutdown fees.
10. Building department permit fees and third party review fees, as distinct from fire department
    permit fees, which bidders do carry.
11. Utility company back charges where the specification names them.
12. Bond and the conventional general liability premium where no owner-controlled program applies.
13. On renovation work, removal and disposal of demolished piping.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate, and in this trade the boilerplate is unusually dense: repeat
bidders reproduce the same list verbatim across projects, and competitors' lists overlap so heavily
that a leveling pass built on exclusion lists alone finds nothing. Read them for the trade map, not
for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else:

- An exclusion carrying a stated reason that points at a document. None shown, not necessary, no
  details, conflicting details. These are the project's request-for-information list pre-written by
  the market. Capture the reason, not just the exclusion.
- An exclusion whose validity depends on another package. A concealed-space deduct that is only
  valid if a different division fills the truss with non-combustible insulation is not a fire
  protection decision at all. On one project the paired insulation add came back at nothing, so the
  option could not be evaluated in either direction.
- An exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: an occupied tenant, a vault belonging to the
  serving utility, a sidewalk, a terra cotta slab, an existing pump to be salvaged.
- A conditional exclusion tied to a threshold. See the hydraulic qualifications below.
- A commercial exclusion nobody else addresses, such as workforce participation requirements, which
  appeared once and is a genuine cost when a local hiring policy applies.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number. Violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order, and in this trade several of them can be violated by a calculation rather than by an event.

- Adequate water pressure and flow at the point of connection is assumed. Stated by nearly every
  bidder, and it is the load-bearing assumption of the whole price. The hydraulic calculations are
  run after award. If the water is worse than assumed, pipe grows, and one bidder states exactly
  where its price stops: it excludes the consequences of poor water conditions above a named
  distribution pipe size and a named crossmain size. That is the clearest disclosure of design-build
  risk in the corpus, and it is worth asking every bidder for the equivalent.
- The exclusion section of the quote letter presides over the bid documents. Stated flatly by one
  repeat bidder on every proposal. It inverts the document hierarchy: whatever the drawings and
  specification say, the bidder's own list governs. Check whether a proposal subordinates the
  contract documents before treating it as a compliant bid.
- This is not a design-build project, and the bidder will not be responsible for devices or heads at
  soffits, beam pockets, built-ins, obstructions or concealed combustible spaces not shown on the bid
  drawings. Stated verbatim by one bidder across projects, and it is the trade's central tension put
  in writing. Every one of the listed conditions is exactly what a hydraulically calculated,
  code-compliant layout discovers after award.
- Design-build requirements excluded, by name, from a proposal that includes a professional engineer
  seal and full hydraulic calculations. Engineering to what is drawn and completing a design are
  different products, and a bidder can sell one while refusing the other.
- Heads in suspended ceilings will be centered in the tile where possible. Nearly universal. It is a
  qualified commitment to the ceiling package, not a firm one.
- Work is priced on normal business hours, usually with the hours stated. Nearly universal, and it
  interacts badly with occupied buildings, testing with the authority, and utility vault access.
- Installation to be complete before ceilings. Stated as the reason a bidder excludes replacing
  ceiling tile it damages. That is a sequencing condition, not a boundary, and if the sequence does
  not hold the exclusion becomes a change.
- The specification's warranty term, answered yes and then narrowed in the same breath to workmanship
  only, not equipment. Seen once, and it is the kind of qualified yes a checkbox grid cannot express.
- Quote validity windows, commonly fifteen or thirty days and occasionally ninety. Short, and shorter
  than in most trades. Material volatility and tariff reservations are cited; one proposal reserves
  the right to seek more money for tariff-driven material increases outright.
- An indemnification clause running in the bidder's favor, covering pre-existing conditions, work by
  others, and failures of the fire alarm system it does not install. Seen once, attached to an
  otherwise ordinary quote, and it materially changes what that number is worth. Contract terms of
  this kind will not appear in any scope comparison unless somebody reads the attachments.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The fire alarm seam, which is the one that matters most

Every sprinkler contractor excludes all electrical wiring. Every one. The exclusion is worded slightly
differently and the difference is the whole seam:

1. Wiring of any electrical or fire alarm devices we install is by others. The most common form. The
   sprinkler contractor furnishes and installs the physical device and somebody else wires it.
2. Devices for the pre-action system will be provided; all installation and wiring by others. A
   furnish-only mode on a subset of the scope, sitting inside an otherwise furnish-and-install bid.
3. All electrical wiring including power wiring of devices furnished by us, and of devices removed by
   us, is by others. The broadest form, and the one that also disclaims the demolition side.
4. Pre-action valves including electrical wiring, carried in base, while fire alarm work associated
   with those same valves is excluded. One bidder, once, and it is the only proposal in the corpus
   that carries any of this wiring. It ranked as an outlier partly because of it.

The devices sitting in this gap are flow switches, tamper switches, pressure switches, pre-action
release panels and their detection, air-sampling detection, manual pull stations, discharge
indicators, corrosion detectors with pressure switches, and the pump controller's power feed. On a
project with pre-action systems the gap is the largest single recurring plug in the trade, and one
bidder quantified it by carrying an explicit allowance rather than a price, which the estimator then
applied to every competitor that was silent.

Two practical consequences. First, put the wiring on its own scope row and route it to the electrical
or fire alarm package deliberately, because both of those packages carve out sprinkler-related wiring
from their side too. Second, when a bidder prices this add, look at how it is qualified. One
quantified its add only on the assumption of a non-union electrical and alarm subcontractor, which
makes the number a labor-market bet as much as a scope.

### The water service entry seam

Every bidder states where its work starts, and the answers are not the same. Seen, in order of
frequency:

- At the flange inside the building, on a service that others furnish, install, flush and test. The
  most common answer by a wide margin.
- Within ten feet of the foundation wall, with the sprinkler contractor installing that last stretch
  and excavation and backfill by others.
- Ten feet outside the building, with the sprinkler contractor installing the underground pipe from
  that point in, excavation and backfill still by others.
- At a flange spigot piece, with the service tested, flushed and chlorinated by others. Same boundary,
  different words, and the chlorination is a separate obligation nobody else names.

On one project two competing bidders used two different start points in the same round, one starting
outside and installing the underground, the other starting at the flange. Their numbers are not
comparable until that is normalized, and the difference does not appear in any total. The scope sheet
row that got a clean answer asked the question directly: are you bringing service in, and are you
providing link seals.

The backflow preventer travels with the sprinkler contractor in every case seen. The excavation,
thrust blocks and street tie-in never do.

### The demolition seam on renovation work

This seam produced the widest divergence of anything in the corpus, and it is worth several scope
rows on its own. The operation decomposes into five steps and every bidder draws its line somewhere
different:

1. Cut and cap, so the remaining system stays live and safe.
2. Make safe more broadly, including capping and pressurizing what stays.
3. Drop to floor, cutting the abandoned pipe down.
4. Remove from the building.
5. Dispose.

Bidders were seen carrying steps one and two only, steps one through three, and steps one through
three with removal by others but disposal ambiguous. On a single leveling row for this operation the
bidders' own stated values spanned roughly fifteen to one, which is not a pricing disagreement, it is
five different scopes wearing one name. One bidder converted the whole block into a separate small
contract between rounds; another moved it out of its base with a two-word sentence edit.

Ask for the five steps as five rows. The demolition contractor's own bid will have its own view of
which of them it carries, and the two views will not match.

### Other seams

- The ceilings package. Heads centered in tile is stated by nearly every bidder as a hedged
  commitment. The counter-obligations arrive from the sprinkler side: no bagging or unbagging of
  heads, no replacement of ceiling tile it damages on the stated grounds that its installation
  precedes the ceilings, and no cutting of ceilings or tiles to accommodate sprinklers. If the
  sequence slips, all three of those become changes. This is a sequencing dependency dressed as three
  exclusions.
- Access panels. Commonly furnish-only, fire-rated where required, installed by general construction.
  Occasionally excluded outright. A row that says access panels without saying which mode is
  unbiddable.
- The insulation package. Two distinct dependencies run this way and both are load-bearing. Adequate
  heat and insulation of wet pipe to prevent freezing is a condition of every price. And on wood-framed
  buildings the concealed-space sprinkler deduct exists only if the insulation package fills the truss
  cavity with non-combustible material. That second one is a compliance outcome owned by another
  division, priced in this one, and it is among the largest single alternates in the trade.
- The electrical package, beyond wiring. Power to the pumps and the controller, transfer switches,
  and power to pre-action and detection equipment. The sprinkler contractor furnishes the equipment
  and needs the feed; nobody's proposal joins those two facts.
- The concrete package. Housekeeping pads for the fire pump and jockey pump. Excluded by the sprinkler
  contractor almost every time, small enough that it falls through, and a hard prerequisite to setting
  the equipment.
- The structural package. Beam penetrations and reinforcement. Excluded by the sprinkler contractor,
  and one scope sheet notes that late submission of sprinkler shop drawings will be back-charged for
  slab or beam penetrations that result. That back-charge is the design-review cycle showing up as a
  commercial term.
- The roofing package. Roof hydrants, roof manifolds and their penetrations get set by the sprinkler
  contractor and flashed by the roofer. Both packages tend to be silent about the other.
- The plumbing package. Genuinely adjacent and genuinely separate: plumbing records fire protection as
  a trade it excluded, and fire protection returns the favor. The one place they touch is the incoming
  service and the backflow assembly, and the seam is usually clean because the sprinkler contractor
  takes the backflow.
- The painting package. Painting excludes painting the sprinkler pipe; the sprinkler contractor
  excludes painting of pipe and protection of heads for painting. Where exposed piping is meant to be
  painted, neither package carries it.
- Temporary protection and the fire watch. A temporary standpipe with a fire department connection is
  usually required by the construction fire safety plan. Some bidders carry it, some assume the
  permanent standpipe can be used temporarily, and some exclude temporary protection wholesale. One
  bidder excluded temporary protection generally in one line and included it for the occupied tenant
  in the next. Fire watch is excluded by nearly everyone and lands on the general contractor.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What sprinkler contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The fire protection drawing set itself, cited by sheet number more precisely than in any other
   trade. The general sheets carrying the head schedule, the pipe specification, the valve details
   and the general notes are the primary documents; the floor plans set the coverage extent.
2. The Division 21 specification, for the pipe schedule, the design standard edition, the warranty
   term, training requirements, seismic requirements, identification and testing. Bidders cite it and
   then deviate from it, which is why it must be read alongside the proposal rather than assumed.
3. The hydraulic assumption. Not a document at all: an assumption about pressure and flow at the point
   of connection that the calculations will confirm or destroy after award.
4. The architectural set, for ceiling type by area, finish expectations at heads, and the balcony and
   soffit conditions that decide whether coverage is wet, dry or unnecessary. One bidder argued from
   an architectural detail that a fire protection callout was unnecessary, which is a cross-discipline
   conflict the fire protection drawings alone cannot surface.
5. The structural set, for truss and joist depth, which decides whether concealed combustible space
   coverage is required at all, and for slab thickness at penetrations.
6. On renovation work, the demolition drawings and their notes, which are where the occupied-area
   obligations and the what-stays decisions live.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff at bid time, and they do not need to, because the head
layout does not exist yet. The normal method is coverage area by hazard classification, plus an
assumed head spacing, plus judgment, converted to a square-foot rate at budget stage and to a hard
takeoff only when a bidder decides the job is real.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Labor market. Union, open shop, merit shop, prevailing wage, and a residential rate class distinct
  from a commercial one. This is the general contractor's own first leveling question in this trade,
  asked in exactly those words. It is also not reliably the answer: on one project the union bidders
  clustered and the single open-shop bidder landed mid-field, while on another the single union bidder
  landed at two to four times the open-shop rate. Record it for every bidder and do not assume its
  direction.
- Whether union market recovery applies. It is real, it moves substantial money, no bidder volunteers
  it, and once one does the general contractor asks everyone and everyone finds it.
- Fire pump lead time and whether it can be bought down. The specified pump controller ran twelve to
  fourteen weeks after approved submittals on one project, with a substituted controller at four to
  five weeks for a stated expediting fee and the pump itself at five to six weeks for another. Lead
  time in this trade is purchasable, which makes it a priced schedule instrument rather than a fixed
  constraint. Ask for it as a line.
- The design review cycle. Shop drawings and hydraulic calculations go to the fire department or the
  authority having jurisdiction and come back with comments. Nothing gets fabricated until they clear.
  A bidder quoted thirty days to produce submittals; nobody quotes the review. This is the trade's
  real long pole and it appears on no drawing.
- Who the authorities are, and that there may be more than one. On one project both the fire
  department and the serving electric utility mandated an air-sampling detection system in the
  vaults, which killed a value-engineering item outright. In this trade a serving utility can be a
  design authority.
- Whether the water is adequate. See section 4. Until the flow test is run and the calculations are
  done, every price in the trade is conditional.
- When the hydrant flow test was last done and whether a new one is needed. Some bidders carry it,
  some price it as a small add with a separate after-hours rate, some exclude it.
- Licensing and the professional engineer. The work is permitted under a licensed contractor and the
  drawings are sealed by a fire protection engineer. Bidders list state license numbers on their
  letterheads across several jurisdictions. The corpus has not shown a bidder disqualified on
  licensing grounds, but the license is a gate, not a preference.
- The insurance basis. Whether an owner-controlled program applies, and the cost of conventional
  general liability if it does not. Quoted as a priced add, and the adds seen differ from each other
  by roughly two thirds for equivalent coverage.
- Bond cost. Quoted as a percentage, and the percentages bidders actually charge diverged from the
  project's own stated bond rate by more than half again.
- The occupancy and phasing plan on renovation work: what stays sprinklered, at what pressure, for
  how long, and whether protection may be dropped a floor at a time or must be maintained continuously.
- Whether off-hours access will be required for testing, shutdowns, or work adjacent to an occupied
  tenant. Excluded by nearly everyone and priced as a named alternate by a few.
- Whether there is space and a path to remove the existing pump and bring the new one in. One bidder
  makes it an explicit assumption; nobody else mentions it, and on a renovation it is a real risk.
- Whether the general contractor's project management portal, payment application fees and
  compliance requirements apply. Some estimator sheets carry them as scope rows with a stated
  percentage; almost no proposal mentions them.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders price a square-foot budget rate and say so only if
asked afterwards; one disclosed the method only when explaining why its permit-set number had dropped.
Between design development and permit the documents themselves move substantially in this trade: on
one project the fire pump changed from diesel to electric and the concealed-space protection was cut
back to roof level only, and two of five bidders did not notice, one still pricing the superseded pump
while citing the current set. By construction documents and conformed sets, bidders switch to citing
sheet numbers individually and answering head counts. Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its
folder's set stage disagree often enough to be a standing check: two bidders on one project priced a
superseded set while filed in the current round, and one document cited a design development set while
sitting in the construction-document folder with a scope sheet answering yes to the construction
drawings.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Gross square feet of building, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every
  estimator sheet ranks on cost per gross square foot, and the general contractor uses it out loud in
  negotiation, telling a bidder what its rate is and how many competitors are below a stated
  threshold. This is the leveling currency of the trade, and it is the one convention that is
  completely stable across projects.
- The denominator itself needs pinning, because it moves. Seen: total gross square feet from plans;
  gross square feet less an occupied tenant area that stays out of scope; and, from a bidder rather
  than the general contractor, actual sprinklered space, which came in a couple of percent below the
  general contractor's figure on the same building. A rate without its denominator's definition is
  not comparable to another project's.
- Sprinkler heads, each, broken by area. The trade's true physical quantity and the one that reveals
  scope divergence. Bidders supply counts only when asked.
- Linear feet of pipe by size and schedule. Used internally, never seen on the face of a proposal.
- Each, for named equipment: pumps, valves, risers, fire department connections, roof manifolds,
  access panels.
- Gallons per minute, for the fire pump, and pounds per square inch for pressure constraints on
  existing systems that stay live. These are design parameters that show up in scope text.
- Labor hours for the whole project, and crew size at peak. Answered when asked, never volunteered.
- Weeks after approved submittals, for long-lead equipment.
- Percent of contract value, for bond and for general liability.
- Square feet, at budget stage only, as the estimating method rather than as a priced unit.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Which kind of company is bidding. Before any scope question, this explains more of the spread than
   anything else. Installing sprinkler contractors cluster; a mechanical contractor and a testing
   company sitting in the same ranking are selling different things.
2. Labor market and rate class. Union, open shop, merit shop, prevailing wage, and the commercial
   versus residential rate distinction. A single reclassification moved one bid by roughly a tenth.
3. Coverage extent in concealed, interstitial and combustible spaces. The largest single physical
   swing in the trade, routinely worth several percent of the base and once worth more than a tenth
   of it. It is also the one most often unresolved in the documents.
4. Demolition and make-safe scope on renovation work. Values for nominally the same operation spanned
   more than an order of magnitude between bidders on one project.
5. Wiring and detection for pre-action and air-sampling systems. Almost never in anyone's base, and
   worth several percent when it is plugged.
6. Pipe schedule and material. A specification calling one schedule regardless of size against a
   bidder proposing a lighter schedule above a threshold is a recurring deviation, and the compliance
   add is worth several percent. Residential plastic piping versus steel is worth about a tenth of a
   base on a tall building. Galvanized versus black on dry systems is smaller but real.
7. The fire pump and its accessories: whether one is required at all, electric versus diesel, and
   whether the controller is the specified one or a substitute. On one small project the pump alone
   was worth roughly a fifth of the base, and two of three bidders did not carry it.
8. Head type. Concealed versus semi-recessed, and dry flex versus standard dry sidewall at balconies.
   Individually modest, quoted by nearly everyone, and the most common substitution in the trade.
9. Nitrogen generation and corrosion mitigation on dry and pre-action systems. Carried by some,
   excluded by others, and a standing value-engineering target that gets rejected on code grounds
   about as often as it is accepted.
10. Temporary protection, temporary standpipes and off-hours access.
11. Building height, which drives standpipe risers, floor control valve count, and pressure reducing
    requirements.
12. Bond, insurance basis, and warranty term.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per gross square foot ran at roughly 1.3 times, 2.6 times, 3.4 times and 4
times on the four projects seen. Bidder counts run from three to about eleven, and are usually smaller
than in the finish trades. In every case the top of the range was a bidder of a different kind or a
different labor basis rather than an expensive installing contractor, and excluding that one bidder
collapses each spread to somewhere between 1.1 and 1.7 times.

An estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus adjustments, where the adjustments
come from the structural gap list in section 3, and it sources several of those plugs from a
competitor's own stated value: one bidder's pre-action wiring allowance became the plug applied to
every bidder that was silent. That is efficient and it is also circular, because it makes one bidder's
assumption the project's scope definition. Watch for it, and watch particularly for a leveling sheet
whose rows are a selected bidder's proposal lines pasted in rather than the estimator's own taxonomy.

### What the spread does not prove

A tight spread in this trade proves less than in almost any other, and here is the evidence rather
than the assertion.

On one project two bidders came in within half a percent of each other on the base bid, both open
shop, both answering the same issued scope sheet, both on the same construction set. Their stated
head counts differ by a factor of about two: roughly five thousand three hundred against roughly two
thousand six hundred for the same building, with the residential count differing by more than a
thousand five hundred heads and the access panel count by two and a half times. On a different
project two union bidders within a few percent of each other on price stated man hours differing by
about forty-four percent. Price convergence and quantity convergence are independent, and in a trade
where the design is finished after award, the quantity divergence is the thing that will surface later
as a claim.

The reverse also holds. A tight spread can mean the scope sheet asked nothing. The nine-row sheet in
the corpus produced the tightest spread of any project and also the project where the largest single
piece of equipment went unpriced by most of the field. Convergence around an under-specified question
is not agreement.

And a wide spread frequently is not a pricing disagreement at all. The widest project spread in the
corpus resolves almost entirely into two facts about who was bidding rather than what was bid: one
inspection-and-testing company and one union bidder in an otherwise open-shop field. Sort by kind of
company and labor basis before concluding anything about scope.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly twenty-nine fire protection proposals drawn from four projects in
one regional market, all bid by one general contractor, together with the four matching estimator
leveling sheets and two returned scope sheets. The proposals span design development, permit,
construction document and conformed sets, and about a dozen companies appear, several of them on more
than one project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4
testable. Three of the four projects are new mid-rise or high-rise multifamily with amenity space and
structured parking; the fourth is a high-rise conversion of an existing building with a tenant that
stays occupied and sprinklered throughout construction, and it supplies nearly all the renovation and
demolition material.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work in one market through one general
  contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors, institutional, healthcare, industrial,
  warehouse, or public prevailing-wage work, and the hazard classifications in those occupancies drive
  the design differently.
- Only one project involved renovation, so the entire demolition, make-safe and occupied-protection
  block in sections 3 and 5 rests on that project alone, however rich it is.
- No project in the corpus required a fire pump on a low-rise, a fire water storage tank, a
  standpipe-only building, or a high-piled storage or warehouse hazard classification. The trade's
  behavior on those is unknown here.
- Special hazard systems appear only as exclusions. The corpus has not shown a clean-agent, deluge,
  foam or kitchen suppression system actually bid, so how they are packaged and who bids them is not
  established.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation. In a trade where several bidders assert that their own exclusion list governs
  over the contract documents, that gap matters more than usual.
- No change order history and no post-award hydraulic calculation is in the corpus. The claim running
  through this entire entry, that the design-build tail converts qualifications into changes, is read
  out of the proposals' own language rather than observed in the field. Treat it as untested here.
- No shop drawing submittal or authority review comment is in the corpus, so the review cycle
  described in section 6 is known only from the durations bidders quoted, not from what actually
  happened.
- The corpus has not shown how the package is bid when the fire alarm and sprinkler work are
  solicited together, which happens in some markets and would collapse the trade's largest seam.
- The corpus has not shown a bid list where a bidder was disqualified on licensing or certification
  grounds, so the licensing gate is described as a gate rather than as an observed elimination.
