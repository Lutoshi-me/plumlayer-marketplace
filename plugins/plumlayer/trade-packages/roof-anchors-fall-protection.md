# Roof anchors and fall protection

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the permanent roof
anchor and fall protection package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and
estimator leveling sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is,
is carried by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. This is a small package that behaves badly, and the reason is
in section 1: the anchor count is not a quantity the drawings fix. It is an output of a design
decision the documents usually leave open, and bidders answer it nine ways.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The package is bid under a division 11 facility fall protection section when the specification names
one, and filed under division 11 by the estimator whatever the section says. It travels under two
trade tokens, roof anchors and fall protection, and the same bidder appears under both on the same
project. Treat the tokens as one package.

Kind of company leads everything else here, because competitors sell genuinely different systems
under one package name. Four kinds bid it:

- Facade access and window washing equipment manufacturers. They think in rope descent and suspended
  maintenance. Their anchors are tie-backs at wide spacing, their cable is a lifeline along the
  working perimeter, and they will also sell davits, catwalks and monorails. They carry the longest
  exclusion lists and the most formal commercial terms in the trade.
- Construction fall protection installers. They think in continuous tie-off during and after
  construction. Their anchors sit at framing spacing, and their counts run an order of magnitude
  above the facade access camp on the same roof.
- Designed access specialists, which sit between the two and sell a suspended access system with an
  installation crew.
- Catalogue supply houses and general steel or ornamental iron fabricators, both of which bid
  occasionally. A steel fabricator bidding a division 11 specialty is why the metals trade treats
  roof anchors as a package it might swallow. It should not; the anchor is a listed, tested,
  certified product and the fabricator's version is a custom fabrication with a different load basis.

Because the four kinds price four systems, a ranking of this package is not a price comparison until
kind of company is established and one system is chosen. See section 7.

What collapses into the package:

- The horizontal lifeline, always. Anchors and cable are never separately bid.
- The engineering, PE stamp, load testing and the closeout logbook and certificate. Nearly always
  inside the number, sometimes as their own priced lines.
- Ballasted, non-penetrating roof guardrail, sometimes. One fall protection bidder priced it as a
  second block inside its own quote while the same project ran it as its own bid package. Expect the
  same scope to appear twice on one project and reconcile before comparing.
- Davits and window washing equipment, where a facade access manufacturer is bidding and the roof is
  tall enough to need them. On renovation work the product family shifts to davits outright.

What leaves the package, and to whom, is section 5. The short version is that this bidder installs an
object and nothing else. Blocking, roofing, flashing, hoisting, access and the structural capacity to
take the load all sit with other trades, and the bidder says so in writing every time.

## 2. How the market bids

The proposal is short. A page or two of scope, a price, and a list of things the bidder will not do.

- A lump sum with a handful of component lines is the norm: anchors as a count, cable as a length,
  then freight, installation, load test and engineering stamp as separate amounts. Common.
- A true unit-priced material list appears when a supply house is bidding, and then every anchor type
  is separately priced with a manufacturer part number.
- A supply-only price and a supply-and-install price quoted side by side as a menu, from the facade
  access manufacturers. Common enough that packaging mode must be read off the price table, not
  assumed.
- A single lump sum with no breakout at all. Seen.
- An email sentence with a number in it and no document behind it. Seen more than once, on more than
  one project, from more than one bidder, including on a package worth six figures. One of them was
  the low bidder.

What a proposal will state:

- The anchor count and the anchor type, nearly always. This is the trade's entire quantity statement.
- The cable length, where a lifeline is included.
- The load test basis. Values of eighteen hundred, twenty five hundred and five thousand pounds all
  appear against the same nominal standard on the same roof. The five thousand pound figure is the
  OSHA anchorage requirement; the lower numbers are the bidder's proof-test load. They are not the
  same thing and a leveling sheet with one load column will mix them.
- Whether the layout is the bidder's or somebody else's, in the scope heading. Both are common.

What a proposal will not state:

- Any breakdown of the installation labor. Almost never.
- A production rate. One bidder stated anchor locations per day and thereby made its own duration
  auditable. Nobody else did.
- The intended use of the system. This is the crucial silence and section 6 covers it.

Revision habits. The dominant behavior is that nothing changes: the same document is reissued months
later, one set stage on, with an identical price and only the letterhead date moved. That happened
across a nine month gap and a set change from one bidder, and a second bidder confirmed no change by
email rather than reissuing at all. When a price does move without a scope statement, the movement
can be large. A supply house reissued byte-identical quantities with the anchor rates escalated and
freight up by about a quarter, on the same quote number, which still encoded the original date.

The revision that matters is the one where the count moves. One manufacturer's anchor count fell by a
factor of nine between rounds on one building, with installation folding into the base at the same
time, and nothing in either document says a design decision was made. A total that falls by two
thirds looks like a saving and is a different system.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets in this trade have run around forty rows, which is generous for a package this small,
and the extra rows are all definition rows rather than work rows. That is the right instinct. Target
thirty to forty five lines and spend most of them on questions.

### What enumerates

- Anchor type, each its own line, with the connection named. End anchor, pass-through anchor and
  four-way anchor are three products at three prices in every material list seen. Bolt-through-joist,
  truss-wrap and deck-mount are three different installations against three different substrates.
- The horizontal lifeline, separately from the anchors, with the cable length and the number of
  termination or rigging sets. Bidders count these independently and they diverge more than the cable
  length does.
- Cable type, as its own decision line: standard cable requiring a lanyard versus a hands-free
  travelling system. This changes the hardware and the price and bidders answer it differently. One
  bidder disclosed it only in the covering email, and an estimator sheet carried it as a row with one
  bidder's cell left as a question mark.
- Davits, monorails, catwalks and tie-back-only anchors, each separately, wherever the facade access
  side is in play. A davit detail on the structural set and an anchor detail on the architectural set
  are two products, and one bidder excluded the davit detail by number while others priced past it.
- Engineering, PE stamp and shop drawings as one line; load testing as a second; the logbook,
  laminated as-built drawings and certificate for use as a third. All three are bought separately
  often enough to need separate rows, and the annual inspection and the ten year recertification are
  a fourth line that no base bid carries.
- Blocking and supports, as an explicit by-others row. Every bidder excludes it and it is real work
  with a real cost. See section 3's gap list.
- Hoisting to the roof. Excluded by every bidder seen.
- Thermal breaks or isolation pads at the anchor base. Excluded or unmentioned by every bidder, and a
  recurring plug.
- Mobilization count, as a row. Facade access manufacturers state theirs (typically one for
  installation and one for testing) and charge for extras; the installers do not mention it.
- Ballasted guardrail, if it is on this roof, as its own row with a note about which package carries
  it.

### What collapses

- Individual anchor locations. Nobody prices by location; the count is the quantity.
- Fasteners, backer plates, thimbles, turnbuckles, shock absorbers and clamps. These are attributes
  of the anchor or the lifeline kit, and even the itemized supply quotes bundle them into a named
  fastener or rigging package.
- Anchor height. It is an attribute, but see section 6: it is a contested attribute.
- Freight. One line, always.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

The test fires almost entirely on the definition questions rather than on the work. A candidate line
earns its row if a reasonable bidder could answer it two ways and the two answers produce different
systems. Applied here, that means the intended use, the cable type, the anchor connection, the anchor
height and who owns the layout all get rows, even though none of them is a scope item in the usual
sense. The physical work items mostly do not need rows: everybody carries the anchors and nobody
carries the blocking.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs a general contractor adds to every number before comparing:

1. Blocking, backer plates and any structural reinforcement at every anchor location, including
   engineering it. Excluded by every bidder seen, in every kind of company, and the anchor cannot be
   installed without it.
2. Hoisting anchors and cable to the roof. Excluded universally and plugged uniformly.
3. Thermal breaks or isolation pads at anchor bases. Not carried by anyone; a plug on a per-anchor
   rate, and on one project the quantity and rate came out of an RFI answer.
4. Roof flashing at every anchor penetration. Excluded here and generally excluded by the roofer too.
5. Opening and closing ceilings, and removing and reinstating fireproofing, where the connection is
   made from below. Excluded by everyone who mentions it.
6. Access to the roof levels during installation, and the safe working platform. Excluded by the
   facade access side explicitly.
7. Certification during construction, if the system is to be used by the trades before turnover.
   Excluded, and one manufacturer prices it separately and warns that the owner may want a second
   test at handover.
8. The parapet or guardrail that the anchorage system's OSHA compliance depends on.

A scope line set naming all eight is doing the plugging in advance.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

| Excluded by the anchor bidder | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Blocking, backer plates, gusset and stiffener plates, supplemental structure (every bidder seen) | Carpenter or steel fabricator, engineered by the engineer of record |
| Certification that the structure can take the load (every bidder seen) | Engineer of record; frequently unassigned in the bid documents |
| Roofing, flashing, sealing at the anchor (every bidder seen) | Roofer, and the roofer's own entry records the same seam from the other side |
| Hoisting and unloading (every bidder seen) | General contractor |
| Sealants, caulking, spun aluminum flashings, heat shrink, mastic tape | Roofer, frequently unassigned |
| Isolation coating between dissimilar metals and concrete, including bituminous paint | Roofer, by name |
| Spray foam insulation of the anchor body, thermal breaks | Insulation or unassigned |
| Removal and reinstatement of fireproofing | Fireproofing contractor |
| Opening and closing ceilings at each anchor location | Drywall, and a real gap on renovation work |
| Priming and painting of the products | Frequently unassigned |
| Safe access to all roof levels, ladders, hatches, guardrails | General contractor |
| Relocation of mechanical units, PV panels and drains that conflict with the layout | Frequently unassigned, and it is a coordination gap not a cost gap |
| Catwalks, and notching of catwalk grating for the anchors | Metals, and unassigned as often as not |
| A soil box where an anchor lands inside a planter | Landscaping, and almost nobody carries it |
| Skylight fall protection | Frequently unassigned |
| Standard harnesses and lanyards for the lifeline | Owner or general contractor |
| Annual inspection and ten year owner recertification | Owner, after turnover, and a real operating cost the bid conceals |
| Building permit and plan check fees | General contractor; one manufacturer notes no permit is required for a window washing system at all |
| Union labor, prevailing wage or certified payroll unless notified before acceptance | Rebids the whole number |

Read the list for the trade map, not for the project. The facade access manufacturers reproduce
thirty-odd numbered exclusions verbatim across projects, so the presence of any one of them carries
no project information.

What is project signal:

- An exclusion naming a specific detail by sheet and number. A bidder that writes out a detail
  reference read the drawings and found something it will not do, and it is worth an RFI.
- An exclusion justified by absence. One bidder stated that several building sections lacked
  sufficient detail to design an accurate solution and highlighted them on a preliminary layout
  rather than pricing them. Another stated that the specification was not provided at bid time.
  Both are documents defects, not bidder problems.
- A disclosed product departure. Anchor models differ in how they engage the structure, and a bidder
  whose product does not match the drawn detail sometimes says so and attaches its own model. That is
  the most useful paragraph in the proposal and it never appears in an exclusion list.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

- The layout is by others and is not a delegated design. The facade access manufacturers state this
  plainly, and then reserve the right to change equipment type and quantity if a delegated design is
  later required. So the count in the proposal is provisional by the bidder's own terms.
- Anchor height is estimated from the architectural plans and must be approved before fabrication by
  the architect, engineer of record, general contractor and roofer. One bidder makes its height
  contingent on receiving the insulation drawing. Two others state a required clearance above the
  finished membrane, typically eight inches for a plain anchor and about ten where the anchor carries
  a lifeline, with the connection element left exposed.
- The system is not intended for use by the construction trades. Stated by both facade access
  manufacturers seen and by the fabricators. If the trades are going to tie off to it during the
  build, that is a separate conversation with a separate cost and possibly a second load test.
- Anchor type, placement and quantity may change once installation begins, based on what is actually
  found in the structure. Stated outright by one bidder.
- Lines are assumed to pass under glass railings with a stated minimum gap, or extra equipment is
  needed to cantilever over them.
- The quotation supersedes all previous writings and agreements between the parties, and the
  exclusion list is to be incorporated into the contract documents. Standard manufacturer terms that
  subordinate the contract documents to the proposal.
- Commercial adders that only appear on the manufacturer side: owner controlled insurance program
  participation as a percentage of installation value, bond as a percentage rising with the term,
  payment portal participation as a percentage to a cap, tariff and steel escalation clauses. None of
  these are in the installers' bids, so the manufacturers look more expensive before any of it is
  applied and the gap widens once it is.
- Title to the anchors stays with one fabricator until paid in full.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

The anchor is the smallest object in construction with the most parties touching it.

The three-party roof seam. Another trade blocks the structure, this trade sets the anchor, and the
roofer flashes it. All three exclude the other two. The roofing entry records the same seam from its
side as a flash-only line count, and the anchor bidders here exclude roofing without exception. One
bidder goes further and states that its insurance forbids it from assisting, directing or supervising
the roofing at all, so it cannot even coordinate the interface it created. Every anchor is a
penetration, and the penetration count is the number to hand the roofer.

Blocking. Where the structure is wood, every anchor needs blocking, and every anchor bidder
excludes designing and installing it. One bidder supplied a photograph of the blocking box it wants;
another stated the bolt spacing the blocking must accommodate. That level of detail is what the
carpenter needs and it lives only in the anchor proposal, so it has to be extracted and handed over.

The engineer of record. Every bidder excludes certifying that the structure can take the load, and
the OSHA figure is five thousand pounds in any direction per anchorage. Somebody has to accept that
obligation and the bid documents frequently do not name who.

Parapet and guardrail. One manufacturer excludes the forty two inch parapet or guardrail by name
as an OSHA requirement. The anchorage system's compliance is therefore partly delivered by whoever
builds the parapet, and a system that passes its own load test can still leave the roof
non-compliant.

Ballasted guardrail. The free-standing counterweight guardrail is a fall protection product but
not a code-compliant permanent guardrail, and the supplier will say so and exclude the picket railing
that would be. It is bid inside this package sometimes and as its own package other times. The
metals trade carries it as its own scope.

Temporary access is a different package. Swing staging, hoists and overhead pedestrian protection
are sold by duration: erect, dismantle, trucking and a first rental period, then a daily rate. They
are driven by the same roof perimeter and the same facade, and the facade access manufacturers bid
the permanent side, which is why they get confused. They should never share a scope line with
permanent anchorage, and their cost behaves like a schedule variable rather than a quantity.

Renovation. On an existing building the anchors bolt through an existing slab or deck, the ceiling
below has to be opened and closed, fireproofing may have to come off and go back, and the product
family shifts toward davits. Access from below becomes the driver, and every one of those items is
excluded by the anchor bidder.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

The central fact of this trade is that the drawings do not fix the anchor count, and readers who
assume otherwise will produce a scope line that cannot be leveled.

What bidders price from:

1. An anchor layout plan, when one exists. It is issued as a separate sheet or as an enlarged roof
   plan, and it may be issued by the architect, by a consultant, or by one of the bidders. Where a
   bidder authored it, its own price is measured against its own design and no competitor is pricing
   the same thing.
2. The architectural roof plan and a typical anchor detail. Cited by nearly every bidder.
3. The structural set, for the attachment detail and any davit detail. Frequently a separate and
   conflicting reference.
4. The specification, mostly for the list of acceptable manufacturers. See below, because this list
   decides the award more often than price does.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- The intended use. Fall protection for the trades, permanent maintenance tie-off, rope descent
  window washing, or some combination. This single unstated question drives the anchor count by an
  order of magnitude. On one roof the facade access camp counted in the high thirties and the
  construction fall protection camp counted in the high three hundreds, with the same cable running
  roughly the same perimeter. One bidder priced fall protection only, excluded window washing by
  name, and told the contractor to verify the intended use. Another priced both camps a year apart
  and moved its own count by a factor of nine when the intent narrowed. If the documents do not say
  what the system is for, no bid on the sheet is comparable with any other, and that is a scope
  definition problem to route upstream, not a leveling problem to absorb.
- Whether the cable is standard or hands-free. Different hardware, different price, and bidders
  answer it silently.
- The anchor height, which depends on the finished insulation and membrane thickness, which is
  another package's information and is often not available when this package is bid.
- Whether the trades will use the system during construction, and whether a second load test at
  turnover is expected.
- Whether the specification's named manufacturer list is binding. It usually is, and it is the
  single most decisive fact in this trade. On one project the five named manufacturers were
  reproduced as rows on the leveling sheet, every bidder was scored against them, exactly one scored
  yes, and that bidder was bought at roughly twice the lowest total. Read the specification's
  manufacturer list before reading any price.
- Union, open shop, prevailing wage. One open shop bidder subcontracted installation to a union
  installer, so the labor market of the number and the labor market of the bidder were not the same.
- The mobilization count, and whether installation and testing are one trip or two.
- Whether the layout will be treated as a delegated design, which changes both the engineering scope
  and the bidder's liability.
- Lead time. The manufacturers ask for award roughly eighteen weeks before the on-site date and offer
  named ways to compress it: early approval of anchor cut sheets, partial shop drawing submission,
  use of stock materials, expediting at a cost, or casting block-outs to be backfilled later.

Set stage behavior. At design development the counts are at their highest and the design intent at
its loosest, and bidders quote supply-only with installation as an alternate. By construction
documents the counts have collapsed toward whichever intent got settled and installation is inside
the base. So a design development anchor number and a construction document anchor number for the
same building measure different systems, and a straight round-over-round comparison reads a design
decision as a saving.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, for anchors, split by anchor type. The primary unit.
- Linear feet, for lifeline cable.
- Each, for lifeline rigging kits, termination sets and hardware packages. Counted independently of
  the cable and diverging more than the cable does.
- Each, for mobilizations, on the manufacturer side.
- Anchor locations per day, for installation duration. Stated once and worth asking for every time,
  because it is the only bridge from a count to a schedule.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, insurance program participation and payment portal fees.
- Lump sums, for engineering, load testing and freight.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. The intended use of the system, which sets the anchor count. Nothing else in this trade is close.
2. Kind of company, which is largely the same variable arriving through a different door.
3. Whether the specification's manufacturer list binds. It can override price entirely.
4. Packaging mode. Supply only against supply and install is roughly a factor of two on the same
   equipment in the two menus seen, so a supply column and an install column cannot be ranked
   against each other.
5. Anchor type and connection. A truss-wrap or bolt-through anchor with blocking is a different
   product and a different installation from a deck-mounted self-drilling anchor.
6. Whether the cable is hands-free.
7. Union, prevailing wage or open shop, including where installation is subcontracted.
8. Mobilization count and whether testing is a separate trip.
9. The commercial adders on the manufacturer side.

On spread, and what it does not prove. The recorded spread on one leveling sheet was about
6.8 times between the lowest and highest ranked totals. Almost none of that is price. The denominator
was the low anchor count, so the bidders quoting nine times as many anchors were divided by the small
number and fell to the bottom of the ranking; the estimator annotated the top column as wrong scope
rather than expensive, and the general contractor's own budget from nine months earlier ranked second
from the bottom for the same reason. A spread of that size in this trade is a definition failure and
must be routed to scope definition, never compared. The mirror caution also holds: the cable length
converged to within a few percent across every bidder on that roof while the anchor count varied
ninefold, so a quantity that agrees does not license a comparison of the quantities that do not.
Count convergence and price convergence are independent here, and neither implies scope agreement.

Two further cautions on denominators. A leveling sheet's own quantity label may name a different
product from the one being priced, so read the label against the line items rather than trusting it.
And the labor type column tends to carry a packaging value such as supplier alongside the genuine
labor markets, which means the union and open shop comparison on that sheet is not like for like.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on around fifteen distinct proposals covering roof anchors and fall
protection, spanning four kinds of bidder, plus one estimator leveling sheet, drawn from two projects
in one regional market and bid by one general contractor. One project is a new mid-rise residential
building with a wood-framed roof structure; the other is a renovation of an existing building with a
composite slab. The proposals span design development, permit and construction document rounds, and
several bidders appear in more than one round on the same building, which is what makes the round
over round count collapse in section 6 observable rather than inferred.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Only one project carried a leveling sheet for this trade, so everything in section 7 about
  denominators, plugs and ranking behavior rests on that single sheet. It is a rich sheet and its
  lessons are internally consistent, but they are one estimator's practice.
- Both projects are residential and neither has a tall facade. Nothing here is tested against a
  high-rise where suspended maintenance equipment, davits, monorails and roof cars are the whole
  point of the package, and that is exactly the regime where the facade access manufacturers'
  full product range applies.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the specification's intended use was clearly stated. The
  central finding of this entry is an absence, and it has been observed on two buildings, not tested
  against a set that got it right.
- The corpus has not shown a signed subcontract or a buyout, so nothing here reveals which of the
  thirty-odd printed exclusions survive negotiation or what the blocking, hoisting and thermal break
  plugs actually cost when they are bought.
- The corpus has not shown a completed installation, so the claim that the anchor count changes once
  the structure is opened up is read out of the bidders' own qualifications rather than observed.
- Only one bidder stated an installation production rate, so the anchor-locations-per-day figure in
  section 7 rests on one document and should be treated as a question to ask rather than a norm.
- No davit, monorail, catwalk or roof car scope is priced in detail anywhere in the corpus, though
  several proposals exclude or reference them. The renovation project's davit layout is on drawing
  pages that resist extraction.
- The load test values recorded here span a wide range against the same nominal standard, and the
  corpus does not contain a specification passage that settles which value governs. Treat the
  proof-test load as an open question on every project until the specification is read.
