# Millwork

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the custom
architectural millwork package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and
estimator leveling sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is,
is carried by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real millwork shops can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

One thing governs everything below, and it is the mirror image of what governs unit casework. A
cabinet supplier is handed a designed kitchen and quietly prices a catalogue; the gap between the
drawn design and the manufactured product is where its money hides. A millwork shop has no catalogue.
It prices what is drawn, elevation by elevation, and it will tell you which elevation it priced. So
geometry is not the risk here. The risk is everything the elevation does not settle: what grade of
material the shop assumed, who builds the structure behind the piece, whether the stone on top is in
or out, and whether the number includes anyone standing in the room installing it. Two millwork
numbers that look far apart are almost never two prices for the same work.

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Section 06 22 00 is what every leveling sheet uses and it is the reliable anchor. A proposal that
names a section at all is more likely to name the interior architectural woodwork section in the
06 40 range, which is where the specification actually lives when there is one. Neither number is a
strong identifier. The trade token is stronger, and even the token lies often enough that opening the
file is not optional.

The package as bid is custom shop fabrication for the front of house: reception and leasing desks,
amenity bars, mail room walls and mailbox surrounds, banquettes, wall and soffit panelling, cased
opening portals, floating counters and shelving, work room and fitness casework, feature slat walls,
and the trim and caps that tie them together. It is a fabricate-to-order trade with a shop drawing
cycle and a real lead time, and almost every commercial term in a proposal exists to protect that
cycle.

Three packages sit adjacent and are constantly confused with this one. Keeping them separate is the
first job of a millwork scope list.

- Unit casework. Modular, factory built, priced per unit, usually supply only, Division 12.
- Finish carpentry. The building's install labor hub, which installs what four other packages
  furnish and itself furnishes little beyond running trim and closet shelving.
- Millwork. Custom, shop drawn, shop finished, usually furnish and install, and priced against the
  interior design elevations.

The same firm will bid all three and send all three in one email as separate proposals with separate
numbers. Beware of the same item being claimed by two packages, and beware of the reverse: a room
that sits in whichever proposal is bought and is missed if only one is awarded. The clearest case is
a back of house work room, priced by one fabricator as a small add on its millwork proposal with a
written warning that the cost would have to move to its casework proposal if the cabinets were bought
there, and priced by a competitor as custom millwork at roughly four times that. Same room, same set,
two packages, and the difference is entirely which document it landed in.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another
division or another spec section:

- Countertops. The strongest attractor and the most contested. See section 5.
- Countertop subtops, in-wall support brackets and concealed outrigger supports, sink cutouts and
  backsplashes. These travel with the top, so they land here whenever the top does and fall in the
  gap whenever it does not. Some shops carry the supports as counted lines even while excluding the
  stone.
- Die wall framing at islands and peninsulas, and planter carcasses behind banquettes. Sometimes
  carried by the fabricator, sometimes offered as a credit if the framer builds them instead.
- Unit entry door trim and transom panels, and typical elevator transom panels and jambs at every
  residential level. Almost always offered as a separate priced option rather than in the base,
  because they are the one repetitive high-count item in an otherwise one-off trade.
- Mailbox build-out structure and its trim, with the boxes themselves excluded.
- Amenity and common area baseboard, where the mark is a stained or veneered wood base. Sometimes
  offered as a substitution for a specified rubber base.
- Elevator cab handrails and cab woodwork. Usually excluded, occasionally carried as counted items
  with stated lengths.
- Upholstery, fabric and leather at banquettes and window seats, and the structure under them.
- Specialty vendor products set into a millwork piece: decorative metal shelving, glass shelves,
  concrete or sintered panels at a desk face, resin panels.
- Grommets, pulls and hardware that the specification requires and no drawing shows.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like woodwork:

- Standing and running trim throughout the residential units and corridors. This is finish carpentry
  and every millwork proposal says so.
- Doors, frames, hardware, casing, sills and primed baseboard, including doors carrying a millwork
  finish mark.
- Wallcoverings, ceilings and all acoustic tile, even where they meet a millwork piece.
- Metal trims and reveals, wall protection panels, felt and acoustic wall panels.
- The stone itself, in most cases.
- Mirrors, glass and glazing, unless a specific mirror is called out inside a millwork frame.
- Signage, wayfinding, custom graphics and menu boards.
- Appliances and equipment, including the panels that conceal them in some proposals and not others.

## 2. How the market bids

Millwork is the most enumerated trade on the bid list and one of the least priced. The default output
is a complete room by room, element by element listing with a drawing reference on every row, and a
handful of commercial numbers underneath it. Maximum scope transparency, minimum price transparency.
Where painting gives you one number and no scope, millwork gives you the whole scope and one number.

Four proposal shapes recur.

- Room and element enumeration with commercial totals. The most common by a wide margin. Level and
  room headings, then one row per element carrying its finish mark, its dimensions and the sheet and
  detail it was counted from, then supply, transport and installation as the only prices.
- Room by room with plan, elevation and detail reference columns, and a qualified-as note under most
  elements naming what the shop actually carried. The most useful shape for a reader, because the
  notes are where the substitutions live.
- Numbered elevation with a measured narrative. Each row is one elevation, described in prose that
  carries real dimensions and counts: the length of a desk, the number of drawers, the square feet of
  wall panels, the linear feet of curving trim. Quantities are present and readable, just not summed.
- Room lump. One price per room or per elevation face with no element detail. Usually from a firm
  whose main business is unit cabinetry.

Beneath whichever shape, the commercial split is the trade's signature and it is nearly universal:
supply, transport, and installation with unloading and distribution, as three separate numbers.
Some shops publish a furnish subtotal and an install subtotal as the proposal's own headline. Read
those three numbers before anything else, because the base bid a leveling sheet records may be any
one of them or their sum.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The drawing set and its date, nearly always, and often distinguishing the plan date from the
  specification date.
- The woodwork institute grade it built to, commonly custom grade, usually paired with an exclusion
  of the certification stamps and seals and a note that it does build to the standards. Grade and
  certification are two different purchases and the market separates them cleanly.
- The actual product it will supply against each finish mark, named. This is the trade's most
  valuable habit and it is what makes a conformance comparison possible at all.
- The sheet and detail reference on essentially every priced row.
- Lead time in weeks, measured from the last of approved shop drawings, guaranteed field dimensions,
  finish approvals and hardware selection.
- The deposit and release structure, in real detail.
- Truck counts and trailer lengths.
- Price validity, thirty to ninety days, with escalation stated after.
- Labor basis, union or open shop, more often than in most trades, because on amenity work it is
  frequently union and the shop wants that on the record.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- A price against any individual element. Every element is listed and none is priced.
- Labor hours, ever.
- The material fraction of a furnish and install number, unless the proposal happens to split them.
- A summable quantity schedule. The counts are there, embedded in the row list or in prose, but
  nobody totals them for you.
- What it assumed about the structure behind the piece, unless it is excluding it.

Exclusion behavior does not split this market. Nearly every proposal carries a long exclusion list.
What matters is that in this trade the exclusion list is worth reading closely rather than skimming
as boilerplate, because a large part of it names specific finish marks and specific rooms, and those
lines are a documents report rather than a legal shield. See section 4.

Revision habits are more varied here than in any sibling trade, and several of the mechanisms move
the headline number in the wrong direction:

- Set change re-issue. The commonest, and usually a few percent.
- Allowance converted to a quoted price and re-presented as its own section. The base falls, a new
  section appears, and the real offer rises. A reader diffing base totals records a saving that did
  not happen.
- Mode change. A shop that quoted furnish and install comes back furnish only, at a total above the
  previous total. Scope shrank and the number grew.
- Breakout restructure at the contractor's request, where rooms leave the base and reappear as
  add alternates.
- Descope call with value disclosure, where the contractor asks what a room is worth and the shop
  answers. Two shops answering the same enquiry may answer differently: one removes the room from its
  base, the other discloses the room's value and leaves it in. Their bases no longer cover the same
  scope and nothing on the page says so.
- Alternate only revision, where the base does not move and the whole revision is new alternates
  answering a commercial questionnaire.
- Package departure, where a scope leaves because an owner's vendor took it.
- Silent reprice, where the number moves and nothing else does.
- Specification degradation while the price rises. Across one ladder the cabinet interiors moved from
  veneer of a compatible species stained to match, to matching laminate, to melamine in an
  unspecified colour, the certified veneer claim was replaced by a weaker low-formaldehyde claim, and
  none of it was flagged as a deviation. Diff the inclusion paragraph, not just the total.
- Commercial basis restatement. Payment platform fees and sales tax fold into the total partway up a
  ladder, so later round totals measure something the earlier ones did not.

Two warnings about revisions specifically. First, filename revision labels in this trade are
unreliable to the point of being misleading. A file with no revision label may be the fourth
revision; a file labelled as the first may be the fifth and one labelled the second may be the sixth,
with two rounds missing entirely. Always read the document's own revision marking and its cited basis
date. Second, the substantive negotiation happens by email and often exists only inside a bundled
PDF. The most informative exchange of a whole ladder may be a contractor asking what is driving the
price and a shop answering that the cost is the designer's finish selections and that the deduct
being requested does not exist, because the specified veneer is only available in the certified
form. That answer is not in any proposal.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Millwork scope sheets have run 45, 53, 69 and 81 rows. Target roughly fifty five to eighty five scope
lines for a full amenity millwork package, and go to the top of that range where the project has a
large front of house, a renovation component, or a stone package that has not been settled.

The primary organizing axis is the room, named and numbered as the architect numbers it. The
secondary axis is the element within the room. Every row carries the interior design detail reference
it came from. This is how the shops bid and it is how every estimator sheet is built, so adopt it. A
millwork scope line without a room and a detail reference cannot be answered.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every amenity and front of house room, by the architect's room number and name, including the rooms
  that turn out to have no millwork. Recording the no-scope rooms is half the work: it is the only
  way to test a bidder's room list, and bidders' room lists come up short often enough to make the
  test worth running.
- Every element within a room, with its finish mark and its governing detail. A room line alone hides
  the argument, because two shops will price a lounge to mean different things.
- Supply, transport, unloading, distribution and installation, separately. These are five answers
  from potentially different parties and the trade already prices at least three of them separately.
- Countertops, split further: the finished top, the subtop, the in-wall support brackets, the cutouts
  and the backsplash. Each is answered differently by different shops and each falls in the gap on
  somebody.
- In-wall blocking and structural supports, naming who installs them and when relative to drywall.
- The structure behind the piece, as its own line for each condition: die walls at islands, planter
  carcasses behind banquettes, furred back walls behind curved millwork, framing for faux beams,
  framing at a fireplace surround, framing under a restroom vanity. Every one of these is excluded by
  someone and presumed by everyone.
- Banquettes, split three ways: seat and back structure, upholstery labor, and the fabric or leather
  itself. The fabric is a materials decision owned by the designer and can move a proposal materially
  on its own.
- Curved and radiused work, broken by face: front face, convex corner, return face, concave corner,
  and the furred back wall behind it. Shops draw the line at different faces and say so.
- Unit entry trim and transom panels, as a per-each item against the door count, and typical elevator
  transom panels and jambs by level range. These belong on the sheet whether or not the base carries
  them, because they are large and they are almost always outside the base.
- Portals and cased openings, with their dimensions, and separately the question of whether a portal
  is millwork or finished gypsum board by others. That fork is worth a great deal and neither answer
  is wrong on the face of the drawings.
- Mailbox build-out and its trim, separate from the boxes.
- Amenity and common area baseboard by mark, split between marks that arrive shop finished and marks
  that arrive primed for field paint. That split is the trade boundary, not a finish preference.
- Elevator cab interiors and cab handrails, as their own line.
- Shop drawings, field measurement, finish samples, templates and mock-ups, each its own line with a
  round count stated. The shop drawing set becomes the governing scope after award, so how many
  review rounds are included is a real commercial term and one shop may exclude shop drawings
  altogether.
- Protection after installation, and it is worth pricing per scope section rather than once, because
  that is how it is quoted.
- Storage, per truck week or per container month.
- Items the specification requires that no drawing shows. Trash grommets and wire grommets are the
  recurring pair: required by the woodwork specification, absent from every elevation, and priced by
  the shops as an add outside the contract value.
- Material and certification compliance as its own block: low formaldehyde, certified wood and
  veneer, fire retardant treatment, and any project certification programme. Each is a material
  substitution with a real price, and on some projects the premium is small while on others the
  deduct is impossible.
- A finish mark conformance block, one row per mark, with the specified attribute stated and each
  bidder's actual product language transcribed into its cell. The richest estimator sheets devote
  seventeen consecutive rows to this and it is where the real leveling happens.
- Commercial rows: bond, general liability where no owner-controlled program applies, payment
  platform fees, warranty term, tariff confirmation and a price hold date. Contractors now ask all of
  these by questionnaire, so put them on the sheet and get them answered in one structure.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual cabinet boxes collapse into the room element they belong to. No millwork proposal prices
  box by box and no purpose is served by asking.
- Construction attributes are attributes, not lines. Frameless against face frame, overlay style,
  interior material and colour, hinge and slide type, edgebanding and drawer construction all belong
  in the conformance block.
- Levels collapse entirely for amenity work, because amenity rooms are individual. They matter only
  for the repetitive items at residential floors, where a level range is the right grain.
- Unit by unit enumeration collapses to a per-each rate against a count. Unit entry trim at forty or
  two hundred doors is one line with a count, not a line per door.
- Veneer species, flitch and match pattern collapse into the mark, unless a match requirement changes
  the price, in which case it becomes an alternate. Sequence matching across panels is a real cost
  and is excluded by name often enough to deserve a row of its own.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable shop could carry it, exclude it, or be silent
about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to millwork
that test fires hardest on the countertop assembly, on the structure behind every piece, and on the
mode. It fires hardly at all on geometry, which is what makes this trade different from its siblings.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding, and in this trade silence is almost always a
finding. Because the shops enumerate, an element that does not appear in a proposal's room list was
not priced. A room that does not appear was not visited. Check every bidder's room list against the
plans before comparing anything, and expect at least one shop to be missing a room the others
carried.

### The structural gap list

An estimator does not compare base bids in this trade either. It compares base bid plus adjustments,
and the adjustments here have a distinctive source: they are usually assembled from a competitor's
own priced lines. Because one shop prices the unit entry panels and another does not, the one that
did becomes the price of that scope for everybody. Where a scope line exists in nobody's base, an
estimator plugs a figure of its own and writes it in the cell.

The plugs to expect, in rough order of how often they are needed:

1. Installation, unloading and distribution, wherever a shop bid furnish only.
2. Countertops, subtops, support brackets and cutouts.
3. Unit entry trim and transom panels, and elevator transom panels.
4. The structure behind the millwork: planter carcasses, die walls, faux beam framing, fireplace and
   mail island framing, vanity framing.
5. Metal trims and reveals adjacent to millwork.
6. Felt, acoustic and fabric wall panels.
7. Protection after installation, and storage if the site is not ready.
8. Amenity and common area baseboard, where the trim package excluded it and the shops did too.
9. Elevator cab interiors and handrails.
10. Bond, general liability, payment platform fees and the second warranty year.
11. Shop drawings and engineering, where a bidder excluded them.
12. In-wall blocking and brackets.

A scope line set that names all twelve explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging in advance.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The product grade declaration, which is where the real information is

In a stock trade the highest-value paragraph is the list of places the catalogue does not match the
drawing. In this trade the geometry does match, so the equivalent paragraph is the one where the shop
declares what grade of material it assumed everywhere the documents were silent. The most disciplined
shops publish this as a standing block, and it is reproduced word for word from project to project:
laminate priced at a standard finish variant from one of four named commercial lines; solid surface
priced at a base colour group; integral sinks priced at one specific model in one colour group;
countertop support brackets priced at one specific part in unfinished mill aluminium; adjustable
shelving standards and brackets priced at one specific series in stock colours; hardwood priced as
maple in clear coat; casework priced as full overlay with white melamine interiors and named pulls
and hinges. Every one of those defaults sits below what an interior designer usually intends, and
each carries an unless-otherwise-noted escape. Where a proposal contains no such block, the
assumptions are still there and simply unstated.

The second recurring shape is the availability finding, and it is common enough to expect one on any
project with a designed finish palette. Specified stone colours turn out to be discontinued.
Specified quartzite is made in one thickness and the finish schedule lists another. A specified
through-counter grommet does not exist in the specified metal because that manufacturer only makes
brass. A specified cabinet pull is discontinued. Decorative metal carries a minimum sheet order and a
pending freight quote. A veneer's certified version cannot be dissociated from the veneer. Each of
these is the shop reading the finish schedule against the market and finding a defect, and each is
worth an RFI rather than a substitution accepted quietly.

The third shape is the invented fill. Where the documents identify millwork without defining it, a
shop will price something and say what it priced: a pair of cabinets at a vestibule that has a plan
symbol and no elevation, thirty five shelves of a stated depth with a stated lip in a market space
with no design intent, a pull assumed at a size and finish the schedule never gave. Read those as
quantity claims to verify, not as inclusions to accept.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Repeat bidders reproduce their general exclusion blocks and their product grade declarations verbatim
across projects and across markets, down to the part numbers and the colour groups. Repeat bidders
make up a good share of any millwork list. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else:

- Any exclusion that names a finish mark or a mark range. When a shop writes out the specific marks
  it will not carry, it read the finish schedule.
- Any exclusion carrying a stated reason and a sheet reference. The recurring reasons are that no
  millwork is shown at a location the contractor believes has millwork, that the structure is
  presumed to be the framer's, and that two sheets disagree. Each of those is a documents defect and
  the exclusion list is, in effect, the project's RFI log written by the market.
- A named contradiction the shop refuses to resolve. A shop may observe that the sustainability
  specification, the casework specification and other documents give contradicting determinations for
  formaldehyde and certification, and ask for direction rather than pricing one side. That refusal is
  more valuable than a resolution would have been.
- A disclosed conflict where the shop did pick a side and said so. Priced per the elevation where the
  finish plan disagreed about the extent of panelling; priced per the elevation where the drawing
  notes counted three trash pull-outs and the elevation showed two; priced the section detail where
  the specification asked for interiors matching the exposed finish; priced the rendering where the
  finish notes scheduled a metallic paint the rendering did not show. When a shop resolves a conflict
  between two documents by picking one, that resolution is a scope decision the contractor never
  made.
- A room list that can be checked. Where a shop enumerates the rooms it carried, count them against
  the plans.
- A disclosure that the documents name a specific fabricator as the source of a cabinet family. It
  happens, and every other bidder has to substitute its own fabrication and disclose it.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the contract into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Shop drawings are not started without an executed contract or purchase order, and lead time runs
  from the last of approved shops, guaranteed field dimensions, finish approvals and hardware
  selection. Ten to fourteen weeks is normal for domestic production and sixteen to twenty for
  overseas, with field dimensions guaranteed sixteen weeks ahead of a contracted ship date. Nearly
  universal, and it puts the millwork buyout on the critical path far earlier than its installation
  date suggests.
- The client guarantees the hold-to dimensions approved on the shop drawings. This is the single most
  consequential clause in the trade: after award the shop drawings, not the contract documents, are
  the governing scope, and a dimension the contractor confirmed is the contractor's.
- The number of shop drawing rounds included, typically one issue plus one redline round plus an
  as-built set, with further redrafting chargeable. A minority exclude shop drawings and engineering
  entirely, which is an unusual and expensive position in a fabricate-to-order trade.
- Temperature and humidity controlled to the woodworking standard before material arrives, and the
  site ready for installation. Otherwise the warranty against warping, cracking and peeling is waived.
- Full truckload or container deliveries only, with an unloading window measured in hours per truck
  or per container and additional shipping charges beyond it. Access within a stated distance of the
  building, and a stated maximum distance from trailer to hoist.
- Hoist, forklift or elevator access, and an on-site dumpster, provided by the contractor free of
  charge. Nearly unanimous.
- Floors level within a quarter inch in ten feet, walls plumb and square, ceiling level.
- Ceilings, wall finishes, rough electrical and rough plumbing complete before millwork installation
  begins.
- In-wall blocking supplied and installed by the contractor as indicated on the shop drawings, which
  means the shop owes a blocking layout and the framer owes the blocking, months apart.
- Continuous uninterrupted installation with one mobilisation. Changes to the schedule inside a
  stated window before the contracted delivery incur remobilisation, travel and storage charges.
- A sign-off within a day or two of completing a unit or an area, after which damage and theft stop
  being the shop's. Protection, where offered, is a one-time application conditioned on the
  contractor signing that the millwork is undamaged first, and replacement of previously signed-off
  work is charged back.
- A staged deposit ladder tied to procurement milestones rather than to progress. Percentages against
  submittals and shop drawings, then a large tranche funded a stated number of weeks before first
  delivery to buy long lead material and a second tranche closer in for domestic material, with the
  balance released by truck. Where production is overseas the ladder is blunter, commonly half at
  fabrication order, a large share before shipment, and a small share on delivery. Deposits of a
  third to a half of contract value before anything arrives are the norm, and on specialty materials
  a shop may require payment up front regardless.
- Price validity of thirty to ninety days with escalation after, and on imported material an explicit
  tariff clause holding prices to conditions at a stated date.
- The bid conditions supersede any conflicting clause in the purchasing documents. Stated flatly by
  more than one shop, and it means a scope written as compliance with the specification may be
  subordinated to the shop's own terms.
- A commercial addendum on indemnity, liquidated damages, arbitration and payment timing. Invisible
  on a price-only leveling sheet and materially changes what the low number is worth.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### Mode: the first question on every line

Millwork is more often furnish and install than its siblings, but the exceptions are large enough
that mode has to be asked explicitly. The shapes seen:

1. Furnish and install as one contract, with supply, transport and installation priced separately
   inside it. The most common shape by a wide margin.
2. Furnish only, with the shop stating plainly that it will work alongside whichever installers the
   contractor chooses. When a shop switches into this mode between rounds, field measurement,
   unloading, distribution and installation all leave the scope together, and the remaining number
   can still be higher than the previous whole.
3. Furnish and install with installation quoted as a separate option the contractor may decline.
   Common, and the cleanest to level, because the option is a real number from a party that has to
   honour it.
4. Different modes for different families inside one proposal. A combined carpentry and millwork
   bidder may be install-only on doors and cabinetry, furnish-only on trim material, and furnish and
   install on shelving and on amenity woodwork, each with its own price.
5. Installation by the finish carpenter, with the fabricator furnishing only. This is the boundary
   the carpentry package describes from its own side, and it changes project to project.

Install runs roughly a fifth to a third of a furnish and install total on concentrated amenity work.
It runs higher, and can exceed the supply cost outright, on scattered repetitive items at residential
floors such as unit entry trim and transom panels, where the labor is spread across a hundred rooms
instead of a dozen.

### Countertops: the seam that decides the number

Stone and solid surface at a millwork piece appear in four commercial shapes and the drawings will
not tell you which one applies:

1. Excluded outright, with every cabinet row annotated that the top is not in contract. The most
   common position by a wide margin, and it is why so many millwork proposals read as unfinished.
2. Offered as its own separately priced section with its own supply and installation lines. Common
   from the shops that fabricate their own tops, and the cleanest answer available.
3. Offered as an add alternate covering the whole mark range. Occasionally, and the alternate can
   approach the size of the entire millwork base bid, which makes ranking on base bids meaningless.
4. Carried inside the base, brackets and substrate and finished top together. A minority, and worth
   recognising because that bidder's number answers a materially larger question than its
   competitors'.

The same firm may draw the line differently in two sections of one document, carrying tops on its
amenity millwork and excluding them on its unit cabinetry. Do not infer a company position from one
paragraph.

What falls in the gap between the millwork and the stone:

- In-wall countertop support brackets and concealed outrigger supports. The millwork shop commonly
  excludes them and the stone fabricator commonly excludes them too. Some millwork shops carry them
  as counted lines, which is the cleanest resolution available, and it matters because in-wall
  supports have to be installed before the drywall closes. That decision precedes both trades'
  mobilisation by months.
- Subtops. Frequently carried by the millwork shop as its own line even where the finished top is
  not, which is the right split and worth asking for explicitly.
- Sink cutouts, splashes and grommets at stone.
- The distinction between a countertop fabricated from stone and one fabricated from veneer or
  laminate. Some rooms have both and a single countertop line will be ambiguous.

### The structure behind the piece: the framer seam

This is the seam most likely to be unassigned at buyout, because it is invisible on an interior
elevation. A millwork elevation shows a finished face; something has to hold it up.

- Curved and radiused millwork. Shops supply the front face, the convex corner and the return face,
  and exclude the furred-out back wall and the concave corner behind it. Two different trades either
  side of a corner nobody drew a section through.
- Banquette planter carcasses and build-outs. Carried by some shops, excluded by others, and offered
  as a credit by a shop willing to let the framer build them. Where that credit is taken, the framing
  and wallcovering costs have to be added back, and the shops say so in writing.
- Die walls at islands and peninsulas. Sometimes carried by the millwork shop as framing, more often
  by the framer.
- Faux beams, fireplace surrounds, mail islands and restroom vanity framing. Recurringly excluded on
  the stated ground that no millwork is shown and the structure is presumed to be the framer's. If
  the framing package does not carry them, nobody does.
- In-wall blocking generally, which the shop will locate on its shop drawings and will not install.

### Electrical, and the things set into millwork

Lighting, wiring, data and cable at or in a millwork piece are excluded by everyone. The shop will
coordinate outlet locations in a banquette base or a desk and will neither supply nor install them,
and where an outlet product is unspecified a budget allowance appears with supply only stated
explicitly. Undercabinet and integral lighting is a live gap on amenity work exactly as it is on unit
casework.

Specialty products set into a millwork piece are their own small seam. Decorative metal shelving,
glass shelves, sintered or concrete desk panels and resin panels are sometimes carried by the shop as
a purchased item and sometimes excluded to the vendor. A shop that carries one names the vendor, and
that naming is what tells you the item exists at all.

### The sibling packages

- Unit casework. The boundary that moves most. A back of house work room, an amenity kitchen, a
  package room or a leasing office may be priced as unit casework by one bidder and as custom
  millwork by another, at very different numbers, because one is a catalogue product and one is a
  shop fabrication. Ask every bidder which of its own proposals a given room sits in.
- Finish carpentry. The clean rule that the market actually uses is the finish: what arrives shop
  finished, stained or veneered stays with millwork, and what arrives primed for field paint goes to
  carpentry. A project may carry two baseboard marks that differ only in that respect and split them
  between the two packages accordingly. Where the marks are not clearly split, both packages will
  exclude the base and the sheet has to say who has it.
- Painting. Because millwork arrives finished, the painter excludes it, which every painting proposal
  confirms. But the millwork shop also excludes nail hole filling, caulking, and field painting of
  the primed items it does supply, and excludes caulking to adjacent and dissimilar surfaces. So the
  joint between a millwork piece and the wall belongs to nobody unless a scope line assigns it.
- Elevators. Cab interiors are usually excluded by the millwork shop and are not obviously the
  elevator contractor's either. Cab handrails and transom panels at cab openings are the two items
  most likely to be carried by a millwork shop, and a wood handrail mark inside a cab is a finish
  mark that may belong to a different package entirely.
- Specialties. Mailboxes, extinguisher cabinets and package shelving are excluded, while the
  millwork surround, topper and build-out around them are carried. That split is the source of a
  recurring argument about the mailbox wall.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

### What millwork shops actually price from, in descending order of what they cite

1. The interior design elevations and details. This is the primary document and it is unusual: no
   sibling trade prices primarily off interior design sheets. Detail references appear on essentially
   every priced row, and a shop's whole scope statement is often the set of details it answered. If
   the interior design package is incomplete, this trade cannot be bid at all, which is why so many
   proposals carry a standing caveat that the quotation is a budget price due to missing details and
   elevations. That caveat survives into construction document rounds.
2. The finish schedule and its mark set. Wood, laminate, stone, paint, metal, glass, fabric, mirror
   and hardware marks are what the shop quotes against and what its substitutions are stated against.
3. Room numbers and names from the floor plans, which are the scope's organizing axis.
4. Renderings. Genuinely load-bearing here in a way they are nowhere else. Shops cite renderings as
   the drawing reference on priced rows, and will resolve a conflict between a finish note and a
   rendering by pricing the rendering. A rendering is functioning as a contract document in this
   trade whether anyone intended that or not.
5. The specification, when there is one, mostly for grade, hardware, grommets and certification
   requirements. It is common for a project to have no millwork specification at all, and shops say
   so plainly.
6. Any narrative or change-summary document, rarely.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff supplied by anyone else. They take off the elevations
themselves, and the enumeration in the proposal is that takeoff.

### Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number

- Whether the package is furnish and install or furnish only, and who installs if not the shop.
- Union against open shop. The same fabricator bids open shop on one project and union on another in
  the same market, and the premium reads at roughly an eighth. Where a project carries a municipal
  hiring policy, that is a labor condition the drawings never mention.
- Whether the stone is in the millwork package, a separate package, or nobody's.
- Whether the structure behind each piece is the framer's, and whether the framing package knows it.
- The grade of material assumed everywhere the finish schedule is silent, and whether the specified
  products are still available in the specified colour and thickness.
- Whether certified wood, low formaldehyde material or a project certification programme is required.
  The premium is a function of the specified species: on a project drawn in a common domestic
  hardwood the certified upgrade is a small stated add, and on a project drawn in exotic veneers the
  deduct for dropping certification does not exist at all, because those veneers are only made
  certified. Never assume a certification line is a paperwork line.
- Lead time, and whether the project can wait ten to twenty weeks after the last approval.
- The shop drawing cycle: how many rounds are included, who pays for further revisions, when the shop
  will start, and who guarantees the dimensions.
- The deposit and release structure, and whether the project can fund a third to a half of the
  contract before anything arrives.
- Whether the site can take full trailers, how long the unloading window is, and whether a hoist and
  a dumpster are free.
- Whether the site will be ready when the millwork is, and who pays storage if it is not.
- Whether the schedule is continuous or phased, and how many mobilisations that implies.
- Whether protection after installation is expected, who maintains and removes it, and who owns
  damage once the contractor has signed off.
- Whether a mock-up, attic stock, templates or a building model are required. Every shop asks the
  contractor to define these rather than guessing.
- Tariff conditions and the currency of the price, on a trade where a meaningful share of the market
  fabricates offshore or across a border.
- Warranty term. One year is the market default. Contractors increasingly ask for two in the base
  with a one-year deduct, and shops answer in whichever structure they prefer.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance programme applies, and whether the shop will offer a credit
  for it. Some will not offer one in either direction.

### The scope definition problem

This is the single largest lever a contractor has in this trade, and it is worth stating on its own.
Where a contractor issues a real scope sheet, the bids converge and the answers become row by row.
Where it does not, every shop invents its own boundary, and the resulting spread is a coverage signal
rather than a price signal. On a package where the contractor authored the question in advance,
including a tiered option ladder for a repetitive item with its own per-each rates, the answers came
back in a form that could be compared directly. On a package where the leveling sheet asked for three
breakouts and every bidder left them empty, the sheet's adjustment row stayed at zero and nothing was
levelled at all.

### Set-stage behavior

At design development, shops price around missing information and label it: budget pricing due to
missing details, allowances for upholstery where no section is drawn, invented cabinets at a plan
symbol with no elevation, a per-each budget for hardware that is shown but not specified. Treat every
allowance line at design development as a placeholder rather than a quantity.

By construction documents the same shops switch to citing detail numbers on every row and their
qualified-as notes multiply, but the missing-details caveat frequently survives, because interior
design packages lag the architectural set. Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its folder's
set stage disagree often enough to check, and that a cited basis date can itself be malformed, in
which case the round cannot be ordered by its own stated basis and the document's revision marking is
all you have.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- The room, which is the natural unit of amenity millwork and the one every enumerated proposal and
  every estimator sheet organizes on.
- The element, counted as each: cabinets by type and width, panels, shelves, portals, drawers,
  grommets, pulls, mirrors, banquette structures, floating counters, handrails.
- Linear feet, for countertops, wall trim, curving trim, wall caps and baseboard.
- Square feet, for wall panelling, portal panels and veneer.
- Front of house square feet, as a normalizing denominator for the whole package. Used where the
  amenity area is the scope.
- The residential unit, as the normalizing denominator on other projects with the same trade. The
  denominator changes by project inside this one trade, so a cost per unit from one job and a cost
  per front of house square foot from another are not comparable and neither converts to the other
  without the area.
- The unit entry or the door opening, as a per-each denominator for the repetitive residential-floor
  items.
- The truckload, by trailer length, for transport. Counts in the low single digits are normal.
- The container month and the truck week, for storage.
- Linear yards for upholstery fabric and hides for leather, which are the textile supply chain's
  units and match nothing else on the project.
- Weeks, for lead time and for the funding milestones ahead of first delivery.
- Hours, for the unloading window.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, general liability, payment platform fees and escalation caps.
  The only way any of these is ever expressed.

Cost drivers. In this trade the commercial and boundary questions outrank the physical ones, so they
are ranked that way:

1. Whether installation is in the number, and whether unloading and distribution came with it.
2. Whether the countertops are in, out, or a separate priced section. On some packages the stone
   alternate approaches the size of the millwork base bid.
3. Union against open shop for the field labor.
4. The designer's material selections, which is the largest driver of the supply side and the one the
   shops name explicitly when asked why a number is high. Exotic veneers, high-end laminates, fabrics
   priced per yard at several hundred and leather priced per hide will each move a package more than
   any quantity decision, and a shop asked to explain its price will point at the finish schedule
   rather than at its own rates.
5. How much scope the shop actually carried, which is to say how many rooms and how many of the
   boundary items in section 3 it answered. This outranks price for most of any observed spread.
6. Certification and material compliance, where the specified species makes it expensive or
   impossible.
7. The structure-behind items, which are large when carried and invisible when not.
8. Unit entry and elevator transom panels, which are the trade's one high-count scope and are usually
   outside the base.
9. Veneer matching requirements, sequence and blueprint matching.
10. Shop drawing rounds, engineering and templates.
11. Freight, truck count and tariff exposure on offshore or cross-border fabrication.
12. Protection, storage, phasing and remobilisation, which bite only when the schedule slips.
13. Bond, general liability, payment platform fees and the second warranty year.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Across a whole raw ranking the spread runs very
wide, seen at about 3.5, 7.7 and 9.4 times, but almost none of that is price. Once the entries that
are not millwork bids at all are removed, the genuine whole-package band tightens to roughly 1.9 and
2.3 times, and on one package the two shops that answered the same scope landed within a few percent
of each other after plugging. Bidder counts per project run from about three to about six companies.

Three cautions on reading that range, and they matter more here than in most trades.

First, a row in a millwork ranking is a round, not a company. It is normal for one shop to occupy
three rows of a nine-row ranking and a second shop to occupy two more, so a bidder count taken off the
ranking can overstate the real list by half. Confirm which rows are the same firm before drawing any
conclusion about competition.

Second, the bottom of the ranking is usually the narrowest scope and frequently is not a millwork bid
at all. Expect to find on a millwork sheet: a unit cabinetry supplier whose amenity section is an
afterthought, a multi-division interiors supplier whose seven-division package had a millwork column
extracted from it row by row, a combined carpentry and millwork bidder whose millwork block is a tenth
of its own total, and a contractor's own budget carried as a competing column. None of those numbers
answers the same question and at least one of them will be the low entry.

Third, the top of the ranking is usually the broadest scope, sometimes the only union bidder, and
sometimes the only shop that carried the structure behind the millwork. In this trade neither end of
the ranking means what it appears to mean, and the useful comparison is the middle after plugs.

Which leads to the pricing convention that matters most. An estimator does not compare base bids. It
compares base bid plus adjustments, and in this trade the adjustments have a distinctive source:
where one shop priced a scope line and another did not, the priced line becomes the plug applied to
everybody. Adjustments have run from zero, on a sheet that levelled nothing, to roughly 56 and 62
percent of a base bid on sheets where the unit entry and countertop scope sat outside every base. On
one package an identical adjustment was applied to both genuine bidders, assembled entirely out of a
competitor's own priced lines. Where a scope line exists in nobody's base, an estimator writes its own
valuation into the cell in plain words, and it does the same for a scope line a bidder included
without pricing, so an unpriced inclusion still enters the comparison at an assigned worth.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on thirty custom architectural millwork proposals and four estimator
leveling sheets, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by
one general contractor. Unit counts across those projects run from forty to three hundred and
twenty five. The proposals span design development, permit, construction document and conformed sets.
Two same-bidder revision ladders were read end to end, one of six rounds across two set stages with
two rounds missing from the evidence, and several shorter pairs. The bidder pool includes shops that
appear on three or four of the projects, which is what makes the boilerplate versus project signal
distinction in section 4 testable, and it includes an out-of-region fixture manufacturer, a
cross-border fabricator and an offshore multi-division supplier, which is what makes the freight,
tariff and lead time material in section 4 testable at all.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and front of house space, in
  one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against hospitality, corporate
  interiors, retail, institutional, healthcare or laboratory millwork, where the architectural
  woodwork scope is far larger, the grade is more often premium, and the certification culture is
  different.
- No project in the evidence base carried a premium grade requirement. Everything here rests on
  custom grade work, so the premium grade premium is unmeasured.
- No signed subcontract or executed purchase order is present, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, whether the structure-behind items were ever bought, or who ended up with the
  countertop supports.
- No shop drawing set and no shop drawing review record is present. Since more than one shop states
  that the approved shops rather than the bid documents govern the delivered scope, and since the
  contractor is asked to guarantee the dimensions on them, the most consequential document in this
  trade is entirely absent.
- No change order history is present. The claim that the delivery, blocking, continuity and
  hold-to-dimension qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own
  language rather than observed in the field, so treat it as untested.
- Only one project's leveling sheet normalized on front of house area, and only that project stated
  the area. The rest normalized per residential unit without recording an amenity area at all, so the
  relationship between the two denominators cannot be established from what is here.
- Renovation and existing-conditions millwork appears only thinly, as a handful of reinstall and
  salvage rows on one project. Historic profile matching, refinishing of existing woodwork and
  millwork in occupied space are all unexamined.
- No project in the evidence base had a feature stair, a monumental millwork element, or an exterior
  woodwork scope, so all three are absent.
- The elevator cab interior boundary is described from outside it. Cab woodwork was carried once and
  excluded everywhere else, and no elevator proposal was read to see whether it picks the scope up.
- No project shows a general contractor self-performing millwork installation, so the install pricing
  here is all shop-quoted or subcontracted.
- No project shows owner-furnished millwork, and only one shows an owner's vendor taking a scope out
  of the package mid-bid, so the owner-furnished case is essentially unexamined.
- Prevailing wage appears only as an exclusion available on request, never as an awarded basis.
