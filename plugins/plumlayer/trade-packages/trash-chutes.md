# Trash chutes

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the trash chute and
compactor package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator
leveling sheets. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. This is a small package that behaves like a supply trade
wearing a subcontractor's clothes, and almost everything that goes wrong with it goes wrong at the
seams rather than in the number.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The package is trash and recycling chutes plus the compaction equipment that sits under them. The
anchor spec section is the trash chute section, and proposals name it when they name one at all.
Two variations are worth knowing. Some sets pair the chute section with a separate solid waste
handling equipment section for the compactors and containers, and a proposal will then cite both
numbers together. And the section number itself is not reliable across projects: a project may run
the package under a neighbouring number in the same series, and on a renovation the leveling
workbook may give that same number to vertical platform lifts and leave chutes with no tab at all.
Identify this package by its content, never by its number.

The bidder pool has two kinds of company in it and they behave differently.

The first kind is the chute and dock equipment distributor. It represents one or two chute
manufacturers, sells compactors from a second manufacturer, quotes furnish and install, and treats
alternates as a product menu. Its proposals are structured, carry substitution request forms, and
name every component.

The second kind is the door and dock equipment company. It sells rolling doors, sectional
doors, high performance doors, elevator smoke curtains and dock equipment, and trash chutes are
one line in a
catalogue. This is the single most important packaging fact in the trade, because such a bidder will
often quote all of those packages in one document under one printed estimate total. Levelling that
printed total as the chute bid can overstate it threefold. Always find the chute line inside the
document and level that, and expect the same bidder to appear in the overhead door package and the
specialties package on the same project.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package:

- Compactors, compaction containers, non-compaction containers and wheeled toters. Effectively
  always carried by the chute bidder even where the specification puts them elsewhere.
- Bin handling and sorting devices at the discharge, including sorters that route between trash and
  recycling and powered bin movers. Carried when the drawings show them, and one bidder will offer
  its own manufacturer's equivalent when the drawn product is another manufacturer's.
- Odour control and sanitizing equipment, meaning the wash down unit, its solution and its access
  door. Carried as furnish only, effectively always.
- Chute sound treatment, meaning sprayed sound coating and isolation pads. Carried, but the grade of
  coating and the type of isolator are silently variable and are one of the places a price falls
  between rounds.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves the package even though it touches the chute:

- The chute shaft itself, its fire rating, its fire wrap and its fire caulking. Excluded by every
  bidder seen, without exception.
- Floor and roof openings, and their framing. Excluded by every bidder seen.
- The roof curb. Excluded by every bidder seen even where the drawings call for one, and often with
  an explicit note saying so.
- Sprinkler piping into the chute. The heads go in the chute as furnish only and the piping is
  always somebody else's.
- All wiring for the door interlocks and the compactor. Excluded by every bidder seen.

## 2. How the market bids

The proposal is an equipment manifest with one number under it. That is the trade's signature and
it holds across every bidder type.

A typical proposal lists each chute as its own block, identified by diameter, by the count of floors
and intakes it serves, and sometimes by the trash room it lives in, and then enumerates that chute's
components line after line: gauge and material, intake throat and floor support, intake door size
and hinge and handle type, vent cap and flashing, floor retainer flange, discharge damper type,
embossing, interlock type, junction box and control box, heat sensor and smoke detection, baffles,
sound insulation spray, isolator pads, sanitizing unit, and the rough floor opening the chute
requires. Then the compactors by model, voltage and phase, then the containers by size and count.
And then one price for all of it.

So the counts and the component list are the comparable, not the total. Two bidders quoting the same
building will agree on the chute diameter and the intake count and disagree on half a dozen
components, and none of that disagreement is visible in the number.

What a proposal will reliably state:

- Chute diameter, gauge and material, and the rough floor opening required. Effectively always.
- Intake door count expressed as floors over intakes, which is the trade's own quantity idiom.
- The chute manufacturer and the compactor manufacturer by name, and whether either is the basis of
  design or an approved alternate.
- Whether the install is union or open shop. Stated by most bidders and recorded by an estimator for
  every bidder regardless.
- Whether tax is included, and whether it is on material only.

What a proposal will reliably not state:

- Any square foot or linear foot quantity. The trade does not think in area.
- Labor content, except from the door and dock equipment companies, whose estimating systems
  print a crew line such as two men for four days. That line matters more than it looks.
- Any schedule or lead time, unless asked.
- Bond cost, which is quoted as a percentage of contract value when quoted at all.

Revision habits are where this trade misleads. A chute revision is very rarely a document response.

- The commonest revision moves no scope and no money and exists only to restate the install labor
  assumption. The door and dock equipment companies file two documents sharing one estimate
  number and one estimate date, differing only in whether the labor line reads as a crew for a
  number of days or as one worker at an hourly rate, with totals several percent apart. Both then
  get ranked as if they were competing bidders.
- The second commonest revision is a brand substitution folded silently into the base. A bidder that
  quoted the basis of design chute and compactor in one round will quote an alternate manufacturer
  for both in the next, inside the base bid, with no deviation flagged, and the total falls by
  roughly a third. Container counts and coating grades move in the same round.
- A revision inside a mixed-trade document may not touch the chutes at all. A total that rises
  because an elevator smoke curtain count went up will read as a chute increase to anyone diffing
  totals.
- Exclusions and inclusions disappear between rounds. Watch for a discharge door specified with a
  released solenoid becoming a discharge door with a fusible link, for a gasket at each intake door
  vanishing from the scope sentence, for a fire stopping exclusion leaving the page, and for a large
  deduct disappearing from the alternates block, all at an unchanged or falling price.

Diff the component sentences and the counts round over round. Diffing the totals in this trade finds
almost nothing that matters.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Chute scope sheets have run around thirteen to forty rows. Target roughly thirty to forty scope
lines. The upper end is not padding: the component checklist is what makes two lump sums
comparable, and a sheet that skips it produces a tight-looking spread that proves nothing.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Each chute as its own line, keyed to its trash room and carrying its diameter, its floors and
  intakes, and its required rough opening. On a multi building project this is also the building
  split, and bidders will offer per building splits even when they say the splits are for reference
  only.
- Intake doors as their own line, with size, hinge direction, label rating, finish, handle type and
  keying. The handle question is a real decision and not a detail: lever handles at a low pull
  force, non-removable keyed cylinders, and one handed pneumatic operation with backup handles are
  three different products at three different prices, and the accessibility requirement and the
  specified cylinder can contradict each other.
- Discharge assembly as its own line, naming the door type and whether it releases on a fusible link
  or on a solenoid. These are not interchangeable and the swap has been made silently mid-ladder.
- Venting as its own line: vent cap, screen, gauge, and the flashing collar, with the roof curb
  named separately as a routed item.
- Sound treatment as two lines, the sprayed coating with its grade and the isolation pads with their
  type and height. Pad height changes the intake door height and therefore the handle height, so it
  is a dimensional decision, not a comfort one.
- Chute wrap or batt wrap as its own line, separate from sprayed coating. It is routinely offered as
  an add, excluded from bases, and specified without a named product.
- Interlock system as its own line, naming voltage class, whether a control panel is required, and
  what the interlock ties into. Interlocked chutes require a manual on and off switch by rule, and a
  low voltage system requires a control panel where a line voltage one may not.
- Heat sensor and smoke detection as their own line, with the routing question answered: the sensor
  is typically the chute vendor's and the detector device is typically somebody else's.
- Sanitizing and wash down as its own line, with its solution, its access door, its solenoid and its
  floor drain requirement.
- Sprinkler heads in the chute as their own line, naming the floors they occur on and stating that
  piping is by others.
- Compactors as their own line by model, horsepower, voltage and phase, with the hopper
  configuration named.
- Containers as their own lines, split between compaction and non-compaction, with counts, and with
  a unit price asked for one more. The container count is the quantity most likely to move between
  rounds and it is worth real money each.
- Toters and bin movers as their own line. Drawings frequently assign a bin mover to the owner.
- Floor supports and the support frame as their own line, with its ownership stated. See section 5.
- Offsets as their own line with their location, because the code limits how far and how often a
  chute may offset and an offset in an unexpected place is a redesign.

### What collapses

- The chute body, its sections, its intake throats and its retainer flanges collapse into the chute
  line. No document prices them apart.
- Freight, unloading and delivery collapse into one line. It is a flat per shipment charge that does
  not scale with chute size, so treating it as a rate is wrong.
- Submittals, shop drawings, start-up and training collapse into one line.
- Per floor enumeration collapses into the floors and intakes figure. Nobody prices a chute by the
  storey.

### The include or exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns a row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or say nothing, and
the three outcomes would produce different numbers. In this trade the test fires hardest on the
furnish only components in section 5, on the handle and cylinder question, and on the wrap and
coating grade. It fires hardly at all on the chute body, which every bidder describes identically.

Silence is a finding here more than in most trades, because the component manifest is long and a
missing component reads as an inclusion to a skimmer. A proposal that does not mention the control
box, the solenoid or the second coat of sound insulation is not offering them.

### The structural gap list

These are the items no chute bidder carries and the estimator must add to every number before
comparing. Naming them explicitly is doing the plugging in advance.

- The rated shaft enclosure, its fire wrap and its fire caulking.
- Floor and roof openings, their cutting and their framing.
- The roof curb, its framing, its flashing and the roofing around it.
- Blocking and any additional support inside the shaft beyond the standard floor supports.
- All interlock and compactor wiring, and the power feed.
- Sprinkler piping to the heads and the testing of it.
- Domestic hot water, shutoff valve and backflow preventer for the wash down unit.
- Air and duct sealing at the shaft.
- Smoke detector devices as opposed to the heat sensor.
- The maintenance agreement, where the specification calls for one.
- Bonds, and insurance beyond the bidder's standard endorsement.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

The exclusion list in this trade is unusually stable. Every bidder seen excludes the openings, the
curbs, the shaft, the fire stopping, the wiring and the water. That uniformity means the list
carries no project information at all, and levelling on exclusion lists alone yields zero
differentiation between bidders. Read it once for the trade map and then stop reading it.

| Excluded by the chute bidder | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Floor and roof openings, cutting and framing | Concrete, structure or general contractor |
| Roof curb, its framing and its flashing | Carpentry and roofing |
| Rated shaft enclosure, fire wrap, fire caulking, fireproofing | Drywall and firestopping |
| Blocking and additional support within the shaft | Carpentry or miscellaneous metals |
| Field wiring of the interlock system and the compactor, and the power feed | Electrical |
| Sprinkler piping to the chute heads and its testing | Fire protection |
| Hot water, shutoff and backflow preventer for the wash down unit | Plumbing |
| Air and duct sealing at the shaft | Insulation or drywall |
| Smoke detector devices | Fire alarm |
| Roofing and roof framing | Roofing and structure |
| Lightning protection | Electrical, and frequently unassigned |
| Bonds and insurance beyond standard endorsements | General contractor |
| Maintenance agreements and non standard warranties | Owner, at closeout |

What is genuine project signal, because it is written for this set and appears in no other proposal
from that bidder:

- An exclusion that names a drawn item and asks for direction. A bidder that writes that the
  drawings show two additional containers marked as required only if needed, excludes them, and
  offers a unit price, has read the drawings and found an ambiguity. That is a request for
  information, not a boundary.
- An exclusion justified by a defect in the documents. A specification calling for a sound
  insulating wrap with no basis of design product named, which the manufacturer does not make for
  the doors, and which the bidder does not recommend, is a documents gap disclosed by the market.
- A disclosed conflict between the specification and a code requirement. The clearest instance is a
  specification calling for non-removable keyed cylinders where those cylinders are not accessible.
  The bidder cannot resolve that and neither can leveling. It is a request for information.
- A statement that no specification existed for the chutes at bid time, with the price made subject
  to a later scope review. That is not a qualification, it is a gate on the whole number.

Qualifications that are really pricing bases:

- Price subject to change until the date of material release, with manufacturer increases passed
  through. Near universal, and it means the quoted number is an offer to hold nothing.
- Openings and jambs taken as existing, which on new construction is a boilerplate carryover from
  retrofit work and should be challenged.
- Field verification required before release, which means the counts in the proposal are the
  bidder's and have not been confirmed.
- Material release conditioned on a deposit of half the contract value, on a signed quote, and on
  approved submittals. That is a cash flow term with schedule consequences.
- Union or open shop install, offered by at least one bidder as a whole project alternate rather
  than a qualification, at roughly a tenth of the package.
- Validity windows of thirty or sixty days, which routinely expire before the round the bid is
  ranked in.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This package is nominally furnish and install and is in practice a furnish and install shell with
several furnish only components inside it. Each of those components needs another trade to finish
it, and each is a place where a chute gets delivered and never works.

The furnish only components, effectively always:

- Sprinkler heads. Set in the chute by the chute vendor, piped and tested by fire protection. The
  heads occur on named floors and the drawings should say which.
- The wash down and sanitizing unit. Furnished with its solution, its access door and its solenoid;
  hot water, shutoff and backflow preventer by plumbing; and a floor drain is required in the
  discharge room, which is a plumbing coordination item nobody mentions until it is missing.
- Heat sensor and smoke detection. The sensor is the chute vendor's and the detector device is fire
  alarm's, and the interconnection between the sanitizer control and the door interlock system is a
  named exception on at least one bidder's list.
- The roof curb, where a bidder furnishes it to site at all rather than excluding it outright.
- Thruwall and access doors, which are furnished loose for another trade to set into a wall.

Other seams:

- The chute support frame. Steel angle floor supports at every level, anchor bolts and isolators,
  and a wood ledger for the frame. A chute vendor supplies the standard floor supports and excludes
  additional support within the shaft, and an estimator's own scope sheet may carry an open question
  about whether the support frame belongs to miscellaneous metals. Settle it before bid, because
  both packages will exclude it.
- The shaft. Drywall builds and rates it, firestopping seals it, and the chute vendor will not touch
  either. The rough floor opening the chute requires is larger than the chute diameter by several
  inches, and that dimension has to reach whoever forms the slab.
- Electrical. Every interlock, every control box, the compactor feed and every connection. A low
  voltage interlock system still needs an electrician in most jurisdictions, and the chute vendor
  will assume it does not.
- Fire alarm. Detector devices, and the relay or interlock tie-in to the alarm system.
- Overhead doors and dock equipment. Where the chute bidder is a door and dock equipment company,
  the same document also carries doors, curtains and dock gear, and the seam is a document seam
rather than a
  scope one. Read the whole document before assigning any of it.
- Elevators. On the mixed-trade documents the elevator smoke curtains sit beside the chutes, which
  means a chute round diff can silently be an elevator scope change.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

The chute drawings are typically an enlarged trash room plan, a chute detail sheet and the
specification. They are unusually good at describing the object and unusually bad at describing its
context.

What the bidder actually prices from, in order:

1. The enlarged trash room plans, for the number of chutes, their rooms and the equipment layout.
2. The chute detail sheet, for diameter, gauge, intake type, discharge, venting and support.
3. The specification, for the approved manufacturer list, the sound treatment, the cylinder and
   handle requirements and the maintenance agreement.
4. The floor count, which is the trade's own denominator.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Whether the install is union or open shop, worth roughly a tenth.
- How many crew days the bidder assumes. Competing labor assumptions on the same equipment differ by
  a factor of two and are worth several percent of the total. This is the trade's production basis
  and it is invisible unless the bidder happens to print its crew line.
- Whether the chute manufacturer in the base is the basis of design or an approved alternate, and
  whether the approved list on this project even includes the brand the drawings named. Approved
  manufacturer lists are project specific and a brand that is basis of design on one project does
  not appear on another project's list.
- Whether a bin mover or waste caddy is the owner's, which the drawings sometimes say and sometimes
  do not.
- Whether the containers drawn as conditional are wanted.
- The lead time, which is quoted only after materials are released and a deposit is paid, and which
  the bidder explicitly refuses to guarantee before then.
- Whether a maintenance agreement is required and who holds it after closeout.
- Whether the project carries an owner or contractor controlled insurance program, which changes
  the bidder's insurance basis and is asked for by email in every project seen.
- Whether the discharge room has a floor drain.
- Freight, which is a flat charge per shipment. Two chutes shipped together cost what one costs to
  freight, so splitting an award between two vendors buys a second freight charge.

Set stage behaviour. At design development the bidder prices off a partial detail set and may state
outright that no chute specification existed. By permit and construction documents the component
lists get longer and the substitution requests arrive. The folder stage is not a reliable indicator
of the round: the same document appears in two set-stage folders on more than one project, and a
filename date has run three months later than the estimate date printed on the document.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Floors and intakes, expressed together as floors over intakes. This is the chute's defining
  quantity and the closest thing the trade has to a takeoff.
- Chute diameter in inches, with the required rough floor opening several inches larger.
- Each, for chutes, compactors, containers, toters, doors and sorters.
- Cost per floor, which is how an estimator normalizes the package on most projects.
- Cost per residential unit, which is how the same estimator normalizes it on others. Both bases
  appear across projects for the same trade, and they are not interchangeable: chute cost scales
  with intakes and floors and does not scale with unit count, so a per unit rate carried between a
  tall thin building and a short wide one will mislead.
- Percent of contract value, for bond.
- Crew days, where the bidder's estimating system prints them.

Cost drivers, roughly in order of how much movement they cause:

1. Which manufacturer sits in the base, chute and compactor alike. A brand swap inside the base has
   moved a total by about a third with no other change.
2. Intake count and chute count. The chute body is a minor part of the cost next to the doors.
3. Union versus open shop install, worth roughly a tenth.
4. Container and toter counts, each container being worth a meaningful fraction of a percent and
   the counts moving freely between rounds.
5. The intake door handle and cylinder decision.
6. Sound treatment: coating grade, wrap, and isolator type. The wrap alone has been quoted at about
   a tenth of a base as a deduct.
7. The crew day assumption.
8. Whether the compactor package is in at all. A deduct removing all compactors, containers and
   toters has run to about half a base bid, which tells you the equipment under the chute is worth
   as much as the chute.
9. Freight, as a step function rather than a rate.
10. Bond, warranty term and insurance basis.

On spread, and no amounts are given here. Base bids on a single project have clustered within about
fourteen percent, which looks like a healthy competitive market. It is not evidence of one. That
tight cluster occurred on a sheet whose entire component checklist went unanswered, where the two
year warranty the subcontract required was left as a question mark by most bidders, and where two of
the four ranked columns were the same company's same estimate differing only in its labor line. A
tight spread in this trade usually means the sheet asked no questions, because the components that
actually differ between bidders are not the ones that appear in the total. Where a scope sheet did
ask, the same trade produced a nearly threefold gap between a general contractor's own budget and
every real bid, for the plain reason that the budget was built on a superseded single chute design.
Convergence on price and convergence on scope are independent here, and in this trade price
converges first.

One more convention worth knowing. Only one bidder type discloses its material, tax, freight and
install split, and where that split is visible the install labor is a small fraction of the package
and the freight is flat. That single disclosure is what makes the furnish and install seam
measurable at all; every other proposal buries it. If a leveling exercise needs to know how much of
this package is labor, it has to come from that bidder or from nowhere.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly twenty chute proposals from five companies across four
multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor, plus
three estimator leveling tabs. The proposals span design development, permit, construction documents
and conformed rounds, and several companies appear on more than one project, which is what makes the
boilerplate and project signal distinction in section 4 testable. Roughly a third of the proposals
were image only scans and had to be rendered rather than read as text, and one byte-identical
document was filed under two different rounds on one project.

The corpus is thin in ways that matter, and the entry is written to that.

- Only one bidder in the entire corpus discloses a material, freight and install breakdown. Every
  claim in section 7 about labor content, freight behaviour and the furnish and install split rests
  on that single company's documents, on two projects. Treat it as a well evidenced pattern from one
  source rather than a market norm.
- Crew day production figures come only from the door and dock equipment companies, whose
  estimating system happens to print them. Whether the distributor bidders assume similar crews is
unknown.
- Every project here is residential with chutes serving four to six occupied floors. Nothing is
  tested against high rise chutes, linen chutes, hospital or hotel work, or chutes with more than
  the handful of offsets these buildings needed. Linen chutes differ in discharge type and intake
  door hinging and are not represented at all.
- Only one project carried a sorting device at the discharge, so the sorter and bin mover scope in
  section 1 rests on that project alone.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout appears in the corpus, so nothing here shows which
  exclusions survived negotiation. In particular the shaft support frame question, which two
  packages both exclude, is never seen resolved.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the furnish only components are where
  chutes fail is read out of the proposals' own language and out of what the exclusions imply, not
  observed in the field.
- Only one leveling sheet in the corpus asked the component questions at all, and the other two
  asked almost nothing, so the row counts in section 3 are a recommendation built from one good
  example rather than an observed market standard.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the chute package was awarded to a bidder other than a
  distributor or an overhead door company, so whether a general trades contractor ever carries this
  work is unknown.
- Lead times are never stated in any proposal seen. Every bidder defers them to after release and
  deposit, so this entry cannot say what a chute lead time is, only that it is not knowable at bid.
