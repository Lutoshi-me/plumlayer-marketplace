# Landscaping and exterior improvements

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the landscape package:
softscape, the hardscape that rides with it, site furnishings, irrigation, and the green roof and
amenity terrace work that sits over structure. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor
proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a
behavior is, is carried by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this
file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real landscape contractors can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

One warning belongs at the top, because it governs everything below. This package is really two
packages that share a division number. On grade, it is a site trade: soil, plants, paving, fences,
furnishings, bounded on one side by earthwork and on the other by site concrete. Over structure, it is
a roofing trade: growing media, drainage board, root barrier and pavers on pedestals sitting on
somebody else's waterproofing membrane, bounded by the membrane below and the warranty that covers it.
Different companies bid them, different exclusions apply, and the two regimes are priced on different
denominators. A scope list that does not separate on grade from over structure will be answered by
half the market and ignored by the other half.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The package lives in Division 32, Exterior Improvements. Where a proposal names sections at all, the
ones that recur are unit paving, stabilized aggregate pavement, site furnishings, turf and grasses,
plants, extended plant maintenance and erosion control. A green roof is sometimes given its own
section in the seventies alongside the roofing work and sometimes a Division 32 number, and the same
proposal from the same company has been filed under both.

Establish the kind of company before comparing anything. This is a trade where bidders sell different
things under one trade token, and the spread between the top and bottom of a landscape ranking is
almost entirely an artifact of that, not of price. The kinds seen:

- Whole-package landscape contractors, who carry soil, plants, lawns, all the hardscape, fences, site
  furnishings, structures and irrigation, furnish and install. These are the only bidders whose
  numbers are comparable to each other.
- Nursery-rooted contractors, who do the same work but price plants species by species because a
  nursery is where they came from.
- Planting-and-turf specialists, who carry plants, mulch, lawn and guarantee and nothing else, and
  who exclude the prepared soil the plants sit in. A bid like this can land at a quarter of a
  whole-package bid and look spectacular.
- Green roof and amenity terrace specialists, who work only over structure and refuse everything at
  grade by name.
- Hardscape and stone installers, who set pavers, granite caps and veneer and carry no plants.
- Granite quarries and fabricators, who quote material only, priced at the plant, at figures two
  orders of magnitude below the package they are ranked beside.
- Site furnishings manufacturers, who quote supply only from a catalogue and issue the same quote to
  every bidder on the list.
- Fence specialists, deck and rail fabricators, steel fabricators selling shade structures, shade
  panel manufacturers, pool builders and fireplace suppliers, each of whom answers a handful of rows.

On one project a ranking ran more than fifty times from top to bottom, and the four whole-package
bidders in it sat within about a tenth of each other. The bottom of that list was a fireplace supplier
whose labor type the estimator recorded, accurately, as "Supplier." Reading the spread without
reading the kind of company produces nonsense.

### How the market bundles and splits the package

The dominant shape is one whole-package bidder carrying everything on grade and, on simpler projects,
everything over structure too. The recurring splits away from that:

- On grade against over structure. This is the primary split and the one that most often produces two
  separate awards. A green roof specialist will state that ground level landscape installation is
  excluded; a maintenance-and-construction firm will title its proposal "on grade landscaping" and
  write "includes ground level only" as its first qualification. When both bid, neither one's number
  is the package.
- Fences and gates, which leave to a fence specialist on most projects even where a whole-package
  bidder also prices them.
- Pergolas, arbors, trellises and cabanas, which are bid variously by the whole-package landscaper as
  a resold manufactured product, by a steel fabricator as a fabrication, by an outdoor-living
  manufacturer from a catalogue, and by a shade panel maker who supplies only the panels. Four kinds
  of company, four different scopes, one row on the sheet.
- Granite curb, wall blocks, copings and veneer, which come from a quarry as material and need
  somebody else to set them.
- Site furnishings, which are always a manufactured product and only sometimes this trade's to buy.
- Pools and water features, which leave entirely, to Division 13.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits elsewhere:

- Fences and gates on grade. Whole-package bidders routinely price them alongside a dedicated fence
  bidder, so the same fence gets two numbers.
- Site furnishings, planters, bike racks and trash receptacles. Carried by nearly every whole-package
  bidder, and also carried by the specialties package on some projects, which is a live double-count
  risk. Beware of the same bike rack being claimed by two trades.
- Outdoor kitchens, grills, pizza ovens, smokers, refrigerators and their counters. Carried by
  whole-package bidders as site furnishings, with all plumbing, gas and electrical connections
  excluded. These are appliances bought by a landscaper.
- Fire pits, fire tables and fire bowls, natural gas, with electronic ignition and emergency stop
  buttons. Almost always the landscaper's to buy and never the landscaper's to connect.
- Light bollards. Carried as a furnishing, with the concrete footing and all the electrical work
  excluded, so the bollard arrives and stands there until an electrician comes.
- Irrigation, which is a plumbing and electrical trade in everything but name and lands here anyway.
- The green roof assembly above the membrane, which leaves the roofing package and lands here.
- Erosion control blankets and matting, which sit in an early Division 31 section and get bid here,
  usually as an alternate.
- Reinforced or drivable lawn, a grass-paving grid product, which is drawn on the civil sheets and
  bid here, almost always as an alternate.
- Pavers on pedestals over structure, which are contested at least three ways and sometimes four.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves even when it looks like landscape:

- Swimming pools and their equipment. A different division and a different world. The pool builder
  excludes the coping, the deck, the deck sealant, the fencing, the backfill, the export and the
  hoisting, and takes the site at a stated depth below final grade.
- Bituminous paving, curbing and pavement markings, which usually stay with the site or earthwork
  package. Decorative granite wall curb is the exception and comes back here.
- Site concrete: sidewalks, pads, flatwork, footings for everything the landscaper sets.
- Retaining walls and site walls, routed to structural and civil by the landscape drawings themselves.
- Tree removal and grubbing, which is a clearing operation bought before this package exists.
- Guardrails, handrails and railings, which appear on the landscape materials legend and are answered
  N by every landscape bidder.
- Tree guards and tree grates, routed to misc metals.
- Movable furniture: chairs, tables, lounge chairs, couches, movie screens. On the sheet, refused by
  the market, and genuinely nobody's. See section 3.

## 2. How the market bids

Landscape is a heavily itemized trade, and unlike a lump-sum trade the discriminating fact is not the
number of numbers but the grain. Two bidders can both produce sixteen priced rows and be enumerating
completely different things.

The grain regimes seen, all of them common:

- Sheet-and-spec-section keyed work groups. Each group names its spec section or its landscape sheet,
  states a quantity, names the manufacturer, product, size, colour and finish, and gives one price.
  Twenty to thirty groups for a full package.
- Materials-legend mark grain. The proposal's line items are the drawing's own material legend keys,
  in legend order, with the legend's mark letters. Where the legend exists this is the most common
  shape, and it is the strongest signal that the bidder actually read the set.
- Detail-referenced grain. Every row names the sheet and detail it prices. Common where there is no
  specification, because the detail is the only place the product is defined.
- Plant-schedule grain. Species and cultivar, size class, count, unit price and extension, forty or
  more rows for the trees alone. This is the botanical cousin of the schedule mark, and it is the only
  grain that lets anybody check a planting number.
- Build-up grain. One price for a named layer stack, root barrier through growing media, each layer
  named with a product and an "or approved equal." Standard on green roofs.
- Layer-decomposed grain, the deepest seen. Every paver mark gets its wearing course row and separate
  rows for its setting bed, its concrete base and its aggregate base, each independently priced or
  independently excluded within the same assembly.

Grain is not uniform inside one proposal. The same bidder that decomposes paving into twenty rows will
collapse ten thousand plants into a single line, and the bidder that lists seventy plant species will
price its irrigation as one lump. Expect the softscape and the hardscape to be at different grains in
the same document.

The plant line specifically comes in three shapes, and they are not comparable:

1. One line reading "plantings per planting schedule," with the mulch volume stated. The count is
   invisible; the schedule is the contract.
2. One line with the total plant count on it. The count is the whole comparable.
3. Species by species with sizes and counts. Checkable against the schedule, and the only shape that
   exposes a substitution.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The basis documents, usually by drawing number range and date, and often by spec section number.
  Two plan dates in one proposal is common. Naming both a drawing date and a specification date is
  common.
- Labor type, union or open shop. Stated on the face of many proposals and recorded by an estimator
  for every bidder regardless.
- Named manufacturers and product models for every furnishing, planter, paver and structure. This is a
  product-heavy trade and the proposals read like purchase orders.
- Soil depths. Where soil is priced it is priced in cubic yards, and the depth that generated the
  volume is nearly always written out.
- A maintenance period and a guarantee period, usually different from each other and often different
  per work family within one proposal.
- Tax status and validity window.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Unit rates. Extensions and quantities are common; a stated rate per unit is not, outside the
  plant-schedule and rate-card shapes.
- What is under the paving. The phrase "below setting bed by others" recurs across many rows of a
  single proposal without ever saying who the others are.
- A takeoff. One bidder seen published a full measured takeoff, as marked-up drawings with computed
  areas to two decimal places, appended to its proposal. That is remarkable, not normal.
- Mobilization or visit counts on the maintenance obligation, unless it is a green roof, where visit
  counts are stated and vary.
- Whether irrigation coverage was designed or assumed.

Revision habits. Rounds in this trade are numerous, and the most common shapes are:

- Conformed-set reconfirmation. A full re-issue against a later drawing date with the scope text
  stable and the number moving.
- Value engineering rounds, which arrive in three quite different forms: appended deduct options on an
  unchanged base, a re-issued base with a lower number, or, most dangerously, a full re-issue priced
  against a set of drawings the bidder drew itself.
- Exclusion-to-alternate migration, where a build-up carried in the base of one round becomes an add
  alternate in the next at a near-identical total.
- Product substitution deducts, where the base is unchanged and a named product is swapped for a
  cheaper grade of the same product.
- Silent reprice, where nothing but the numbers moves.

Two consequences for a reader. First, a bidder can produce five documents on one project across two
set stages, including base and value-engineered variants of the same round, and every one of them can
end up as a separate row in the ranking. Ordering them by the set they cite, not by the letterhead
date or the filename, is the only reliable sequence. Second, and specific to this trade: a
value-engineering round may be priced against a drawing set the bidder authored, stamped as its own
peer review. From that round forward the number is not a price for the architect's design, and a
round-over-round delta measures a design change rather than a market movement.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Landscape scope sheets have run 55, 59 and 90 rows. Target roughly sixty to ninety scope lines for a
full package with both on-grade and over-structure work, and roughly forty for on-grade only. The
organizing axis is area, and under each area the same three blocks repeat: planting, hardscape, and
miscellaneous improvements. On projects with terraces the areas are the terraces, named as the
drawings name them. On projects without, the areas are at grade and courtyard.

Where a landscape materials legend exists, use it as the spine. The legend's own mark families are the
scope families, its mark keys are the row identifiers, and its detail references are the row
references. Bidders that read the set produce line items in legend order with the legend's letters on
them, so a sheet built the same way can be diffed against a proposal directly. Note that the mark
letters are architect-specific discovered vocabulary and not a fixed set: one project's hardscape,
walls, fences and furnishings families ran A, B, C, D with site lighting as L, and nothing guarantees
the next project uses those letters or that grouping.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Area, always, and separately for every terrace, courtyard, roof level and at-grade zone. This is the
  primary axis in every estimator sheet and in most proposals.
- On grade against over structure, as a hard division inside the area axis. The build-up, the access,
  the pricing and the bidder pool all change across it. Over-structure paving has carried more than
  one and a half times the on-grade rate for a nominally similar paver.
- Every paving mark separately, by mark. Precast, permeable, porcelain, wood tile, vehicular and
  stepping stone are separate products at separate rates and they appear as separate marks on the
  legend. Do not merge them into "unit paving."
- The paving build-up, layer by layer, under each paving mark: the setting bed by type, the concrete
  base, the aggregate base, the geotextile, the pedestals and their cores, the edging. This is the
  single most important enumeration in the trade. Bidders sell these layers independently and include
  some and exclude others within the same assembly, so a single "pavers" line invites two bidders to
  price two different stacks. A proposal has been seen pricing the concrete and aggregate base under
  three paver marks and excluding the same two layers under two others, in one document.
- Edging by material and profile, and separately in hardscape areas and planting areas. It is
  routinely a five-figure line and it is routinely excluded by name in the same proposal that prices
  it.
- Soil by depth and by location, not by area. Depth is the quantity driver: a proposal carried seven
  depth-differentiated soil components in one line, from a shallow bed to a bed over six feet deep,
  and a separate structural soil with sand drainage. Soil at a given depth in a bed, a lawn, a planter
  and a tree pit are four different quantities and often four different mixes. State the mix ratio
  where the specification gives one.
- Structural soil separately from planting soil. It is a different product and it dominates the soil
  number where it appears.
- Plants at whatever grain the schedule supports, and at minimum split into trees, shrubs, perennials
  and grasses, groundcover, and annuals. Trees split further by caliper or height band, because the
  band drives the price steeply: within one bid the largest evergreen band ran more than three times
  the smallest.
- Lawn by method: sod, hydroseed, native or meadow seed mix, deer-resistant mix, shaded roadside mix.
  These carry different rates and different guarantee periods.
- Reinforced or drivable lawn as its own line, always. It is drawn on the civil sheets, bid as an
  alternate, and priced at many times the seeded lawn rate per unit area.
- Mulch by type and depth, and separately where a courtyard mix differs from the general site mix.
- Irrigation as its own line, and see section 5 before writing it.
- Each named site furnishing by product family and count: benches by type, bike racks, trash
  receptacles, dog waste stations, planters by size and material, bollards.
- Bollards specifically, separated from other furnishings, because they carry a footing and an
  electrical connection that nothing else in the furnishings block does.
- Fire features by type and count, separately from the outdoor kitchen.
- The outdoor kitchen decomposed: the modular cabinet system, each appliance by type, the countertop,
  and the code-required remote emergency gas shut-offs. The shut-offs are a specification requirement
  and a real number, and they are easy to lose.
- Each shade structure by type, size and count: pergolas by letter, arbors, trellises, cabanas. Their
  footings are a separate line belonging to somebody else.
- Fences by height, by product line and by run, and gates counted separately from fence length. Gate
  counts are large and they are often buried inside a fence line's description rather than stated as
  their own quantity.
- Granite by element and by finish condition: coping, veneer, treads and risers, monolithic wall
  blocks, water table stones. Anchor holes are included on some pieces and excluded on others within
  one quotation, so anchor holes are a line attribute that has to be asked about.
- Curb by type and by curvature. Straight, curved at each radius band, transitions cut in the field
  and corner pieces are separately rated, and the tightest radius has run well over double the
  straight rate. A curb line stated only in linear feet is unbiddable.
- Boulders, by count and by minimum dimension, and separately where they are reclaimed or reused from
  on site rather than imported.
- Establishment maintenance and the plant guarantee, as their own lines, with their periods stated.
- Watering, as its own line, where it is not inside the maintenance line.
- Freight and delivery, as its own line per manufacturer. This is not a courtesy: a bidder priced
  fourteen separate delivery-fee rows, one per manufacturer per shipment, and the site furnishings
  freight alone was a five-figure number.
- Labor to set furnishings, separately from the furnishings, where the bidder separates them.
- Lift, hoist and craneage as its own line. On a project with terraces this is scope, not general
  conditions.
- Erosion control by product and area.
- Mock-ups, where the drawings require them, which they often do in a general note rather than a
  specification.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual plants within a species and size. The species-size-count row is the atom; a row per plant
  is meaningless.
- Coat, colour and finish attributes of a manufactured product. They are attributes of the product
  line, and the product line is already a row.
- Room-scale or bed-scale subdivision within one area at one build-up. Beds within one courtyard at
  one soil depth are one line.
- The components inside a proprietary planter or bench system. The system is the product.
- The individual layers of a green roof build-up. Over structure, unlike at grade, the layer stack is
  sold as one assembly by every bidder seen, and splitting it invites a bidder to price a layer twice
  or not at all. The exception is the insulation over the membrane, which genuinely detaches and
  belongs on its own line, for the reason in section 5.
- Levels within a terrace, unless the material or the access changes.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be silent
about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to landscape,
the test fires hardest in four places: everything below the wearing course of a paving assembly,
everything that needs a concrete footing, everything that needs a wire, and everything on the roof
side of a waterproofing membrane.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding, and this trade has a distinctive silence problem.
Because bidders reproduce the drawing's material legend as their line list, an item that is on the
legend and absent from a proposal has usually been dropped rather than declined. That is a scope gap
to surface, not an inclusion to assume.

There is also a fourth state to watch for beyond in, out and zero. Landscape proposals carry items
that are neither included nor excluded but deferred: "unit price to be negotiated if required," and
"price to follow" on a row that has a quantity. Both read as included to anyone skimming a total, and
both leave the number open after award. Capture them explicitly.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs an estimator adds to every number before comparing, because the market
systematically will not carry them. A scope list that names all of them is doing the plugging in
advance.

1. Concrete footings for everything the landscaper sets: bollards, benches, pergolas, arbors,
   cabanas, fences, trash receptacles, granite curb walls. Excluded by nearly every landscape bidder
   and carried by some fence bidders, which is exactly the sort of inconsistency that makes a fence
   line incomparable.
2. Everything below the setting bed. Rough grading, compacted aggregates, subgrade preparation and
   subgrade establishment are excluded by essentially every whole-package bidder, and the required
   subgrade elevation differs area by area because the landscape build-up depth differs.
3. Electrical for the landscape: bollard wiring, controls and circuitry, festive and catenary
   lighting, planter underlights, task lights at the grill station, and the conduit connecting plant
   beds to irrigation and to landscape lighting.
4. Plumbing and gas for the landscape: the irrigation point of connection, backflow, meter, sleeves,
   and the gas runs and final connections for every fire feature and grill.
5. Movable furniture. Chairs, tables, lounge chairs, couches. On the scope sheet with a general
   contractor quantity and rate, and answered N by essentially every bidder.
6. Site lighting fixtures beyond the bollard itself, and festive lighting.
7. Railings, guardrails and handrails at terraces and ramps. On the landscape legend, answered N by
   every landscape bidder.
8. Tree guards, tree grates and detectable warning strips. On the landscape sheets, routed to misc
   metals and site concrete, answered N by everyone.
9. Curbing, where the landscape package does not carry it and the site package assumed the landscape
   package did.
10. Winter conditions, off-hours and premium time, excluded by essentially every bidder.
11. Permits, police details, engineering and bonds.
12. Lift, hoist and craneage on projects with over-structure work.
13. Erosion control blankets and matting, routinely offered only as an alternate.
14. Watering during establishment, which one bidder prices as a water-truck line and most do not
    mention.
15. The insulation build-up over the waterproofing membrane, which is refused in printed exclusion
    lists and priced in breakouts by the same companies.
16. Soil testing, weed fabric, root barrier and straw mulch, each excluded by name often enough to be
    worth a row.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Much of the first table is boilerplate: repeat bidders reproduce their exclusion lists verbatim
across projects, and competing bidders sometimes reproduce each other's. Read boilerplate for the
trade map, not for the project.

Read this instead as project signal:

- An exclusion that names a mark, a sheet or a detail. When a bidder writes out the mark it will not
  do, it read the set.
- An exclusion justified by a contradiction between documents. A bidder excluded raised metal planters
  on one frontage on the stated ground that the plan showed planters and the sections showed beds at
  grade. That is a documents problem, not a bidder problem, and it belongs upstream as a request for
  information rather than absorbed as a leveling difference.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the documents. A row reading "no detail shown" beside a
  priced build-up the bidder invented, or "no spec" beside a furnishing, is the project's gap list
  written by the market.
- An exclusion of a whole spec section by number. This is a routing statement: it tells you precisely
  which section has to land in another package.
- A depth-triggered exclusion. A bidder carrying no shoring for a planting bed several feet deep is
  naming a cost that belongs to earthwork and that nobody has priced.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Seasonal planting windows. This is the qualification unique to this trade and the one most likely to
  become a claim. Bidders publish explicit windows, and they differ by plant type: evergreen and
  deciduous material carry different spring and fall windows, and lawn seeding carries a third pair.
  Bidders state that where a job is non-specified, the standard landscape-architect dates govern.
  Green roof bidders state a season outside which they will not guarantee plants at all, and one
  restricts installation of the whole system to the growing half of the year. Any schedule that lands
  the landscape work outside the window converts a guaranteed installation into an unguaranteed one,
  and the exposure is the replacement cost of the plants.
- Establishment and guarantee durations, which are contract terms priced as scope. A base commonly
  carries a short maintenance period plus a one-year plant guarantee, with an extended maintenance
  period offered as its own priced line to bring the total to a calendar year. Sod and seed carry
  their own shorter periods in the same proposal. A specification calling for a two-year duration is
  quantifiable: the deduct to fall back to one year has run around a sixteenth of a landscape package
  base. Warranty duration is not paperwork here; it is a line.
- Maintenance visit counts. Where a bidder states them they vary materially: one carried a minimum of
  five visits a year and another up to eight over the same establishment period, and the same company
  later described its visits as simply "necessary." The visit count, not the period, is what is being
  bought.
- Nursery-network pricing. A bidder may condition its plant price on using its own supplier network
  and warn that being directed elsewhere incurs cost or relief on terms. Plant pricing is stated to be
  valid for a short window because plants are perishable and market-priced, and one bidder required
  all plants to be purchased within a month of award to hold the price.
- Plant substitution as a standing condition. Rather than disclose a specific substitution, bidders
  declare that regional shortages may require substitutions generally. That is a grade floor, not a
  deviation, and it means the schedule may not be what gets planted.
- Water. Bidders require water at no charge from an active source within a stated distance, available
  around the clock, and one requires a specified pressure at the roof surface. Water before the
  irrigation system is live is the general contractor's, and it is a real cost on a large planted site.
- The irrigation point of connection, spelled out to the fitting. One bidder specified a copper stub
  of a stated diameter at a stated distance off the foundation, plus a core hole with conduit to the
  controller within inches of the stub. Another required the controller on an outer interior wall.
  These are conditions on somebody else's work.
- Access, hoisting and crane assumptions. Green roof bidders state the working day they assumed,
  including on crane days, and reserve a change order if it is not met. Whole-package bidders assume
  direct machine access to all work areas.
- Sequencing. Bidders condition their price on completing before finish paving, or exclude protection
  of finish paving on that basis. A bidder that has to come back after the paving is down is doing a
  different job.
- Tariffs and escalation, handled three incompatible ways by three bidders on comparable work: one
  guaranteed tariff-affected costs for a stated short window and required a fully executed contract
  rather than a letter of intent to procure; one excluded tariff escalation outright; one pushed steel
  escalation risk back to the general contractor as a provision to carry. These are not equivalent
  numbers.
- Validity windows, which run short in this trade and are stated in days.
- Award conditions. Green roof bidders state that pricing is contingent on awarding all referenced
  scopes and may be revised if only some are selected. That invalidates per-terrace ranking, which is
  precisely how these packages get bought.
- Labor type. Open shop dominates in suburban work and both appear in urban work, sometimes within one
  project and occasionally annotated per row on the estimator's sheet.
- Preemptive overrides. A fence bidder listed its exclusions as applying "even if noted on specs and
  drawing," and disclaimed its own quantities by making the general contractor responsible for them.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The membrane seam: who owns the assembly above the waterproofing

This is the seam that costs the most and is written down the least.

Over structure, the landscape or green roof bidder builds on a membrane installed by somebody else,
and every one of them refuses the membrane. What they do not agree on is the obligation that comes
attached to it. Most membrane warranties oblige somebody to remove the overburden, meaning the entire
green roof, if a leak has to be investigated. The market answers that obligation in opposite
directions:

- One green roof installer carries it, offering a two-year workmanship and overburden-removal warranty
  written to the waterproofing spec section, with a long component warranty above it.
- Another refuses it, excluding by name a no-fault removal and replacement warranty, membrane
  manufacturer warranty requirements, and leak detection and electric vector mapping.

Two proposals for what looks like the same scope, and one of them contains an open-ended liability the
other has priced. That difference will never show up in a total.

The same bidder that refuses the warranty protects itself procedurally instead, and those protections
are scope for other people:

- A structural engineer's affidavit verifying the structure supports the designed load, before it
  starts.
- A report from the roofer documenting that the membrane was tested and inspected within a week of the
  green roof contractor's mobilization, and that the area was then closed off to other trades and to
  foot traffic. If that is not met, the cost of removing and replacing the assembly to investigate a
  suspected leak is charged to the client.
- A pre-site visit to confirm as-built dimensions, water pressure and the conveyance plan before the
  contract price is final.

Each of those is a general contractor obligation hidden in a terms section, and the second one is a
sequencing constraint on the roofer that appears nowhere in the roofing scope.

The insulation over the membrane is the other half of this seam. It appears in green roof bidders'
printed exclusion lists and is priced as a named work group in the base of the same companies'
proposals on the same project. The priced breakout is what an estimator levels, and it should be: a
boilerplate exclusion list is a pasted template. Never level this seam off exclusion lists alone.

Finally, over-structure pavers are contested more widely than anything else in the trade. On one
project pavers on pedestals at the roof terraces were priced by the waterproofing contractor, by a
hardscape installer, and by the whole-package landscape bidder, while a green roof specialist priced
the drainage layers underneath them and the hardscape installer excluded exactly those layers by name.
Four companies, one assembly, and no two of them drawing the boundary in the same place.

### The subgrade seam: where earthwork stops

Earthwork strips and often stockpiles the topsoil, and the landscaper places the soil. Earthwork
excludes planting soils, loam import, seeding, irrigation and hardscape; the landscape bidder excludes
subgrade preparation, rough grading, compacted aggregates and excavation. The two exclusions meet
cleanly on paper and badly in the field, for three reasons:

- The required subgrade elevation is not one number. It is finish grade minus the landscape build-up,
  and the build-up differs by area: six inches of soil under lawn, eighteen in beds, three feet or
  more at a tree pit, a paver plus setting bed plus base under hardscape. A bidder wrote this out as a
  qualification, that the subgrade in its work areas be left at the required levels below finish grade
  by others. The earthworker cannot honour that without reading the landscape sections.
- The paving base is sold layer by layer and the split moves. Some bidders price the aggregate base
  and the concrete base under some paver marks and exclude them under others in the same document. A
  scope line reading "unit pavers" leaves the whole stack unassigned.
- Deep beds trigger excavation support nobody carries. A bidder disclosed that it carried no shoring
  for a planting bed several feet deep. That is an earthwork cost created by a landscape dimension.

Two adjacent notes. First, soil is imported, screened and blended to a ratio in this trade, commonly
loam to compost, and the ratio and depth are stated on the priced row. That is a different product
from the topsoil earthwork stockpiled, so a project can simultaneously stockpile topsoil for reuse and
import screened loam. Second, a bidder that excludes "loam, spreading of loam and amendment of
existing on-site soils" in its boilerplate while pricing a continuous plant soil mix as a work group
has contradicted itself. The priced breakout governs.

### The civil seam, and the useful fact that the drawings often draw it

Landscape materials plans frequently route work to civil by note, on the drawing, in words: concrete
paving, bituminous paving, granite curb, bollards, site utilities, site drainage, site walls, all
carrying a "see civil" or "see structural" annotation in the legend or the site notes. Where those
notes exist, the bidders' exclusion lists mirror them almost exactly, which means the boundary is
knowable from the set. Read the landscape legend's notes column before writing the boundary rows; it
is the cheapest boundary evidence in the trade.

Decorative granite is the exception that runs the other way. Municipal and vehicular curb stays with
site work, and a granite wall curb or a granite-clad planter wall comes back into landscape, at a rate
several times the ordinary curb rate.

### Irrigation: the seam that is really three trades

Irrigation sits in the landscape package and is executed at the boundary of plumbing and electrical.
One bidder attached eleven separate qualifications to its irrigation line, more than the rest of its
proposal combined. The recurring boundary set:

- The point of connection, the backflow preventer, the meter, the pumps and all interior plumbing are
  by others, and the bidder specifies the stub it needs.
- Sleeves under paving are excluded, or furnished material-only for someone else to place. Sleeves have
  to go in before the paving does, which puts an irrigation decision on the site paving schedule.
- All 110-volt work, conduit from the controller to each point of connection, coring and link seals
  are by others. One bidder stated flatly that no electricians are included and that any request for
  one is an add.
- A master valve and flow sensor may be furnished material-only for the owner's plumber to install on
  interior piping.
- Low-voltage permits may be offered as a percentage add on the irrigation value with a stated minimum.
- The system is priced against an assumed pressure and flow. If the water service cannot deliver them,
  the design changes.

An irrigation line that says only "irrigation" will be answered by bidders carrying wildly different
fractions of the work. On one project the estimator's own sheet cell read "Irrigation?" with a
question mark, which is an honest summary of the state of the row.

Note also that irrigation appears as an allowance rather than a price on some proposals. An allowance
is not comparable to a quote, and a ranking that treats them as the same number is comparing a
commitment to a placeholder.

### Other seams

- Bollards. The landscape package buys the bollard and sets it. The concrete footing is excluded, the
  electrical is excluded, and on a project with several dozen bollards the fixture cost is one of the
  largest lines in the package. The drawing's lighting note usually says controls and circuitry by
  others, see electrical. Between the landscape legend and the electrical drawings there is a fixture
  that both packages assume the other is handling.
- Fire features and outdoor kitchens. The landscaper buys the fire table, the grills, the pizza oven,
  the refrigerator drawers and the counter, and sets them. All plumbing, gas, electrical and final
  connections are by others. The gas run to a rooftop fire table is nobody's until someone notices.
  The code-required emergency shut-offs are the landscaper's on at least one project, priced per
  device.
- Site furnishings and the specialties package. Bike racks, trash receptacles and benches drift
  between packages. They appear on landscape proposals, on landscape scope sheets, and in the
  specialties package on some projects. Interior bike parking has appeared on a landscape scope sheet
  alongside a note recording a count discrepancy against the supplier's quote.
- Structures. Pergolas, arbors and cabanas are bought as manufactured products by the landscaper,
  fabricated by a steel shop, or sold from an outdoor-living catalogue, and in every case the footings
  are by others. The panel and the structure are frequently separate purchases from separate vendors,
  and installation is frequently a third line. On one project the number that finally landed on the
  sheet was assembled from a structure vendor's figure, a panel manufacturer's figure and a
  general contractor plug for installation, none of which was a proposal for the whole thing.
- Granite and stone fabricators. Material only, priced at the plant, with delivery per load and
  demurrage separate. Whether anchor holes are drilled, whether treads are tipped in the field and
  whether installation is included at all are determined item by item, sometimes by a checkbox on a
  form. A supply rate and an installed rate for the same curb differ by several times, and neither
  document usually names its mode.
- Site furnishings manufacturers. These issue a bidder's quote to every bidder on the list, addressed
  to nobody, so several competing landscape proposals rest on the same underlying furniture price and
  differ only in markup, freight and install labour. Their terms subordinate the contract documents:
  the quote states that only the manufacturer's standard limited warranty applies and that its terms
  control any conflict between documents. A specification's warranty requirement does not survive that
  unless somebody negotiates it back in. Cancellation penalties, custom-order price adjustments after
  engineering, and storage fees after fabrication completes are all real and all in the fine print.
- Fences. Fence specialists commonly carry their own concrete footings while the whole-package
  landscape bidder excludes footings on everything. Two fence numbers on one project can differ by
  exactly that.
- Pools. The pool builder takes the site at a stated depth below final grade, disposes its excavation
  spoil on site, and excludes the coping, the deck, the deck sealant, the backfill, the export, the
  hoisting, the fencing and every electrical and gas connection including bonding and grounding of the
  pool anchors. It supplies rails, ladders and a lift and requires somebody else to install their
  anchors, and supplies depth markers for the deck contractor to install. The deck around a pool is
  the landscape or hardscape package. The coping is an option on the pool bid and an exclusion on the
  landscape bid, which is how it falls between them.
- Green roof against roofing. The assembly above the membrane has left the roofing package and lives
  here. See the membrane seam above.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What landscape bidders actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The landscape drawings, by sheet, and specifically the materials plan and its material legend. On
   a project without a Division 32 specification, the legend is the specification. That is not an
   inference: asked directly for the missing spec sections, a landscape architect answered that it was
   not providing specifications at design development and that material and product information is
   found in the materials legend on the materials plan, and the general contractor instructed the
   bidder to price off the drawings. Expect the legend to carry the manufacturer, the model, the
   finish, the colour and the detail reference, and expect that to be all there is.
2. The details, by sheet and detail number. Bidders quote the detail number on the priced row, because
   the detail is where the build-up lives. A build-up with no detail is a build-up the bidder invents.
3. The planting schedule. At construction documents it carries species, size and count. At design
   development it frequently does not: on one project the planting sheet carried tree quantities only,
   and the quantities for shrubs, perennials and groundcover simply were not on the set. Treat a
   design-development planting line as an allowance carry, not a quantity.
4. The civil drawings, for everything the landscape sheets route there and for the drivable-lawn areas
   which are drawn on civil and bid here.
5. The specification where one exists, cited by section number, mostly for the maintenance and warranty
   durations and the soil mixes.

They do not price from a measured takeoff, with one striking exception: a bidder published its own
takeoff as marked-up drawings with computed areas and lengths appended to its proposal. That document
is worth more than the proposal, because it lets a quantity be checked. It also exposed a two-to-one
divergence against a competitor's stated quantity for the same paver mark, on a project where the
totals sat within a third of each other.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Whether the package is on grade only, over structure only, or both. The single largest variable.
- Labor type, union or open shop, which varies by site rather than by trade: one suburban project drew
  an entirely open-shop list while an urban project on the same bid calendar drew both.
- The construction schedule against the planting season. A landscape package installed outside the
  planting windows is either unguaranteed or replanted.
- The install date itself, which in this trade can be a long way out. One permit-set proposal named an
  installation season more than a year after the proposal date, which is a very long exposure on
  perishable, market-priced goods.
- Whether the plant material is available. Regional shortages produce substitutions, and bidders
  declare the possibility generically rather than naming a species.
- Whether the schedule is even internally plausible. A bidder found a planting schedule listing
  groundcover at a height class appropriate to a small tree, told the general contractor, and excluded
  it. That kind of schedule error is invisible to anyone reading the sheet as a quantity source.
- Whether the specification exists at all for this division. See above.
- The maintenance and guarantee durations the owner actually wants.
- Water availability, pressure and cost during establishment.
- Whether a mock-up is expected, which is often demanded in a drawing general note and priced by
  nobody.
- Crane and hoist availability and cost for anything over structure, and the working day assumed on a
  crane day.
- Whether the roof membrane will be tested and closed off before the green roof mobilizes.
- The sequence against finish paving, and whether the landscaper can complete before it goes down.
- Access for machines. Bidders assume it; a courtyard reached only through a building is a different
  job.
- Tariff and escalation exposure on steel, aluminum and imported stone.
- Whether there are prevailing wage requirements. Bidders state it, always as a negative.
- Whether the owner or the general contractor is buying the movable furniture.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders openly price around missing information and say so:
budgets that state the documents have insufficient information to fix a price, "no detail shown" rows
with invented build-ups, "no spec" rows, planting lines that cite a partial schedule, and irrigation
carried as an allowance. One bidder produced a pre-request-for-proposal budget explicitly offered as a
starting point and not as a commitment. By permit and construction documents the same companies switch
to citing mark ranges, sheet numbers and detail numbers and stating quantities. Note also that a
proposal's cited basis and its folder's set stage disagree often enough to check every time, and that
a bidder can cite two different plan dates in one document.

One design-development artifact deserves its own warning, because it propagates. That
pre-request-for-proposal budget later supplied the quantity column of the general contractor's own
leveling sheet, and the estimator's scope rows reproduce the bidder's note text word for word. The
project's scope definition, its soil depths, its assumed products and its at-grade versus courtyard
organization were all adopted from one landscape contractor's concept-stage guesses. That is worth
knowing before treating a scope sheet's quantities as the general contractor's independent view.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, for plants, by species and by size class. Trees are sized by caliper in inches for deciduous
  and by height in feet for evergreen; shrubs, perennials and grasses by container size. The size class
  drives the rate steeply and non-linearly: within one bid the top evergreen height band ran more than
  three times the bottom, and a large-caliber deciduous tree ran more than double a small one.
- Each, for every furnishing, planter, fixture, structure and fire feature.
- Square feet, for paving of every kind, lawn, seed, planting area, green roof area and geotextile.
- Cubic yards, for soil, loam, mulch, sand, aggregate base and stone dust. Depth generates the volume
  and the depth is always stated.
- Tons, for stone dust and bituminous base, where a bidder buys them by weight rather than by volume.
- Linear feet, for edging, curb, planter curb, planter wall, fence, bench runs and privacy screen.
- Linear feet, also, for custom timber benches and planter benches, which are sold by length rather
  than by count.
- Per load, for granite delivery, with demurrage separate.
- Per shipment and per manufacturer, for site furnishings freight, which is priced as its own row and
  is not small.
- Per visit, for green roof establishment maintenance.
- Days and weeks, for maintenance periods, guarantee periods and validity windows.
- Percent of contract value, for bonding and for insurance where the general contractor asks for them
  as alternates.
- Square feet of site area less building footprint, or cost per residential unit, as the normalizing
  denominator. Which one is used varies by project, and see the warning below.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. On grade against over structure. Over-structure paving has carried more than one and a half times
   the on-grade rate for a comparable paver on the same sheet, before any of the assembly beneath it.
2. Scope breadth, which is not a price variable at all but dominates every ranking. See section 1.
3. The plant schedule: the count, the size classes, and how much of it is large-caliper tree.
4. Soil volume, which is depth times area and where depth is the variable nobody reads. A soil line can
   rival the planting line.
5. Structural soil, where specified, which can be the largest single soil component.
6. Paving area by mark, with the specialty marks (porcelain, wood tile, vehicular) at multiples of the
   common precast rate in some bids and below it in others, depending on what is under them.
7. The paving build-up split, meaning how many of the setting bed, concrete base and aggregate base
   layers the bidder carries.
8. Site furnishings and structures, which are catalogue goods and therefore a nearly pure add-or-delete
   block. Outdoor kitchens, cabanas and pergolas are individually large.
9. Irrigation, which on one project was the second-largest line in an on-grade bid.
10. Bollards and lit planters, where the fixture count is high.
11. Establishment maintenance duration and visit count, and the plant guarantee term. A two-year to
    one-year deduct has run around a sixteenth of a package base.
12. Freight, delivery and mobilization on the manufactured goods.
13. Craneage and hoisting on over-structure work.
14. Tariffs and material escalation on steel, aluminum and imported stone.
15. Bonding and insurance basis, quoted as alternates at meaningful percentages of the base.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. The honest summary is that this trade's spread
measures scope breadth first and price second, and any statement about it has to say which.

Within a single project, the whole-package landscape bidders priced on the same set have landed close:
about a tenth apart on one project and about one and a half times apart on another. Include the narrow
bidders and the suppliers in the same ranking and the spread runs to three times, twenty times, three
hundred times and, on one project, more than fifty times. Those larger numbers are not price
dispersion; they are a fireplace supplier, a granite quarry and a plant-only contractor being ranked
against a whole package.

What the tight spread does not prove. On the project where the whole-package bidders sat within a tenth
of each other, the estimator still added a plug worth about a quarter of the selected bidder's base for
scope its quote had missed, and carried an adjustment worth about a sixth on that bidder's earlier
round. Two bidders within a tenth of each other on the total have been observed stating quantities for
the same paver mark that differ by a factor of two, and a bidder can price a movable-furniture block
that every competitor refused and thereby rank highest for reasons that have nothing to do with its
rates. Scope convergence and price convergence are independent, and in a trade where the market and the
general contractor disagree by a quarter of the package about what is even in it, a tight spread mostly
means the bidders read the same drawing legend.

Two further warnings on rates specifically.

The denominator changes by project inside this trade. The same estimator normalized one project's
landscape package on square feet of site area less building footprint and another on cost per
residential unit. Neither rate travels to the other project. Worse, the site-less-building denominator
was applied unchanged to an over-structure green roof package, where site area has no relationship to
the work, producing a rate several times the on-grade rate that means nothing at all. Always name the
quantity source before comparing anything.

The same unit can straddle the furnish and install seam invisibly. A general contractor's own rate per
linear foot for granite curb has run several times a granite supplier's material rate for the same
curb, because one is furnish, install and base and the other is material at the quarry gate. Neither
document said which. The same trap sits under every paver rate, since some include the base and some
do not.

Finally, how an estimator actually compares. It does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus
plugs, where the plugs are the structural gap list in section 3, and in this trade the plugs are large:
adjustments of a sixth and a quarter of a base bid on the selected bidder, and a plug worth about half
its own bid again on a bidder that priced only part of the package. The estimator's own rate card is
worth knowing too, because it is coarse where the market is fine: one sheet applied a single rate per
square foot to five different paver types, from a small precast unit to a large-format porcelain,
because the point of the plug is coverage rather than accuracy.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about thirty landscape, green roof, hardscape, fence, structure, granite,
site furnishings and pool proposals, plus three estimator leveling sheets covering five scope tabs,
drawn from three multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general
contractor. The proposals span pre-request-for-proposal budgets, design development, permit and
construction document rounds, and the bidder pool includes several companies that appear on more than
one project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4
testable. Several bidders are represented by three to five documents each, so the revision patterns in
section 2 rest on real ladders rather than single pairs.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity courtyards and roof terraces,
  in one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial, institutional,
  campus, municipal, streetscape or public prevailing-wage work, and streetscape in particular has a
  different sponsor and a different approval regime.
- No project in the corpus carried a large at-grade site with substantial stormwater landscape
  infrastructure: no bioretention basin, rain garden, tree trench or structural cell system appears,
  and those are the places where the landscape and civil packages genuinely fuse.
- Only one project carried a swimming pool, so the pool carve-out in sections 1 and 5 rests on that
  project alone.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation. In particular, nobody knows who ended up owning the overburden-removal
  obligation on any of these projects.
- No change order history and no maintenance or replacement history is in the corpus. The claim that
  planting outside the seasonal window converts into replacement cost is read out of the bidders' own
  language rather than observed, so treat it as untested here.
- The plant-schedule grain rules rest on one bidder that prices species by species. Two other grain
  shapes for the same scope are documented, but the species-level rates come from a single source.
- No project in the corpus used a native or restoration planting palette at scale, and none involved
  wetland replication, conservation-commission conditions or a mitigation planting requirement, all of
  which carry their own monitoring and reporting obligations that would sit in this package.
- Tree protection and preservation of existing trees appears only as an exclusion. No proposal in the
  corpus prices arborist work, root pruning or protection zones, so where that scope actually gets
  bought is unknown.
- Snow and winter operations appear only as an exclusion. Multi-year projects carrying seasonal work
  across a winter are not represented, so the carry-over question raised by long installation dates is
  open.
- No project used a landscape performance bond, a maintenance bond beyond the establishment period, or
  a long-term maintenance contract, so the commercial shape of extended landscape maintenance is
  unknown here.
- Only one bidder in the corpus published a measured takeoff, so every statement about checkable
  quantities rests on that single document.
