# Concrete

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the cast-in-place
concrete package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator
leveling sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried
by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real concrete contractors can bid it and a real
estimator can level it.

One thing to settle before anything else. This trade runs in two regimes, and they share almost
nothing but a division number.

New construction is a structure package. Footings, foundation walls, columns and piers, slab on
grade, and a supported podium slab that is usually post-tensioned. Numbers run into the millions,
the bid list is deep, and the whole package is normally furnished and installed by one contractor
who buys its own concrete, its own steel and its own pump.

Renovation and infill is a patch package. Slab infills at existing openings, slabs on metal deck,
piers on existing footings, elevator and sump pits cut into an existing basement, stair pan fill,
sidewalk replacement. Numbers are two orders of magnitude smaller, the scope is defined by where in
the building it occurs rather than by what element it is, the access governs the price more than the
volume does, and the exclusion list is longer than the scope list by a factor of six.

Say which regime you are in before you write a single scope line. A renovation scope list organized
by element family will miss most of the work, and a new-construction list organized by sheet number
will be unreadable.

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 033000 (cast-in-place concrete) is the anchor and the token estimators sort on. Related
sections that show up on the same leveling workbook as separate tabs: 034000 for precast, 035413 or
035400 for cementitious underlayment, and 033816 for post-tensioning where a supplier cites a
section at all, which is rare.

The thing to understand about this package is that it is not one trade. It is a chain of six
operations, and the market sells them in different combinations:

1. Formwork
2. Reinforcing, meaning rebar, welded wire fabric, and post-tensioning tendons
3. Concrete supply, meaning the ready-mix material itself
4. Pumping or placing equipment
5. Placement and finishing
6. Curing and protection

The default in new construction is that one contractor carries all six and calls it furnish and
install. But every one of the six is separately purchasable, every one has its own market with its
own kind of company and its own pricing instrument, and quotes for the separable pieces arrive in
the same folder as the whole-package bids. This is the single largest leveling hazard in the trade.
A rebar supplier's quote, a ready-mix rate card, a post-tensioning material quote and a pump rental
sheet are not bids and are not additive to a structural contractor's number, because that number
already contains all four. Adding them double-counts. Ranking them against each other compares a
material price to a completed structure.

So the first question on any concrete document is not what it costs. It is which of the six
operations it contains.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits elsewhere:

- Reinforcing steel and post-tensioning. Nearly every full-package bidder writes supply and install
  rebar, mesh, tendons and stud rails into its base, so reinforcing is inside the concrete package
  by default even though it is a distinct supply chain with its own suppliers and its own
  escalation clauses.
- Vapor barrier under slab on grade. Carried by nearly every full-package bidder, sometimes as its
  own priced line. Watch the thickness, because it is a routine value-engineering target.
- Rigid insulation under slab and at foundation walls. Genuinely split. Some bidders carry it at one
  face of the wall and under slab, some exclude it entirely, and one excluded it on the ground that
  it sits on the outside face of the wall and therefore belongs with waterproofing. Insulation
  position is the discriminator, so a scope line reading only "rigid insulation" is unbiddable.
- Grouting of column base plates. Nearly always carried, and nearly always in the same breath as
  installing anchor bolts furnished by others.
- Concrete general conditions, meaning mobilization, engineering and shop drawings, punch and close
  out, and hoisting. Some bidders price this as a first-class named block worth a few percent of the
  package. Others bury it. Comparing a base bid that contains a general conditions block against one
  that does not is a routine and invisible error.
- Site concrete, sometimes. See section 5, because this is the most contested boundary in the trade.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like concrete:

- Precast structure, including precast parking decks. A different industry with a different
  procurement track. It may be bought by the owner in design assist before a general contractor is
  even selected, and priced first as engineering services rather than as construction.
- Cementitious underlayment and gypsum-based floor toppings. Always a separate package with its own
  bid list, its own leveling tab and no overlap in bidders. Do not let a topping slab callout pull
  it into this scope.
- Shotcrete. A specialty placement method with its own applicators, bought when a wall cannot be
  formed conventionally.
- Curbing, whether cast in place, slip formed, precast or granite. Excluded by every full-package
  bidder seen, without exception, and routinely also excluded by the site concrete bidder. This is a
  reliable gap.
- Light pole bases and bollard foundations. Frequently assumed precast and furnished by others, and
  frequently carried by nobody.
- Polishing, staining, sealed decorative floors and architectural form-lined finishes. Excluded
  almost universally, usually with an explicit note that only a plain as-cast class of finish is
  included.

## 2. How the market bids

There is no single proposal shape in this trade. There are four, and which one you get depends on
what kind of company is bidding.

The full-package structural bid. One lump sum, or a small number of family subtotals, with a scope
narrative that carries counted quantities on nearly every element. This is the dominant shape for
new construction. Element counts and areas are stated, usually marked approximate; unit prices are
not. The families are almost always some cut of foundations, flatwork and site, or of building and
garage, or by building level. Expect somewhere between one and eight priced numbers.

The quantified takeoff bid. Rarer, and unmistakable when it appears. Every scope family split into
separate material, labor and equipment subtotals, with flatwork labor sometimes broken out as a
fourth column, and a full element takeoff attached with dimensions and counts. This is the only
shape that lets you see the labor and material split, and it is worth reading closely even when the
bidder does not win, because it is the best quantity source in the folder.

The material rate card. From ready-mix suppliers, rebar fabricators, post-tensioning suppliers and
pump companies. Priced per cubic yard, per ton, per square foot or per hour, with no project
quantity, no total, and often no reference to the drawings at all. It is a price list, not a bid.

The renovation patch bid. A short scope paragraph, a lump sum, and a very long exclusion list. Often
split into two or more separate proposals covering different parts of the building, each explicitly
excluding the others.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The basis drawings and their date. Nearly universal, and the most reliable field in the document.
  Some cite the sheet range explicitly, and a few cite two different dates within one set where a
  partial reissue landed.
- Counted quantities on the major elements. Common in new construction, absent in renovation.
  Footings by count, walls by linear foot, slabs by square foot at each thickness, and concrete
  volume in cubic yards.
- Which hardware is furnished by others and installed by this bidder. Nearly always stated, and
  usually the clearest paragraph in the document.
- Whether pumping is included. Commonly stated, and it matters, because it is also sold separately.
- Labor type, union or open shop, or at least a statement that the price is non-prevailing-wage.
  Stated by about half; an estimator records it for every bidder regardless.
- Mobilization count, and a price per additional mobilization.
- Whether sales tax is in the number.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Any breakdown between material and labor. Only the quantified takeoff shape shows it.
- Rebar tonnage, unless the bidder is a steel supplier. Full-package bidders commonly carry
  reinforcing as an unquantified line inside each family, so the steel quantity is invisible in a
  bid that plainly contains steel.
- Concrete strength by element. The mix schedule is spec and structural information that bidders
  price against silently, and only the supply rate card exposes what each strength costs.
- Testing. Excluded by every bidder seen, in every regime, without exception. The general contractor
  or owner buys it.
- Winter conditions. Excluded by nearly every bidder seen, and the exclusion is usually a long list
  in itself: hot water, ice, blankets, heaters, fuel, enclosures, temporary heat, wind breaks,
  accelerating admixtures, snow and ice removal. One bidder in the corpus priced a weather provision
  covering the whole project as an option, and it was worth several percent of its base. That is the
  scale of what everyone else is leaving out.
- Durations. Almost never stated, though where a bidder did volunteer one it named a foundation
  duration of about twelve weeks from footings to completion before steel could start, and another
  gave a pour-day count for every single placement.

Revision habits are more varied here than in most trades, and the revision mechanism matters more
than the price movement. Shapes seen:

- Silent reprice. Family subtotals move, every scope word and every stated quantity is identical,
  and the proposal number, letterhead date and expiry date are all unchanged from the previous
  round. Nothing inside the document marks it as a revision. Expect this and check the numbers, not
  the header.
- Alternate-only revision. The base is byte-stable across three rounds and each round appends a new
  priced block. No money moves in the base at all; the information is entirely in what got added.
- Scope entry with a price decline. A round total falls while the scope list materially grows,
  typically across a structural set revision. This is the most dangerous shape in the trade, because
  comparing round totals reads it as a small saving when it is a large one. Always diff the scope
  lists across rounds before believing a delta.
- Scope entry as a price rise. A named, sheet-cited line is added and the base moves up modestly.
  The honest version of the same event.
- Negotiation ladder. Revision numbers into the high single digits, with the intermediate rounds
  frequently missing from the file.
- Conformed-set reconfirmation. The general contractor re-solicits on a later set and asks in
  writing for either updated pricing or a written confirmation that pricing is unaffected. A written
  no-change reply is accepted as the revised bid, so a revision may be one sentence in an email and
  no document at all.

Two consequences worth holding onto. First, a document labelled as a revision is often not one. A
second bid from the same company may be a complementary scope that explicitly excludes the first
one's work by quote number, in which case the two are additive and treating the later as superseding
the earlier silently deletes an entire chunk of the package. Second, the commercial terms that
decide the award frequently exist only in email: bond premium as a percentage of contract value,
credit for an owner-controlled insurance program, credit to shorten a warranty term. The proposal
will not carry them and the folder may.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Concrete scope sheets in this market have run from about forty rows on a small renovation to well
over three hundred on a large new-construction package where the sheet carries the commercial and
contract questionnaire as well. For the scope portion alone, target roughly eighty to a hundred and
twenty lines for a full podium or garage package, and forty to sixty for a renovation infill
package.

### The primary organizing axis

For new construction, organize by structural family first and by location second: foundations,
walls, columns and piers, slab on grade, supported slab, then repeat the set for each distinct
structure (residential building, parking garage, amenity area), then exterior and site.

For renovation, organize by structural sheet and building level. Sub-basement, basement, level one,
mezzanine, typical floors, roof. This is what estimators actually do on renovation work, and it is
right, because the scope is defined by where in the existing building it occurs.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Each foundation element type, separately: isolated and integral column footings, continuous wall
  footings, mat foundations, uplift or hold-down footings, piers, and grade beams. These are counted
  differently by every bidder and the counts diverge. One project produced three different counts of
  the same uplift footing family across three documents, and one bidder did not carry the family at
  all.
- Each slab by thickness and by elevation. Not "slab on grade" but five inch slab on grade at each
  distinct elevation, four inch slab on grade, six inch garage slab, topping slab at each thickness.
  Thickness is the priced variable and it is also the first value-engineering target.
- Supported slab by system and thickness. Post-tensioned slab at each thickness is its own line,
  because the tendon layout, the shoring, the stressing and the long lead all attach to it. Slab on
  metal deck is a different line again, with a different reinforcing and a different finish problem.
- Walls by thickness and height band. Bidders price walls as linear feet at a stated thickness and
  height, and a single "foundation walls" line will not survive contact with a real bid. Frost
  walls, shear walls, retaining walls, elevator and stair shaft walls and one-sided walls all price
  differently.
- One-sided walls against existing structure or against a support of excavation system, always its
  own line. This is specialty formwork, it carries a substrate and a tolerance allowance, and one
  bidder priced it as a lump plus an explicit per-square-foot adder. Another logged it as scope to
  be determined pending a survey, which is to say unpriceable at bid time.
- Pumping, as its own line, whether or not it is expected inside the base. It is bought both ways.
- Concrete supply, as its own line, for the same reason.
- Reinforcing, split at minimum into rebar, welded wire fabric, and post-tensioning, and split again
  for any coated or specialty bar. Epoxy coated, galvanized, stainless, weldable, threaded and
  assembled bar are excluded by essentially every supplier and every placer as standard boilerplate,
  so if the drawings call for them and no line names them, they are a gap.
- Anchor bolts, embed plates, sleeves and hold-down hardware, as separate lines, with the furnish
  side named. See section 5, because this is where the trade leaks.
- Housekeeping and equipment pads, enumerated by the discipline that owns the equipment: fire
  protection, plumbing, heating and cooling, electrical, elevator. Not as one line. This is the
  single most reliable gap in the trade and section 6 explains why.
- Winter and hot weather provisions, as their own line with a stated basis. Nobody carries it and
  somebody has to.
- Testing and inspection, as its own line, assigned to the general contractor or owner.
- Concrete general conditions as its own line: mobilization count, engineering and shop drawings,
  layout and survey, hoisting, punch and closeout.
- Curing and sealing method, as its own line. Curing compound, wet curing, cure and seal, and a
  densifier are four different answers at four different prices and bidders exclude water curing
  almost universally.
- Saw cutting and joints, split by control joints, expansion joints, isolation joints and joint
  sealant. Joint sealant in particular is excluded by nearly every structural bidder and picked up
  by nobody.
- Each site concrete element as its own line, and each site object footing as its own line. Not
  "site concrete." See section 5.
- On renovation, an existing-conditions block: demolition and roughening of existing concrete,
  bonding agent, drill and epoxy dowels into existing, patching at existing openings, shoring of
  existing structure to permit the new work, and repair details. Six to ten rows, each answered
  differently by different bidders.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Formwork does not get its own line separate from the element it forms. Every bidder prices form,
  reinforce, place and finish as one operation per element. The only exception is the quantified
  takeoff shape, and that is a bidder's internal structure, not a scope grain.
- Individual footing marks collapse into a footing type line. A takeoff may distinguish two dozen
  footing marks by dimension; a scope line set does not need to, because they price at one rate.
- Mix design by element collapses into the element line. Strength is an attribute, and it is the
  supplier's line item, not the scope list's.
- Curing compound, saw cutting and edge forming within a slab family collapse into the slab line
  unless the specification calls for a method the market treats as separate, which wet curing and
  densifiers are.
- Individual column counts collapse into a columns line per height condition.
- Room-by-room or bay-by-bay enumeration collapses to the level or the structure. No document seen
  prices a single bay.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be silent
about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers.

Applied here, the test fires hardest on three things: any line where the furnish side and the
install side could sit with different parties, any line that sits at the boundary between the
building and the site, and any line describing a condition rather than an element, which is to say
weather, access, sequence and tolerance.

It also tells you when silence is a finding. If a bidder is silent on exterior flatwork while its
competitor prices it as a named family, that silence is a gap to surface, not an inclusion to
assume. An estimator will resolve it by lifting the competitor's priced line straight into the
silent bidder's column, which is a defensible plug and an obvious signal that the scope sheet had no
row for it.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs. Scope that no bidder reliably carries, that the general contractor must add to
every number before comparing, and that a scope line set naming them explicitly is plugging in
advance:

1. Winter and hot weather provisions.
2. Testing and inspection.
3. Housekeeping and equipment pads.
4. Joint sealants and caulking at concrete joints.
5. Curbing of every kind.
6. Site object footings: bollards, light poles, fences, benches, bike racks, signage, trash
   enclosures.
7. Concrete washout provision and disposal of excess concrete.
8. Bonds, and the delta between an owner-controlled insurance program and conventional coverage.
9. Temporary shoring, reshoring and underpinning where it is not the sequencing the bidder assumed.
10. Duct bank and conduit encasement.
11. Safety railing and edge protection at elevated slabs, which some bidders carry and many exclude.
12. Moisture mitigation, whether admixture or topical.

Twelve items. A scope line set that names all twelve is doing the estimator's plugging for it.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

The exclusion lists in this trade are long, boilerplate, and near-identical between competitors.
Forty to fifty lines is normal on a new-construction bid and one renovation bid ran to fifty-four
against an eight-line scope. Repeat bidders reproduce their lists verbatim between projects. Read
them for the trade map, not for the project.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Nearly all of the table above is boilerplate. What is project signal, because it was written for
this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's other proposals:

- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. A supplier that writes "reinforcement details
  unavailable" and then discloses the reinforcing it assumed instead is telling you the documents
  are incomplete and showing you exactly where. Another wrote that a post-tensioned slab did not
  appear on the plans at all, and assumed a tendon quantity between two gridlines.
- An exclusion justified by absence of locations. A repair detail excluded because the locations are
  not shown, with a note that it will be tracked on time and material, is a documents defect. When
  two independent bidders exclude the same sheet for the same stated reason, that is the market
  pre-writing your request for information.
- An exclusion with a sheet reference. A bidder that names the detail it will not build has read the
  drawings.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a support of excavation system, an
  existing structure to remain, a specific tenant space, a sub-basement.

### Deviations, which are neither exclusions nor inclusions

This trade has a habit of disclosing substitutions quietly, inside the exclusion list, phrased as an
exclusion with a parenthetical. A bidder excluding an exposed aggregate sidewalk while noting that
it carried a broomed municipal mix instead has not excluded the sidewalk. It has priced a different
sidewalk. Read every exclusion for a parenthetical that names what was carried in its place.

The most consequential deviations seen came from a precast fabricator working in design assist,
which disclosed that a drawn floor-to-floor height did not meet code and it had carried a taller
one, that the garage had no structural grid at all so it had supplied one, and that a drawn stair
did not work structurally so it had substituted another type. When the documents are incomplete
enough, the trade authors them, and the deviations paragraph is where it says so.

Watch also for the proposal that contradicts itself. A site concrete bid priced exposed aggregate
paving as a counted, quantified line and excluded specialty slab finishes including exposed
aggregate in the same document. The priced breakout is authoritative and the exclusion list is a
pasted template. Never level off exclusion lists alone.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts a lump sum into a change
order.

- Placement outside normal working hours. One bidder's price is explicitly based on being permitted
  to start and complete placements outside normal hours without zoning restriction; others base
  their price on standard weekday hours and charge for anything else. These two bases are opposites
  and they are both stated as qualifications rather than as scope.
- Mobilization count. Almost every bidder states one, and a price per additional mobilization is
  common. On a phased job this is a real number.
- Grade tolerance at turnover. Bidders state the tolerance at which they will accept the subgrade,
  and it varies from a tenth of a foot to a quarter of an inch depending on what is being poured on
  it. This is a handoff specification the site contractor has to meet and rarely knows about.
- Open cut excavation assumed. A bid based on open cut explicitly excludes working within a support
  of excavation system, the surcharge limitations that come with it, and single-sided walls poured
  against it. If the site has a support system, this qualification voids a large part of the price.
- Truck, pump and hoisting access to all work areas at grade. Assumed by nearly every bidder and
  guaranteed by nobody.
- Coordination drawings for embeds furnished and approved by others. Not merely furnished. Approved.
- Continuous uninterrupted work once mobilized.
- Price validity, commonly thirty to ninety days, and one supplier quote expired ten days after
  issue against a process that ran for months.
- Union or open shop, which is the single largest price driver in this trade.

Two commercial qualifications deserve their own mention because they are not really qualifications.
Bidders reserve the right to negotiate subcontract language after award even where a sample
subcontract was issued with the bid documents, and they state payment terms that can be materially
better than the contract's. Both change what a number is worth.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section that matters most in this trade, and it is long because the seams are where the
money leaks.

### The six-operation seam, and how it is priced

The clearest statement of what each operation is worth comes from the specialty applicators, because
they price the seam explicitly. A shotcrete proposal carried its base price and then a deduct
alternate if the general contractor supplies the concrete, worth roughly a quarter to a third of the
base depending on the wall. That is the material share of a placement price, stated as an either or.

The same seam appears elsewhere less visibly. A site concrete bid listed every single element three
times: once under forming and preparation in square feet, once under placement, finishing and curing
in the same square feet, and once under concrete supply in cubic yards. Reading that document as a
quantity list and summing the square foot column triple counts the work. Read the operation heading
before you read the number.

### Reinforcing: the furnish and install seam that hides in a unit

Rebar is sold three ways and all three are priced per ton.

1. Furnish only. A fabricator delivers bundled, tagged, cut and bent steel to the site, priced per
   ton by bar type and destination, with mesh by square foot, chairs and bolsters by linear foot,
   freight by delivery count, and an escalation clause. It excludes placement in language you have
   to look for.
2. Install only. A rebar placing contractor, frequently union ironworkers, priced per ton, with
   terms that are all labor terms: crane and lift provided by others at no cost, straight-time
   forty-hour week, premium time reimbursed, material delivery tickets not its responsibility.
3. Furnish and install, inside a full-package concrete bid, unquantified.

The trap is that one and two look identical on the page. Both are a per-ton rate with a tonnage. One
project carried two rebar quotes whose per-ton rates differed by about eleven percent, which is far
too small a gap to contain placement labor, and neither document ever states its package mode in
words. Establish furnish versus install before you compare anything, and never add either to a
full-package bid that already says supply and install rebar.

Post-tensioning has the same structure and states it more clearly. A material-only tendon supplier
excludes every field operation by name: unloading, installation, inspection, concreting, stressing,
cutting, capping and grouting. It also excludes the rebar and suggests an allowance percentage for
it, excludes the support and backup bars, and counts the bulkhead holes and grout pockets that
others must provide. It is entirely non-additive to a structural bid that carries tendons.

### Embeds, anchor bolts and hold-downs

This is the most reliably enumerated seam in the trade and it still leaks.

The market convention is settled and nearly unanimous: anchor bolts, embed plates, sleeves, inserts
and similar hardware are furnished by others and installed by the concrete contractor. Bidders write
it as its own qualification. One general contractor's scope sheet listed eleven separate embed
conditions, one per element type, and every one of them was answered install only.

Three complications:

- The furnish side is somebody else's package and frequently nobody's. The embed plates for a
  pergola, the weldable angles for railings, the plates for masonry clips, the plates for a canopy
  and the relieving angle plates each belong to a different trade's scope, and the concrete
  contractor will set whatever arrives.
- Hold-downs on wood-framed work break the pattern. Bidders push hold-down anchors to the framer and
  one went further, requiring the framer to guarantee the locations before the pour. That makes it a
  three-party seam: the concrete contractor forms it, the framer sets it, the framer warrants it. If
  the framing scope does not carry that, it lands back on the concrete contractor as rework, and
  concrete rework is demolition.
- Coordination drawings for embeds are furnished and approved by others, and the concrete
  contractor installs to those drawings. If the coordination drawings are late, the embeds are late,
  and the pour waits.

The related seam is edge stops and pour stops at slabs on metal deck, which the concrete contractor
assumes are bent plate or metal pour stop by others. On renovation work this recurs, and it belongs
to steel or miscellaneous metals.

### Site concrete versus building concrete

The hardest boundary in the trade, and the drawings actively work against you here.

Site concrete is drawn on landscape sheets. On one project every single row of the estimator's site
concrete tab keyed to a landscape detail, not to a structural or civil sheet. A structural bidder
that reads only the structural set will not see it, and one bidder said so directly, excluding any
concrete scope shown only on the landscape drawings.

So the concrete package's scope is defined across five disciplines: structural for the frame,
architectural for topping slabs, canopies and stair fill, civil for sidewalks and municipal work,
landscape for everything in the courtyard, and electrical for equipment pads. Read all five.

Where the boundary actually falls, based on how bidders answer it:

- Flatwork, paver base and equipment pads: carried, by either the structural bidder or a site
  bidder, and often by both.
- Footings that hold up an object: pergola, arbor, cabana, trellis, fence, gate, bench, bike rack,
  light bollard, dog waste station, monument sign, fire pit. This is where site bidders stop. Most
  answered no to most of these. It is the reliable gap.
- Curbing and light pole bases: nobody.

The bidder pool crosses the boundary too. An excavation contractor may bid the site concrete, a
structural concrete contractor may carry exterior flatwork as a named priced family, and one company
split its own bid into a building proposal and a site proposal a day apart, priced off two different
drawing dates, each assuming the other carried a set of equipment pads. The same three pads appeared
in one document's base citing an architectural sheet and in the other's alternate citing a civil
sheet. Accepting both double counts them and accepting neither drops them.

### Post-tensioned slabs and the mechanical trades

You cannot core a post-tensioned slab afterward. Cutting a tendon is a structural failure and a
safety event, so every penetration has to be located and sleeved before the pour.

Asked what it needed from the mechanical, plumbing and sprinkler contractors, one concrete
contractor gave the same answer three times: CAD files and penetration locations in the
post-tensioned slabs. That is a hard gate, and it sits upstream of trades that have usually not been
bought yet at the time the podium is poured. It is the least visible schedule risk in the package.

The related gate is the pour sequence itself. A tendon supplier requires a written finalized pour
sequence with construction joint locations within two weeks of award, because shop drawings cannot
start without it, and it priced on the basis that the structural drawings showed no construction
joints at all. Intermediate anchors at joints that appear later are a chargeable scope change.

### Housekeeping and equipment pads

Nobody carries them, and the reason is instructive rather than commercial.

One general contractor enumerated ten housekeeping pad rows on its scope sheet, organized by the
discipline that owns the equipment: fire pump and jockey pump, plumbing equipment, a fuel oil tank,
electrical transformers and switchgear, a roof generator pad on dunnage, electric vehicle charging,
elevator pumps, seismic pads for all floor-mounted equipment, and a curb at a busway penetration.
Every one was answered as excluded and available on time and material.

Asked directly what it would need in order to price them, the bidder answered: size, location and
quantity. Those three facts live in equipment schedules owned by trades that have not bought their
equipment yet. The pads are not refused because they are hard. They are refused because they are not
yet knowable, and they stay not-knowable until well after the concrete is bought.

Two consequences. Carry housekeeping pads as an explicit allowance or a unit price with an assumed
area, which is what estimators do, at a rate per square foot. And expect to hear that the general
contractor will self-perform them, which is a legitimate answer and should be recorded as one.

### Precast

Where a precast structure adjoins cast-in-place work, the cast-in-place bidder prices a specific set
of interface items: footings for precast support, precast support piers, one-sided curb poured
against precast panels, precast embed plates, and a large count of precast connections. One takeoff
carried over five hundred of them.

The interface is priced from both sides by parties on different contracts, and the precast package
may have been procured by the owner before the general contractor was selected. Reconcile the two
sides deliberately.

### Other seams worth their own words

- Concrete supply and the general contractor. Ready-mix is quoted directly to the general
  contractor as often as to the subcontractor, because the general contractor uses the card to value
  engineer. One VE worksheet priced nine ideas almost entirely off per-cubic-yard mix differentials.
- Pumping. Bought twice. Most structural bidders include it; the pump company also quotes the
  general contractor directly on a rate card. Confirm which.
- Steel erection. The foundation duration gates steel, and one concrete VE idea, changing a pier
  detail so a steel column starts higher, saved money in the steel package as well as the concrete
  package. The bidder left the steel saving as a blank line for the general contractor to fill in.
  Concrete VE routinely moves another trade's money.
- Flatwork tolerance and the deck. A finisher excluded the specified floor tolerance on metal deck,
  because the flatness of a slab on deck is inherited from the deck and the frame, not produced by
  the finisher. The same bidder excluded grinding and flash patching caused by deflection of the
  steel frame. This is a genuine conflict between a specification written against the finisher and a
  condition owned by the erector, and it is worth its own scope line.
- Moisture mitigation. Contested and usually excluded from both sides. Admixture-based mitigation is
  excluded by nearly every concrete bidder, sometimes priced as a separate add. Topical mitigation
  sits with flooring and is contested there too. Where the specification calls for it and the
  drawings do not show it, the whole market will price it as an option rather than resolve it.
- Cementitious underlayment. A separate package entirely. Expect a row for it on the concrete
  leveling sheet purely as a coordination marker.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What concrete contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The structural drawings, by set date. Cited by essentially every bidder, frequently by sheet
   range. This is the primary document and the most reliable field in any proposal.
2. The structural details, individually. Foundation and wall sections drive the formwork, and
   estimators key their scope rows to specific detail numbers.
3. The architectural drawings, for topping slabs, stair pan fill, landings, canopies, and anything
   at grade in an occupied space. Bidders that carry stair fill cite architectural sheets for it.
4. The landscape drawings, for all site concrete. Frequently missed.
5. The civil drawings, for sidewalks, aprons, municipal work and duct banks.
6. The specification, mostly for mix designs, finish class, curing method and tolerance. Cited by
   number occasionally and quoted almost never. Suppliers routinely state that they have not
   reviewed the specification and will quote specification concrete only after they do.

They do not price from a model. One bidder named the drawing scale of each sheet it took off, which
is a tell that the takeoff was measured off a scaled sheet.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Union versus open shop labor. The largest single driver. On one project the five open-shop bidders
  sat within eleven percent of each other and the single union bidder was more than half again above
  the low, while carrying the narrowest scope of the six.
- Prevailing wage applicability, which is a third basis and one that leveling sheets have no column
  for.
- Whether the concrete supply, the pumping and the reinforcing are inside the bid or bought
  separately. This is the difference between comparable and incomparable numbers.
- The season the work will be placed in. Winter provisions are excluded by nearly everyone, and a
  ready-mix supplier surcharges every cubic yard delivered between the start of November and the
  middle of April. Hot weather temperature control is surcharged too, and counterintuitively the
  cooler the required delivered temperature the higher the charge.
- The pour sequence and construction joint locations, which gate post-tensioning shop drawings.
- Whether mechanical, plumbing and sprinkler penetration locations will exist before the podium
  pour.
- Site access and laydown. Whether a truck and a pump can reach every work area at grade, and
  whether there is space adjacent to the work for equipment and material. Assumed by everyone.
- Boom reach, which sets the pump size, and the price of the largest boom is nearly three times the
  smallest. Reach is a function of site geometry, laydown position and building height, stated
  nowhere.
- On renovation, the placement method. With no crane and no pump reach, concrete goes in by buggy
  through the freight elevator, and the availability of that elevator is a negotiated term that
  bidders write into their proposals as a general contractor obligation.
- Whether the subgrade will be delivered at the tolerance the bidder assumed.
- Whether there is a support of excavation system, because a bid based on open cut excludes working
  inside one.
- Mobilization count, driven by phasing.
- Whether placements can happen outside normal working hours, and whether zoning permits it.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies, and the warranty term. A two-year warranty
  as a base-bid requirement is invisible on the drawings and the credit to reduce it to one year is
  material.
- Project portal and payment application fees, quoted as a fraction of a percent of contract value
  plus a flat fee per sub-tier subcontractor.
- Tariff and escalation exposure on steel. A post-tensioning supplier disclosed specific tariff
  actions on imported steel and instructed the buyer to reconfirm price and schedule at award. A
  rebar supplier held price only to a stated month and then escalated per ton every six months. A
  ready-mix supplier escalated per cubic yard every January.
- Lead times. Rebar runs about two weeks from approved shop drawings, post-tensioning cables and
  non-standard stud rails about five weeks, and shop drawings themselves about two weeks from award.
  Post-tensioning is the long lead that sets the buyout date.

Two things deserve their own paragraphs because they are counterintuitive and they recur.

The formwork quantity is not the finish quantity. Asked to price an architectural board-form finish,
a bidder noted that the exposed board-form area was about seven hundred square feet but that the
formwork required to achieve the finish extends well beyond the exposed surface. Any architectural
concrete finish taken off the elevations understates the work that produces it, and this generalizes
past board form to any formed finish.

The drawings show the finished condition, not the means of reaching it. On a renovation, a bidder
warned by email that installing the drawn sump pits would require removing more existing slab than
shown and shoring the existing walls, and that it had priced exactly what the drawings show. The
drawn change was priceable and the buildable change was not. On renovation work, assume the demo,
the temporary support and the access that the new work implies are in nobody's number until you find
the line that carries them.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Cubic yards for concrete supply. The universal supply unit, priced per cubic yard by mix design,
  with a menu of per-cubic-yard admixture adders. Every ready-mix quote seen uses it.
- Tons for reinforcing steel, split by bar type and by coating. Every supplier and every placer.
- Square feet for welded wire fabric, and for every slab.
- Linear feet for walls, continuous footings, thickened slabs, haunches, edge forms and joints.
- Each for footings, columns, piers, anchor bolts, embeds, precast connections, stressing ends,
  stud rails and site object footings.
- Square feet of wall for shotcrete, quoted alongside a cubic yard volume.
- Pounds for post-tensioning strand, both bare and gross, and per pound for scope changes in both
  directions with the credit rate lower than the add rate.
- Hours for pump time beyond the on-site minimum, for operator overtime, for ready-mix waiting and
  standby, for unloading beyond an included allowance, and for re-detailing.
- Pump days or pump events, counted per work family. Two bidders did this and it is the only way to
  audit a pumping number against a rate card.
- Deliveries, as a counted quantity with a price per additional delivery.
- Pour days, as a placement schedule. Rare and extremely useful.
- Percent of contract value, for bonds and for project portal fees.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Labor type, union versus open shop. Tracked on every bidder in every leveling.
2. Package mode, meaning which of the six operations the number contains. This is not a price
   variable so much as a comparability variable, and it swamps everything else when it is wrong.
3. Total structural volume, driven by slab thickness, footing sizes and wall heights. Slab thickness
   is the first value-engineering target and a one inch reduction across a large slab on grade is
   worth a couple of percent of the whole package.
4. Formwork cycles, meaning the number of separate form-and-pour operations rather than the volume.
   Consolidating many small individual footings into thickened slab sections saves money even when
   it increases the concrete volume, and one bidder's own arithmetic showed exactly that.
5. Mix strength by element. Specifying a single high strength throughout rather than matching
   strength to element is a routine and easily recovered overspend, worth roughly seven percent of
   the supply cost per step of strength.
6. Post-tensioning extent and thickness, which carries shoring, stressing, long lead and
   coordination with it.
7. Admixtures. Corrosion inhibitor in particular is expensive per cubic yard and is frequently
   specified across a whole garage volume when only the genuinely exposed elements need it.
8. Season, through winter surcharges, hot weather temperature control, and the excluded winter
   provisions somebody has to buy.
9. Access, pump reach, hoisting and laydown.
10. Mobilization count and phasing.
11. Finish class and tolerance, including any architectural formed finish.
12. Escalation and tariff exposure on steel and cement, which is real and time-dependent rather than
    a contingency.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. Within one project the spread from lowest to highest
ran about one and a half times, and the entire open-shop field sat within about eleven percent of
each other with the single union bidder accounting for the whole rest of the range. Bid lists of
five or six structural bidders are normal on a large package. Renovation packages draw fewer, and
two independent renovation bids landed within a tenth of a percent of each other, which is a much
tighter market than painting or the finish trades.

One more convention worth knowing, and it is the most important one in this section. An estimator
does not normalize concrete on volume. It normalizes on a project denominator: building footprint
square feet on one project, dwelling units on another. Those are not comparable to each other, so a
cross-project rate in this trade is meaningless without its denominator travelling alongside it.

And the estimator's own budget rates are not element takeoffs. On one project footings, walls and
slab on grade were each priced against the same quantity, the garage footprint area, at three
different rates per square foot. Only the post-tensioned slabs, which do not scale with footprint,
were given their own measured areas. That is the working rule of thumb: take the footprint once, and
monetise it at a rate per element family. Expect it, and expect the bidders' element counts to
disagree with it and with each other.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about thirty concrete-family proposals plus five estimator leveling
tabs and one fully answered general contractor scope sheet of over three hundred rows, drawn from
four projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The documents span design
development, permit, construction document and conformed sets, and include several complete
same-bidder revision chains as well as bidders appearing on more than one project, which is what
makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable.

The proposal mix is unusually broad for one trade and that breadth is what section 1 rests on: full
package structural bids, one fully quantified labor-material-equipment takeoff, two ready-mix
supplier rate cards from different suppliers, two rebar quotes at different package modes, a
post-tensioning material quote, a pump rental rate card, a shotcrete applicator's two-configuration
menu, three site concrete rounds, a precast design assist proposal, and four renovation infill bids.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Three of the four projects are new multifamily residential with structured parking, and one is a
  renovation of an existing commercial building. Nothing here is tested against industrial, heavy
  civil, healthcare, institutional, tilt-up, or public prevailing-wage work, and the tilt-up and
  mass concrete exclusions that appear in boilerplate are therefore untested.
- The renovation regime described up front rests on a single project. It is internally corroborated
  by four independent bidders on that project, but a second renovation would tell us which of its
  patterns are the regime and which are that building.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is present, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation. In a trade where the exclusion lists are this long and this uniform, that is
  the largest single gap in the entry.
- No change order history is present. The claim that the access, sequence and weather qualifications
  convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed.
- Only one bid decomposed material from labor, so the labor share of a placed cubic yard rests on
  that single document plus the shotcrete deduct alternates.
- The rebar furnish-versus-install ambiguity described in section 5 is recorded as unresolved
  because neither document states its mode. It was not resolved by correspondence in the corpus
  either, so the market convention behind it is inferred from the rates rather than established.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the general contractor bought concrete supply, pumping
  and reinforcing directly and hired placement labor only, though every instrument needed to do it
  is present. Whether that is because it does not happen in this market or because the corpus has
  not caught one is unknown.
- The corpus has not shown slab on grade over contaminated or methane-bearing soil, mass concrete
  requiring thermal control monitoring, or architectural concrete as a primary finish, all of which
  appear only as exclusions.
- Testing is excluded by every bidder and the corpus does not show who actually buys it or what it
  costs.
