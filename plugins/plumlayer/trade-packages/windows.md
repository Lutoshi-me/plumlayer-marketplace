# Windows

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the windows package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. The
entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real window bidders can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

One orientation note before anything else. Painting is a trade where the proposal is nearly just a
number. Windows is the opposite. A window proposal will usually tell you exactly what it is pricing,
mark by mark, unit by unit, with a price on each one. That precision is a trap, because the thing it
is precise about is a product list, and the product list is only part of the package. The money and
the risk in this trade sit in what surrounds the product: who installs it, who receives it, who
flashes it, where it ships from, what it costs to land it, and whether the counts on the proposal
are the counts on the drawings. A scope line set that enumerates windows and stops has scoped the
easy half.

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The section number is unreliable as an identifier. The same trade appears under a generic windows
section on most projects and under a polymer-windows section where the specification is written
around a plastic frame system, and a single project can carry vinyl windows and vinyl terrace doors
as two separate specification sections while every bidder prices them as one package. Some proposals
name the section, many do not, and one proposal in the sample invented a null section number for a
scope block it did not know where to file. Use the trade token and the window schedule, not the
section number.

The package as bid in the market is windows and exterior doors that live in the window wall, not
windows alone. Balcony and terrace doors travel with the windows by default and almost always come
from the same manufacturer in the same frame system, because they are the same extrusion family. A
bidder that quotes windows and is silent on balcony doors has not narrowed its scope on purpose, it
has usually just been sent a partial schedule.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another specification section:

- Balcony, terrace and patio doors. The default, and the single most reliable inclusion after the
  windows themselves. Where the schedule tags them separately, bidders still price them in the same
  document, often as their own family with its own subtotal.
- Storefront at punched openings, transoms over entrances, and infill in louver openings. Genuinely
  contested. A window bidder will price a storefront family inside its windows quote while its own
  boilerplate exclusion list says storefronts are excluded, and both statements sit in the same
  document. Read the price table, not the note block.
- Interior storm windows on renovation work. Sometimes carried by the window bidder as its own
  section, sometimes bid directly by a specialty storm window supplier, sometimes both on the same
  project.
- Insect screens, limiters and window opening control devices. Nearly always included, and nearly
  always worth naming anyway, because they are a real cost and occasionally excluded at doors and
  sliders while included at windows in the same sentence.
- Nailing flanges, frame extensions and panning. Sometimes included, commonly excluded, and a
  recurring gap. See section 5.
- Field mulling of multi-unit assemblies. Included when the units arrive factory mulled and a real
  cost when they do not, and never shown on a drawing.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like windows:

- Curtain wall and true storefront systems. A different product, a different erector, and usually a
  different package, even where the same bidder can supply both.
- Glass guardrails, smoke baffle glass, handrail glass, mirrors, fire rated door lites, and interior
  glazing generally. These belong to the glazing package. They appear in windows proposals only
  because a glazing contractor is bidding the windows package and bringing its own product line
  along, and when it does they arrive as add alternates.
- Skylights, louvers, window washing anchors and bolts, and brake metal or composite panel at the
  facade. Excluded by essentially every window supplier seen.
- Window treatments, shades, blinds and drapery. A different trade in a different division. The word
  window appears in both trade tokens and they share nothing. Anything filed under a window
  treatments token belongs to the interior specialties package, and reading it into this package is
  a pure token collision.
- Demolition of existing windows on renovation work. Sometimes carried, sometimes an alternate,
  sometimes nobody's. See section 5.

The glazing contractor overlap deserves its own warning. On projects with any meaningful amount of
interior glass, a glazing subcontractor will bid the windows package and its number will carry
mirrors, fire rated door lites, smoke baffle and handrail glass, storefront doors, and sometimes
facade metal panel, all inside the base bid. That bidder is not expensive, it is bidding a different
scope, and its number cannot be ranked against a window supplier's number without stripping the
extras out first. The same bidder in a later round will move all of it into alternates and its base
will fall by a quarter with nothing having gotten cheaper.

## 2. How the market bids

This is an enumerating trade. The default output is a priced list keyed to the window schedule, and
a lump sum with no internal breakout is rare enough to be a finding when you see one.

Proposals arrive in three recognizable genres, and the genre tells you what the document can and
cannot answer.

1. A manufacturer or dealer quote generated by the manufacturer's order system. Priced per mark with
   a net unit price and an extended price, plus per-mark performance, egress and glazing data, and a
   packaged weight. Precise about product, silent about site.
2. An importer or distributor order confirmation, often for an overseas factory. Priced per position
   with unit price and extended total, with performance in metric and occasionally with untranslated
   source-language fields left in the technical data. Carries the hardest commercial terms in the
   trade. Sometimes the document is the bidder's own upstream supplier confirmation, forwarded
   whole, which means the estimator is looking at the bidder's cost basis rather than at a proposal.
3. A contractor-written proposal letter. Priced by scope family or by opening type with counts, not
   by mark. This is the genre that shows up on renovation work and whenever installation is in the
   package, and it is the only genre that reliably talks about site conditions.

Common to all three:

- Multi-configuration menus. A supplier will put six to nine complete priced configurations in one
  document, each a different glazing or finish package, each with its own subtotal, tax line and
  total, with none of them labelled as the bid. Which number enters the leveling sheet is the
  reader's choice, and the internal spread across configurations can run ten to fifteen percent. If
  you record a supplier's price without recording which configuration it came from, you have
  recorded nothing.
- Supply only is the default posture. Across the sample, essentially every window bidder on new
  construction is a supplier, and the estimator records the labor type as supplier for all of them.
  Installation is a separate package. This is the single most important structural fact about the
  trade and section 5 is mostly about its consequences.
- Sales tax broken out as its own line. Because this is a material purchase rather than a
  construction service in most of these packages, tax is a first-class line and bidders disagree
  about whether to carry it. A proposal that omits tax is not cheaper by that amount.
- Freight, delivery mode and customs duty as priced lines on imported systems. See section 7.
- Email as the real medium. A proposal here is frequently not one file. It is an email thread with
  the manufacturer's estimate attached, plus product literature, plus a scope sentence in the body
  that appears nowhere in the attachment. Some of the most decisive scope statements in this trade
  live only in a forwarding email.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Counts by mark, with a total. Nearly always.
- A unit price per mark. Commonly in the dealer and distributor genres, rarely in the letter genre.
- Glazing buildup, lite by lite, with coating and coating surface, gap fill and spacer.
- Assembly performance values, and per-mark egress clear opening where the manufacturer's system
  produces it.
- Finish and color, interior and exterior separately.
- Warranty, broken out by component with different terms for product, glass, coating, hardware and
  screens.
- Lead time, in production weeks plus shipping weeks.
- The document it priced from, usually a named and dated drawing set.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Installation. Excluded by nearly every supplier, usually in a single clause.
- Field measurement. Nobody offers it and nobody says who does it.
- Perimeter flashing, sill pan, air barrier tie-in, sealant, shims and fasteners. Excluded almost
  universally, and see section 5 for where they land.
- Who offloads the truck. See section 5.
- Whether the units meet egress. See below.
- Whether the units satisfy the local building department.
- Sequencing constraints, other than as a warranty condition buried in the terms.

Exclusion behavior differs from a labor trade in a way that changes how you read it. Supply-trade
exclusion lists are long, printed, and largely house terms. A supplier's list will exclude curtain
wall installation, revolving doors, automatic entrances and card readers on a punched-window
project, because the list is written once for every product the company sells. Reading that list for
project information is a waste. What matters in a supply proposal is the deviation paragraph, and
section 4 opens with it for that reason.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs and near-pairs:

- Escalation re-issue, with the scope byte-identical. A quote re-issued days later at a materially
  higher unit price on every product line, with accessory lines held exactly flat, is the
  characteristic revision of an imported system in a moving tariff and freight environment. A
  double-digit move in under a week is not unusual. Nothing about the scope changed and the second
  document is not a correction of the first.
- Product package substitution. The same bidder issues two complete quotes on the same day against
  the same takeoff, differing only in the profile system, with a large price gap between them. These
  are two coexisting offers, not a revision pair, and an estimator will rank them as two separate
  entries. One bidder can occupy two slots in a ranking, and on one project three bidders did.
- Scope departure into alternates. The base falls sharply because scope families left the base and
  reappeared as voluntary add alternates. A price drop that is not a price drop. Worse, the
  reinstated alternate is often not the same scope that left: the mark list changes, the count
  changes, and the price comes back higher.
- Product downgrade presented as a price concession. A revision that holds the headline count and
  drops the price while substituting a stock profile for a custom one, or dropping a sealant and a
  flashing bullet from a section's inclusion list. Comparing totals without reading the bullets
  credits the bidder with a saving it did not give.
- Breakout restructure. Counts and families move between rounds while the total barely does. The
  restructure moves no money and is still the information, because it tells you the bidder's
  understanding of the scope changed.

Two consequences for a reader. First, a revision in this trade is frequently a commercial event
rather than a document-change event, so do not assume a later round reflects an addendum. Second,
revision labels are close to useless. A filename date, the date printed on the proposal, the
document's internal revision marking and the referenced set date routinely all disagree, revision
sequences skip numbers, and two versions of one quote get filed under two filenames that read as two
different scopes.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Window scope sheets have run roughly seventy to two hundred and sixty rows, and the row count tracks
how much of the surrounding scope the sheet tries to hold rather than how many windows there are.
The largest sheet in the sample belongs to a project with about a thousand units and spends most of
its rows on glazing composition and commercial terms, not on marks. Target a sheet that carries a
row per mark plus a full commercial block plus a full seam block, and expect the non-mark rows to
outnumber the mark rows.

### The mark is the atom, but the mark is not one thing

This is the discriminating question in the trade, and getting it wrong makes every bid
incomparable. A window tag on a schedule has a base type, and then it has qualifiers: handing,
tempering, fixed versus operable, and the frame or jamb condition it sits in. Bidders subdivide the
base tag along different axes and the estimator subdivides it along yet another.

Observed in the sample, all against the same schedules: one supplier splitting a base tag by handing
and by variant digit, another splitting the same base tags by tempering and by fixed versus
operable, a third splitting by the cladding condition at the jamb, and an estimator splitting by
tempering and by the brick-extension condition. None of them are wrong and none of their line counts
match. A mark row that says only the base tag will collect four different answers.

So enumerate at the full tag. Give each of these its own line:

- Every mark on the window schedule, in the schedule's own vocabulary. Tag vocabularies are
  architect-specific discovered vocabulary and never a fixed prefix set. Read the schedule for the
  tags rather than assuming a scheme.
- Each handing variant of a mark, where the mark occurs in both hands. Suppliers price handing
  separately as a matter of course and a merged row loses the split.
- Each tempered variant. Tempering is a per-position price difference and appears as a distinct
  priced line in every genre that prices by mark.
- Each fixed versus operable variant of the same tag.
- Each frame condition variant, meaning the same window at brick, at thin brick, at fiber cement,
  and at metal panel. The unit is the same and the extension, panning and trim are not, and the
  details are drawn per condition.
- Composite units, meaning a door with an integrated window above or beside it. These are priced as
  their own tag by the market and are one of the recurring sources of count disagreement, because
  one bidder counts the assembly and another counts the door and the window separately.
- Balcony and terrace doors as their own family with their own tag block.
- Storefront and any transom or infill glazing at a window opening, as its own family.
- Interior storm windows on renovation work, as their own family.
- Mockup units. Suppliers routinely mint a mockup tag because no scheduled window matches the
  mockup dimensions shown, and the mockup is a separate purchase with its own credit terms.

### Name your denominator

There are at least three counts of the same set and they are all correct:

1. Positions or types, meaning distinct tags. Runs in the dozens to low hundreds.
2. Units, meaning individual window or door assemblies. Runs in the hundreds to low thousands.
3. Elements or lites, meaning the glazed panels inside those assemblies. Always the largest, and can
   exceed the unit count by twenty percent or more.

A supplier quoting a thousand and a supplier quoting seventeen hundred against the same schedule may
be quoting the identical scope at different denominators. Every count claim in a scope line has to
say which one it is. On top of that, a proposal may state a unit count in one place and a per-mark
table that sums to a different number in another, so sum the table.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Color, finish, hardware type, hardware color, screen type and glazing buildup within one mark.
  These are attributes of the mark, not lines. They vary by area type, which is already an
  enumeration axis.
- Individual sashes, vents and lites within an assembly, for scoping purposes. Track the element
  count as a stated quantity on the mark row, not as rows.
- Levels and floors. No document in this trade prices by level. Windows repeat vertically and the
  schedule is the organizing document, not the floor plan.
- Individual glazing layers as scope rows. Carry the glazing buildup as a compliance block against
  the specification, which is what an estimator does, rather than as scope lines.

### Counting basis is a scope line of its own

Bidders count from different documents and they say so only sometimes. In the sample, one supplier
counted from the floor plans and scaled its dimensions manually off the schedule, another counted
from the elevations and dropped every scheduled type it could not find in an elevation, and the
estimator's own count sat between them. On one project the schedule and the elevations use different
tags for the same two positions, and the estimator's sheet carries dual-tag rows to hold both.

That is the mechanism behind almost every count disagreement in this trade. It is not carelessness.
It is that the schedule, the elevations and the floor plans do not agree, and each bidder resolves
the disagreement silently. Any scope line set should carry an explicit row asking which document the
count came from, and a row for each known schedule-to-elevation discrepancy.

### The structural gap list

The estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus adjustments, where adjustments
are plugs added to a bidder's number to cover what the market systematically will not carry. Across
projects the recurring plugs in this trade are:

- Installation labor, whenever the bidder is a supplier, which is nearly always on new construction.
- Sales tax, where the bidder excluded or omitted it.
- Customs duty and import taxes, where the bidder was silent or ambiguous.
- Freight and delivery, where not stated.
- Perimeter frame extensions and brick extensions at the masonry condition.
- Prefinished metal sill enclosure or sill pan.
- Perimeter metal flashing at head and sill.
- Exterior trim and brickmould, furnish and install.
- Nailing flange, where the bidder quoted a flangeless system against details drawn for a flange, or
  the reverse.
- Mockup units and the mockup opening.
- Shop drawings beyond fabrication drawings, and a licensed engineer's stamp.
- Field water testing and third party testing.
- Glazing upgrades at the specific positions that failed the specified performance.
- Balcony doors, where the solicitation did not carry them. On one project the identical balcony door
  plug was applied to every single bidder, which is the clearest possible signal that the family was
  missing from the solicitation rather than from any one bid.
- Caulking, at every interface.

Those items are, in effect, the trade's structural gap list, and a scope line set that names all of
them explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging for it. The two most expensive are installation and
the perimeter package, and neither is a window.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In this trade
the test fires hardest on the perimeter and the logistics, not on the product. Whether a window is
tempered is answered by the schedule. Whether anyone flashes it, hoists it, or pays duty on it is
answered by nobody unless you ask.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. Supply-only proposals are silent on installation
by construction, so silence there means nothing. Silence on tariff, on delivery mode, on who
offloads, or on frame extensions is a real scope gap, because those are items some bidders price and
others omit.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### Start with the deviation paragraph

In a supply trade the highest-value paragraph in a proposal is the one where the bidder discloses
where its product does not match the drawings. It is neither boilerplate nor an exclusion, it is
rarely flagged, and it is the paragraph most likely to survive into a change order. The deviations
seen recur enough to look for by name:

- The units may not meet egress requirements unless specifically requested in writing. Stated
  plainly by more than one supplier, and it means the supplier sized to the schedule without
  checking the clear opening against code. This is the most consequential disclosure in the trade
  and it is written as a routine terms bullet.
- Handle heights may not comply with accessibility requirements unless specifically requested.
- Tempered glazing will be used everywhere unless otherwise specified, which is a supplier defaulting
  to a safe product rather than reading where the code and the schedule actually require it.
- The specification was not reviewed, and the quote may deviate from the bid documents. Written on
  the face of a proposal that then prices the whole schedule.
- Named marks annotated as not fire rated, on a project where the schedule calls some of them rated.
- Maximum available fixed and operable dimensions to hold a given performance grade, which silently
  caps what the schedule can be built to. Any scheduled unit larger than the cap needs a mullion or a
  transom that is not on the elevation.
- Threshold and sill conditions available only if recessed into the slab, which is a structural
  condition disclosed as a product note.
- The bidder's own tag for a position differing from the schedule's tag, stated as an aside.
- Performance quoted in a different measurement system and standard than the specification uses, so
  that compliance cannot be read off the face of the document.
- Details not found in the construction documents, carried on an assumption the bidder names.
- The product is non-impact rated.
- No responsibility taken for conformance to local, state or federal building codes, and no
  responsibility that the local building department will accept the product. Two different suppliers
  push code acceptance back onto the buyer in the same words.

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the trade that must pick the work up, and
if no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| Excluded by the window bidder | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Installation of the units (nearly every supplier on new construction) | A separate installation package, commonly the siding or trim carpenter |
| Offloading, hoisting and site handling (nearly every supplier) | General contractor or the installer, and frequently unassigned |
| Field measurement (every supplier seen) | Nobody, until it becomes an installer problem |
| Installation hardware: screws, shims, anchors, perimeter sealant (most suppliers) | Installer, and a real material cost |
| Perimeter caulking and interior caulking (most suppliers) | Installer or a caulking package, commonly unassigned |
| Sill flashing, counterflashing, sill pan, brake metal (most suppliers) | Waterproofing, siding, or unassigned, and a recurring real gap |
| Air and vapor barrier at the opening, and its tie-in (most) | Siding or envelope package |
| Blocking at the opening (most) | Framing or carpentry, and on one renovation none was shown on the drawings at all |
| Frame extensions, trims, panning, brickmould (about half) | Unassigned, and a recurring plug |
| Detailed shop drawings beyond fabrication drawings (about half) | Charged extra by the same bidder, or unassigned |
| Structural calculations, engineer's stamp beyond standard certification (about half) | Charged extra, or an engineer of record question |
| Mockup units and mockup testing (most) | General contractor, sometimes with a supplier credit against the unit cost |
| Third party field testing (most; some will witness only) | Owner's testing agent |
| Field testing at full laboratory pressure (commonly, with a reduced field pressure named instead) | A specification conflict to resolve before award |
| Demolition of existing windows on renovation (varies widely, see section 5) | Demolition package, or an alternate, or unassigned |
| Temporary protection at open holes (commonly) | General contractor, or nobody where the bidder assumes same-day replacement |
| Staging, swing stage, lifts, sidewalk shed, overhead protection (most on renovation) | General contractor |
| Police details, permits, parking (most on renovation) | General contractor |
| Abatement and hazardous material (every renovation bidder seen) | Abatement contractor |
| Final cleaning and cleaning of glass (most) | General contractor or cleaning package |
| Louvers, skylights, window washing anchors and bolts (every supplier seen) | Specialty suppliers, frequently unassigned |
| Curtain wall, storefront installation, all-glass systems (most suppliers, as printed house terms) | Glazing package |
| Interior glazing, mirrors, glass guardrails, rolling shades (most) | Glazing and specialties packages |
| Modification of the manufacturer's warranty terms (some) | Nobody; the specification's warranty requirement may simply not be obtainable |
| Removal and reinstallation of window treatments on occupied renovation (some) | Owner or general contractor |
| Interior finish repairs after installation (some renovation bidders) | Drywall, plaster and paint, and a real cost on replacement work |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate. A supplier reproduces the same exclusion list on every
project regardless of product, which is why punched-window quotes carry exclusions for revolving
doors and card readers. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- A named mark excluded, or a named mark carried with a stated deviation. When a bidder writes out
  specific tags, it read the schedule and found something.
- A count reconciled in writing against a second document. A bidder that writes out which scheduled
  types it could not find in the elevations, and which elevation positions are missing from the
  schedule, has done the work everyone else did silently. Treat that paragraph as a drawing-set
  defect report and act on it.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. A bidder pricing blocking as an alternate on
  the stated ground that none is shown is telling you the detail is incomplete, not that it is
  expensive.
- A minted tag. A bidder that adds a mockup tag because nothing on the schedule matches the mockup
  dimensions, or carries positions labelled unknown, is reporting a schedule gap.
- Any exclusion naming a project-specific condition: a historic approval, a stretch energy code, a
  phasing requirement, an occupied floor, a specific elevation excluded from the scope.

### Contract terms that are really scope

Supply proposals carry commercial terms that change what the number is worth, and they sit in the
terms block where a scope reader will skip them. Each of these was seen and each materially changes
the risk position:

- A deposit at order, running as high as three quarters of the contract value before anything ships,
  with the balance due before delivery.
- Custom fabrication declared non-returnable and non-refundable, with any post-order change treated
  as a separate order at additional cost.
- Binding arbitration with a class action waiver.
- Consequential and liquidated damages not accepted.
- Warranty disclaimed by the seller and passed through to an overseas manufacturer, so the entity you
  have a contract with warrants nothing.
- Price held only for a short window, with a stated escalation rate per quarter thereafter, or with
  price re-opened if approved shop drawings are not returned within a stated number of days.
- Currency conversion and exchange rate movement placed on the buyer.
- Delivery acceptance required within days of notification, with storage fees thereafter.
- A short claim window, measured in days from delivery or from unbundling, for damaged or
  nonconforming product. Once it closes, damage is the buyer's.
- Product acceptance transferring on receipt, so that once the units are offloaded, installation and
  handling problems are no longer the supplier's.
- A per-technician hourly charge for any field adjustment traced to installation or operation.

A bidder with the softest terms and the highest number can be the cheaper buy. None of this is
visible on a leveling sheet that carries only base bid and plugs, which is why a windows scope sheet
should carry a commercial block as scope rows.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This section is where the trade actually lives, and on a windows package it should be read before
section 3 rather than after it.

### The supply and install seam

On new construction, the window bidder supplies and does not install. That is not a preference, it
is the shape of the market: across the sample every bidder on every new-construction windows package
was recorded by the estimator as a supplier. Installation is bought separately, and in the corpus the
installer is the siding and trim carpenter, whose proposal carries a single line reading install only
windows inside a package of weather barrier, strapping, cementitious siding, trim and flashing.

That single arrangement produces most of the trade's real risk:

- The install labor is invisible in the windows package and can only be found in another division's
  proposal. If you scope windows without scoping the install, the leveling sheet is missing its
  largest single adjustment. If you scope it and the carpentry package also carries it, you have
  bought it twice.
- The installer's number cannot be separated from its siding number without asking, because the
  window install is one line in a bundled labor and material scope.
- The seam between the unit and the wall belongs to the installer, not the supplier. The weather
  barrier, the flashing and counterflashing, the fasteners and the installation caulk were all
  carried by the carpenter in the sample and excluded by every supplier. If the installation package
  is written as install windows and nothing more, the entire water management detail at every
  opening is unassigned.
- Unloading, hoisting and conveying was carried by the installer. Every supplier excluded it. On
  renovation, several bidders assumed a material hoist provided by others and carried no equipment
  at all, and one qualified its whole bid on the units fitting through the building's elevators.

On renovation and replacement work the picture inverts. There the window bidder does furnish and
install, the estimator tracks union versus open shop as a live comparison field, and the labor market
becomes a real price driver in a way it never is on a supply-only package.

### Receiving, delivery mode and staging

Treat these as scope lines, because bidders price them and the drawings never show them.

- Delivery modes seen: containerized from a port with a stated container count, flatbed, tailgate,
  and staged release from a local warehouse. They are not equivalent. A tailgate delivery means the
  product comes off the back of the vehicle and no further, and the seller states plainly that the
  buyer must have people and equipment there to move it.
- Container count is recalculated when the shipping sequence is issued, and exceeding the estimate
  incurs additional charges. The quoted freight is an estimate against an assumed packing plan.
- Weight is real. Imported systems in the sample ran to six figures of pounds for a package, with the
  seller warning that the units are considerably heavier than domestic equivalents and that suction
  cups and moving equipment are required. Published packaged weights per assembly and per position
  are common and should be captured, because they drive the hoisting method.
- Storage is a differentiator and a cost. One supplier offered a local warehouse and staged release
  to the site as its main value proposition, explicitly contrasting it with competitors that require
  the site to take the whole order at once. Others charge a monthly rate per square foot plus a fee
  per delivery. Others assess storage fees if the buyer does not accept delivery within days. A
  project without lay-down space and a project with one are buying different things.

### Mulling, factory versus field

Whether multi-unit assemblies arrive factory mulled or need field mulling is a genuine scope
difference between suppliers, is never shown on a drawing, and is worth real money in labor and in
water risk. One distributor named it as the discriminating difference against its main competitor.
Where units are field mulled, the mull weatherproofing becomes an installer scope line that exists
in no proposal.

### The perimeter package

The most reliably unassigned scope in this trade. It comprises:

- Frame extensions and jamb extensions, which vary by cladding condition and are drawn per condition
  on the typical detail sheets.
- Brick extensions at masonry, sometimes factory installed and sometimes not, and the estimator
  tracks which.
- Panning, head and sill trim, and snap trim on replacement work, where matching an existing profile
  and accepting a stock profile are different products at different prices.
- Brickmould, furnished and installed as two separate questions.
- Prefinished metal sill enclosure and sill pan flashing.
- Perimeter metal flashing at head and sill, which one supplier priced as a separate add-on package
  rather than carrying.
- Interior and exterior perimeter sealant, routinely split so that a bidder carries one and excludes
  the other.
- Low expanding foam at the perimeter, and insulation at the opening.

Every one of these appeared as a plug or an unanswered row on at least one estimator sheet. Give each
its own line.

### The storefront and glazing boundary

Contested in both directions and worth fine-grained boundaries.

- Window suppliers price storefront families inside windows quotes, at punched openings, at transoms
  over entrances, and as infill in louver openings, while their own boilerplate excludes storefront.
- Glazing contractors bid the windows package and bring mirrors, fire rated door lites, smoke baffle
  glass, handrail glass, storefront doors and door hardware with them.
- On renovation, the same document can carry a storefront section and an aluminum storefront
  exclusion, which is not a contradiction if the storefront section is a punched infill and the
  exclusion is the entrance system, but nothing in the document says so.
- Beware of the same item being claimed by two trades. Storefront square footage levelled in the
  windows package and again in the glazing package is a real double count, and estimator sheets cross
  reference between tabs precisely because of it.

### Renovation and replacement seams

On existing buildings the package acquires an operation sequence that new construction does not
have, and estimators write the scope as an ordered operation list rather than as a product list:
demolish the existing window, install new blocking, wrap the rough opening, set the metal sill pan
flashing, install the new window, install brickmould, caulk exterior, caulk interior, foam the
perimeter, provide exterior access, provide overhead and sidewalk protection, and carry police
details. On a replacement job the operations, not the units, are what bidders answer differently.

Specific seams that only exist here:

- Demolition of the existing window. Carried in the base by one bidder and offered as an alternate at
  a substantial add by another on the same project. Where it is an alternate, ask where the debris
  goes: one bidder's alternate turns the debris over to the general contractor in a centralized
  location on the floor rather than removing it from the building, which leaves a disposal scope
  behind.
- Temporary protection at the open hole. Commonly excluded on the stated assumption that removal and
  installation happen the same day. That is a schedule commitment disguised as an exclusion, and if
  the schedule does not hold, the exclusion becomes a change.
- Fire rated blocking at each opening, where none is shown on the architectural drawings.
- Historic approval and the submittals that go with it, and any energy code variance the historic
  status requires.
- Interior finish repair after installation, and caulking of existing windows that remain.
- Interior storm windows, which are a distinct product with a distinct supplier, priced per opening
  and per panel, and whose installation is commonly plugged by the estimator on an hourly basis
  rather than bid.

### Other adjacent trades

- Framing and blocking, for the rough opening dimension and its tolerance. Suppliers state rough
  opening sizes and disclaim everything about the opening they are given.
- Waterproofing and the air barrier, for the tie-in at head, jamb and sill. Drawn on the typical
  detail sheets and carried by neither the supplier nor, unless asked, the installer.
- Siding and cladding, which owns the exterior condition at the jamb and which is why the window
  detail sheets are organized by cladding type.
- Prefabricated wall panels, where used. One manufacturer's terms require the panels to be erected
  before the window units are installed into the envelope, which is a sequencing constraint hidden in
  a warranty condition.
- The testing agent. Field water testing at a reduced fraction of the laboratory pressure is a
  standard supplier qualification, and where the specification calls for full pressure that conflict
  should be resolved before award rather than at the first failed test.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What window bidders actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The window schedule. The primary document, and the source of the tags. Bidders scale dimensions
   off it, and at least one supplier stated that it scaled every dimension manually with a rough
   opening allowance and asked for labelled plans, elevations and the schedule together in order to
   produce a precise takeoff.
2. The elevations. The count document for some bidders, and the source of the mark occurrences. Where
   a scheduled type does not appear in an elevation, some bidders drop it and say so, and some drop
   it silently.
3. The floor plans. The count document for other bidders. A supplier that counts from plans and one
   that counts from elevations will not agree.
4. The typical window detail sheets, organized by cladding condition, which drive the extension,
   panning and installation method.
5. The specification, for performance targets, acceptable manufacturers, warranty duration and
   glazing composition. Cited more often than quoted.
6. The exterior finish schedule, for color by area type.

The unit and area totals are the leveling currency. Every estimator sheet in the sample normalized
every bid to a cost per square foot of glazed window and door area and ranked on it, not to a cost
per unit and not to a cost per opening. If the set does not make the glazed area and the mark counts
unambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Whether installation is in this package at all. On new construction it usually is not.
- Who installs, and whether that scope includes the flashing and air barrier tie-in.
- Country of origin. Domestic, and various European sources, all appear against the same schedule,
  and origin drives duty, freight, lead time and warranty enforceability all at once.
- Customs duty exposure. A first-class priced line on imported systems, sometimes a flat allowance
  identical across two product packages of very different value, sometimes stated as a percentage
  adder, sometimes partially absorbed by the seller with the absorbed portion undisclosed, and
  sometimes simply absent with no way to tell whether the bidder omitted it or is domestic.
- Freight mode, container count and whether the site can receive a container.
- Lead time, which ranged from six to eight weeks for a domestic supplier with local stock to
  eighteen to twenty weeks of production plus about four weeks of ocean freight for an overseas
  system, before any shop drawing approval time.
- Whether the price is even the price. One bidder offered an unquantified volume discount available
  only once a conversation started, which makes the quoted number a list price.
- Deposit terms and the cash flow they impose.
- Whether the local building department will accept the product, which more than one supplier
  explicitly pushes back onto the buyer.
- Whether the units meet egress, which more than one supplier explicitly declines to warrant.
- Whether the specified warranty duration is obtainable at all. Suppliers commonly offer half the
  specified product warranty and decline to modify their terms.
- Union versus open shop, on any project where installation is in the package.
- Prevailing wage applicability.
- Site access, lay-down space, hoisting method and elevator dimensions.
- Whether a mockup is expected, and whether the mockup opening is a scheduled opening.
- Whether the schedule and the elevations agree.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, proposals price a stated glazing package and carry the
alternative as an add, because the specification and the basis of design frequently disagree about
double versus triple glazing at that stage. Expect a glazing alternate on every design-development
bid and treat the base as provisional. By construction documents and conformed sets, the same
bidders switch to citing mark ranges and per-mark counts, and the arguments move from product class
to counts and to commercial terms. Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its folder's set
stage routinely disagree: bids priced off an earlier permit set arrive in a construction-document
round, and email threads about one set get filed against another.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, per mark. The priced unit in every quote that prices by mark.
- Positions or types, units, and elements. Three simultaneous denominators, published side by side by
  some suppliers, and the source of most apparent count disagreement.
- Square feet of glazed window and door area. The leveling currency, and the basis for every ranking
  in the sample.
- Square metres, on imported systems, alongside metric thermal and acoustic values that do not map
  directly onto the specification's units.
- Pounds and kilograms, for profile weight, glazing weight and total package weight. Published by
  suppliers and load-bearing for hoisting and receiving.
- Linear feet, for perimeter, for exterior trim and brickmould, and for perimeter sealant.
- Openings, on renovation work, where a single opening may contain a multi-panel assembly.
- Panels, within an opening, on storm windows.
- Hours, for installation labor on renovation, where an estimator plugs at a stated hourly rate. One
  sheet carried sixteen hours per opening for interior storm window installation.
- Containers and pallets, as a freight denominator.
- Percent of contract value, for sales tax, and occasionally for a duty adder.
- Mounting brackets and anchors, counted in the tens of thousands on large packages and worth
  capturing because they are an installer material.

Cost drivers, with the commercial shape ranked ahead of the product variables, because in this trade
the commercial shape moves the comparison more than the glass does:

1. Whether the number includes installation. The largest single swing, and the one that makes an
   unadjusted ranking meaningless.
2. Country of origin and the duty, freight and lead time that follow from it.
3. Frame system and product class. The widest spread in the sample, roughly four to one across a
   single project's bid list, was driven almost entirely by product class rather than by
   competitiveness, spanning an imported plastic system and a premium domestic fiberglass line quoted
   against the same schedule.
4. Glazing package. Double versus triple, the number of low-emissivity coatings and which surfaces
   they sit on, the gap fill and the spacer. A triple-glazing add on a design-development bid can run
   a quarter of the base.
5. Tempering extent, which is a per-position adder and which some suppliers apply universally by
   default.
6. The perimeter package: extensions, panning, trim, flashing, sill enclosure.
7. Delivery mode, storage and staged release.
8. Escalation exposure, meaning how long the price is held and at what rate it moves after. A
   re-issued quote in the sample rose by a sixth in six days on identical scope.
9. Labor market, on any package where installation is included.
10. Shop drawings, engineering stamps and testing, each of which is charged extra by roughly half the
    market.
11. Mockup.
12. Warranty term and whether the manufacturer will modify it.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per square foot ran about 1.4 to 1, about 1.5 to 1, about 2.75 to 1, and
about 4 to 1 across the four projects. The tight spreads occur where every bidder is quoting the same
product class as a supplier. The wide ones occur where the bid list mixes product classes, or mixes
supply-only bidders with furnish-and-install bidders, or mixes window suppliers with glazing
contractors carrying extra scope. Bidder counts per project run from about eight to nine, but ranked
entries run higher than bidder counts, because a bidder that offers two product packages occupies two
ranking slots and an estimator ranks the offers rather than the companies. A ranking row in this
trade is a product offer, not a company, and reading it as a company overstates how deep the bid list
really is.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly two dozen window-package proposals plus a storm window supplier
quote and an installation-only carpentry proposal, together with four estimator leveling sheets,
drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general
contractor. The proposals span design development, permit, construction documents and conformed
sets, and include several same-bidder revision pairs, two same-day product-package pairs, and one
project bid across three successive rounds by the same supplier. Several bidders appear on more than
one project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4
testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work in one market through one general
  contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial curtain wall projects, institutional, healthcare,
  or public prevailing-wage work.
- The new-construction picture rests entirely on supply-only bidding. Only the renovation project
  carried furnish-and-install window bidders, so every claim about installation pricing, labor market
  and the install operation sequence rests on that one project.
- Only one installation proposal is in the corpus, and it is a bundled siding and trim package with
  window install as a single line. Nothing here shows what a standalone window installation bid looks
  like or what it costs per opening.
- The tariff and freight environment is a snapshot. The escalation and duty behavior described in
  sections 2 and 7 was observed during a period of active tariff movement, and the magnitudes should
  be treated as evidence of volatility rather than as rates.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, whether the deposit terms held, or which of the plugs were actually bought.
- No change order history and no field record is in the corpus. The claims that the egress
  disclaimer, the same-day-replacement assumption and the reduced field test pressure convert into
  changes are read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed, so treat them as
  untested here.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the windows and the curtain wall were bid as one package,
  so the boundary in section 1 between this trade and curtain wall is asserted rather than observed.
- The corpus has not shown aluminum-clad wood or steel window systems in any quantity, nor a project
  where impact rating was required, so the product-class spread in section 7 covers plastic,
  fiberglass and thermal aluminum only.
- The corpus has not shown how the package is organized when a single supplier both furnishes and
  installs on new construction, which is the arrangement much of the market outside this general
  contractor's practice uses.
