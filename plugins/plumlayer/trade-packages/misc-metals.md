# Miscellaneous Metals

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the miscellaneous
metals package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling
sheets. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the
words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. Misc metals is the package that has no natural edge. Almost
every other trade defines itself by a material and a location; this one is defined by what is left
over after the structural steel package, the aluminum package and the specialty suppliers have taken
what they want. The scope list is the boundary, and if the scope list does not draw it, nobody does.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The anchor is metal fabrications, section 055000, with metal stairs and railings at 055100 and
055200 close behind. An estimator's package is usually titled metal fabrications and keyed to
055000, and it routinely swallows work specified in half a dozen other sections. Bidders cite
section numbers unevenly: some list five sections across the structural, deck and fabrication range
in a header line, most cite none and quote sheet numbers instead. Sheet references are the reliable
identifier in this trade, not spec sections.

The package as bid is not one package. It splits along a fabrication axis that has nothing to do
with the drawings:

- The steel fabricator's package: stairs, rails, ladders, grating, clips, lintels, relieving angles,
  embeds, elevator metals, dunnage, hatch frames, bollards. Shop-fabricated black steel, primed or
  galvanized, delivered and erected by an iron worker.
- The aluminum package: guardrails, balcony rails, prefabricated balconies, fencing, gates, screen
  walls. Extruded or welded aluminum, powder coated, from a manufacturer with a catalog. Steel
  fabricators exclude this block wholesale and by name.
- The specialty supply packages: roof screens, ballasted non-penetrating roof guardrails, louvers,
  roof anchors, proprietary railing systems, masonry support brackets. Each is a manufacturer
  selling a configured product, not a fabricator selling scope.

An estimator may run these as one leveling tab or as three. On one project the same division carried
three separate tabs, one for metal fabrications, one for ballasted roof railings and one for
balconies and railings, each normalized on a different denominator. Whether the split is made is a
project decision, not a market fact, and getting it wrong makes the whole comparison meaningless.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another spec section:

- Structural steel on a renovation. When the structural work is reinforcement of an existing frame
  rather than a new frame, fabricators bid both halves in one number, in two labelled sections. On
  such a project the estimator's sheet carries the annotation "in misc or structural?" against a
  dozen rows, and different bidders answer differently. On new construction with a real steel frame,
  the split holds and the two packages stay apart.
- Relieving angles, shelf angles, loose lintels and masonry support brackets. Always priced in this
  package, almost never installed by it.
- Embed plates, embed angles, leveling plates, anchor bolts and shear wall plates. Always furnished
  here.
- Site and landscape metal: bollards, tree guards at tree pits, catenary light posts, fencing,
  gates, site railings at exterior stairs, bike racks. Some bidders carry it, more exclude it, and
  on one project a landscape-drawing tree guard was priced by the metals bidder at a figure
  comparable to the entire stair package.
- Roof anchors and fall protection. Excluded by nearly every fabricator and routed to a specialties
  division where anchor manufacturers bid it directly. One bidder carried it inside the base, and
  the estimator flagged it by annotating the bidder's name on the leveling sheet.
- Roof screens and mechanical screens. Bid by proprietary manufacturers, filed in this division, and
  split on the leveling sheet into a frame line and a cladding line with a note to check the cladding
  against the siding package.
- Louvers. Bid by louver manufacturers against the architect's louver schedule, materials only. The
  support steel, installation angles, clips and fasteners the louver excludes land here.
- Generator and equipment dunnage. Excluded by most, carried as an allowance by some, and plugged by
  the estimator on nearly every project.
- Glass guardrails and glass-supported railings. Genuinely contested. On one row four bidders gave
  four different answers: not carried, not applicable, carried complete including the glass, and
  routed to the door and glass division.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like misc metals:

- Everything aluminum. Steel fabricators exclude decorative railings, glass railings, aluminum rails,
  prefabricated balconies and tie rods, balcony rails, roof rails and terrace rails as a single
  named block.
- Ornamental fencing and gates, arbors, trellises and pergolas.
- Storefront, curtain wall and glazing support steel.
- Light gauge framing, cold-formed platforms and unistrut or slotted channel.
- Countertop supports and brackets, cabinet supports, toilet partition supports and any misc metal
  associated with millwork.
- Abrasive stair nosings, treads and thresholds, which are specified with the stair but bought with
  the flooring or the specialties.
- Corner guards, edgings, dock edge angles and vehicle barriers.
- Expansion joint covers and frames.

## 2. How the market bids

There is no default proposal shape in this trade. The same package on the same project draws
documents that have nothing structurally in common, and the shape a bidder chooses tells you what
kind of company it is.

The shapes actually seen:

- The enumerated take-off. Every scope item gets a bidder-minted mark, its own drawing references, a
  quantity with a unit, a finish, and separate supply and install columns. The deepest of these ran
  to roughly forty marks with landing areas, member sizes and per-line assumptions beneath each. A
  document of this kind is a take-off presented as a proposal, and it is the single most valuable
  document a general contractor receives on this trade.
- The priced element table. Sheet references, description, finish, quantity, price, grouped under
  headings for stairs, elevators, railings, miscellaneous and structural. Common among mid-size
  fabricators.
- The lump sum with a numbered bullet list. Eight to twelve bullets, each naming the element, its
  finish standard and the sheets it comes from, no quantities, one number. Common among union
  erectors and the larger fabricators.
- The furnish-and-install list paired with a furnish-only list. Two headed lists, and the split
  between them is the document's primary organizing axis.
- The mode-coded list. Every scope block carries a suffix declaring what the bidder does to it:
  fabricate and supply, fabricate prime and install, fabricate prime and supply, fabricate galvanize
  and install, fabricate galvanize and supply, fabricate color-galvanize and install. The legend is
  printed in the qualifications. This is the trade's own native notation for the supply and install
  seam, and where it appears there is no ambiguity left.
- The product configuration table. Deck number, deck size, railing type, quantity, hanger type,
  mounting type, unit price; or rail number, height, rail type, linear feet, location, attachment
  method, plan sheet. The aluminum and balcony suppliers all price this way.
- The product parameter sheet. Not a scope list at all: linear footage, frame type, frame span, panel
  gauge and finish, trim types, degrees of tilt back, wind speed, wind exposure, occupancy category,
  design code, insulation depth, gate count. Roof screen and similar specialty manufacturers quote
  this way, and every commitment they make is a parameter value rather than a sentence.
- The answered scope sheet. The general contractor's own sheet returned with a yes, no or dollar
  column and a comments column. Where the sheet was issued, bidders answered it, and the answered
  sheets are the richest documents in the corpus.
- The email. Five to eight sentences naming stairs by number and what rails each carries, one number,
  no exclusions.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The finish per item, always. Mill, shop primer, hot-dip galvanized, color galvanized, powder
  coated, polished. Finish is enumerated at a grain no other attribute reaches, because it drives
  both cost and who touches the item next.
- The basis set with a date, and usually the individual sheet numbers, which is the sharpest
  citation habit of any trade seen.
- Labor type, union or open shop. An estimator records it for every bidder regardless.
- Whether shop drawings and delegated design calculations are included, though often as separately
  priced lines rather than as an inclusion.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- The riser count. Even bidders who price per riser rarely put the count on the page, and where two
  bidders both stated theirs on one project they differed by more than half on one stair.
- Weight or tonnage. Seen only where a structural package is folded in, or where an aluminum
  manufacturer states total material pounds.
- Lead times, from steel fabricators. Manufacturers of proprietary systems state them precisely;
  fabricators almost never do.
- Field measuring responsibility, unless it is a priced line.
- Which of two conflicting details it priced, except in the enumerated take-offs, where saying so is
  routine.

Exclusion behavior is where this trade differs most from every other. Exclusion lists run long, from
roughly twenty lines to nearly sixty, and one bidder's list ran thirty-five numbered scope-specific
exclusions each carrying the sheet or detail it refers to. In this trade the exclusion list is not
boilerplate defense. It is a documents-gap report. The recurring reason given is not "not our trade"
but "no details", "none shown", "locations unclear", "insufficient information", "too many
conflicting details". Read the exclusion list of the most thorough bidder and you have the project's
misc metals request-for-information list, already written, sheet references included.

The corollary matters as much. A bidder with a short exclusion list has not read the set as closely,
and a bidder with no exclusion list at all has told you nothing about where its number stops.

Revision habits:

- Rounds are frequent and deep. Four rounds from one bidder over three months is normal on a
  complicated package, and a bidder may still be submitting a first round two months after the
  leveling sheet was built.
- The base moving is usually not a price change. Across one four-round ladder the base went up, up
  and then down, and only the last movement was a real price. The first two were excluded scope
  acquiring a price and moving into add alternates; the third was scope leaving the base and
  reappearing as an alternate after a descope call.
- Exclusion-to-alternate migration is the characteristic mechanism. Items leave the exclusion list
  only by acquiring a price, and each carries a pointer to the alternate that now covers it. Watching
  the exclusion list shrink is a better read of a negotiation than watching the number.
- The package itself can change between rounds. A bidder that quoted misc metals only at one round
  may quote structural plus misc at the next, at more than twice the number. Nothing on the face of
  the document warns you.
- Quantities get restated. Relieving angle footage and clip counts moved by a fifth and by two fifths
  between rounds on one project, tracking a set change.
- The tax line changes basis. A flat tax line became tax on fabricated material only across one
  ladder, and the tax fell while the base rose. Comparing tax-inclusive totals across rounds measures
  something other than price.
- The commercial comeback drives a whole round on its own. A general contractor issues an identical
  checklist to every bidder near the end: hold pricing to a date, confirm tariffs are included, carry
  a two-year warranty with a deduct for one year, list value engineering separately as alternates,
  give an insurance-program credit, give bond cost above a contract-value threshold, commit to
  environmental product declarations. The answers come back as a revision that moves little money and
  changes the terms materially.
- Product substitution rounds move no scope at all. A supplier issues a revision whose only content
  is a deduct for its second product line in place of its first, and the two versions then occupy two
  separate rows in the ranking.
- Two same-day offers from one supplier at different configurations are not a revision pair. Order
  rounds by the basis set cited, never by letterhead date; letterhead dates and covering email dates
  disagreed by six weeks on one document.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Misc metals scope sheets have run roughly forty-five, seventy-five, one hundred and two hundred and
forty rows. The floor is around seventy-five for a normal new-construction package. Below about
forty-five the sheet stops distinguishing anything: the thinnest sheet seen carried misc metals as a
single budgeted block at a rate per square foot, and its ranking put three railing-only suppliers
above the one bidder who priced the whole package.

The two hundred and forty row sheet was a renovation, where the structural reinforcement is itself
enumerated level by level and detail by detail. Expect a renovation sheet to run two to three times
a new-construction sheet on this trade.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every stair, individually, by number and by level range. Stairs never collapse. Each stair then
  breaks further into stringers, treads, risers and landings as one line, guardrail as a second,
  offset handrail at the guardrail as a third, wall mounted handrail as a fourth, and access gate as
  a fifth. That five-way split is how the most careful bidders present it and how the most careful
  estimator sheets ask for it, and it exists because a bidder can carry the stair and exclude the
  rail, or carry a wall rail on one side and not both.
- What each stair is built of. On a project where the stairs are wood, precast and cast in place, the
  metals scope is only the rails and the nosings, and the package is a completely different shape.
  A scope sheet that does not record the stair construction per stair invites bidders to price a
  metal pan stair that does not exist.
- Riser count per stair. It is the pricing unit and it is the quantity bidders most often disagree
  about. Put it on the sheet.
- Wall mounted handrail at existing stairs, separately from new stairs, by level range and by whether
  it is one side or both. A run through ten existing floors can equal a whole new stair.
- Cane detection rails and standpipe cane detection rails, as their own line. Code-driven, no drawing
  schedules them, and bidders that carry them say so explicitly.
- Alternating tread devices and ship's ladders, per location, with the tread count. Fabricators price
  them as fabricated stairs or exclude them in favor of a manufactured unit, and the difference is
  large.
- Roof access: catwalks, roof stairs, platforms, guardrail at low parapet, and roof anchors, each its
  own line. This block is where the most exclusions cluster, because roof details are typically the
  thinnest in the set.
- Relieving angles and shelf angles, split into furnish and install as two rows, and further split by
  elevation and by level where the assembly changes. One bidder enumerated bent plate footage per
  elevation per level across a dozen elevations. Two rows minimum, because the mason installs them
  and the fabricator supplies them, and both halves have to land somewhere.
- Thermal breaks at relieving angles, as their own line. No bidder seen carries them and an estimator
  plugs them against every bidder.
- Loose lintels, counted by opening, and separately from relieving angles. Furnish only.
- Embeds as a block of their own: embed plates, embed angles, leveling plates with anchor bolts,
  shear wall plates, clip angles, post bases, knife plates. Enumerate by condition, not by count.
  Furnish only, every time. On one leveling sheet this block is the only one where no bidder gave a
  number at all and the estimator carried a single plug for the lot.
- Anchor bolts, as their own line, with the setting responsibility stated. The specification
  typically says supply by the steel contractor and setting by the concrete trade, and one general
  contractor's sheet carries the instruction "confirm all understand this split" against that row.
- CMU wall clips and top-of-wall clips, counted, and located by room or level where the angle type
  changes.
- Seismic clips, separately from CMU clips.
- Elevator metals, broken all the way down: pit ladders per pit, sill angles per opening, divider
  beams, hoist and safety beams, sump pit grates and frames, buffer ladders, cants. A single elevator
  metals line will be answered five different ways. One bidder carried divider beams below a level
  and furnished only above it, because access changes.
- Grating and pit covers, by location, with the depth and mesh stated, in square feet, plus the angle
  frame as its own footage.
- Hatches and covers, per hatch, with the performance requirement stated. Watertight sidewalk hatches
  for a utility company are a specialty item and a supply-only quote for the highest water-tightness
  grade came in at several times the general contractor's plug.
- Bollards, pipe guards and vertical pipe protection, counted, and separated by whether they are
  garage, site or transformer.
- Canopy steel, separated into frame, deck, and trim or fascia. The frame is metals; the trim,
  fascia, flashing and sometimes the decking are excluded by the same bidder in the same paragraph.
- Overhead supports at garage doors, folding walls and storefronts, per location, in linear feet.
- Generator and equipment dunnage, its own line, with delegated design called out separately.
- Aluminum guardrails and balcony rails, by level and by location, in linear feet, and split by
  mounting method. Stem mount, fascia mount, surface mount to concrete, mount to pavers and dirt
  mount are all different prices for the same rail, and stem mount ran about a quarter above fascia
  mount for the identical profile.
- Prefabricated balconies, counted, and split into supply and install as two rows. Different
  companies bid the two halves and the estimator adds them.
- Gates, counted separately from the fence or rail they sit in. A gate cost roughly fifteen times a
  foot of the rail around it, and gate hardware is a third line because panic hardware and electronic
  closures are routinely excluded.
- Fencing by height and by location. Yard fence, pool enclosure, courtyard, terrace and screen fence
  price differently and are separately named by the aluminum bidders.
- Shop drawings, engineering calculations, stamped calculations and delegated design, as separate
  lines. Multiple bidders price them as named line items rather than folding them into overhead, and
  a stamped-calculations add appeared separately from a shop-drawing add on the same sheet.
- Finish and coating standards as their own rows: galvanizing standard, shop primer surface
  preparation grade for interior and exterior, and the ornamental joint finish grade. A sheet that
  asks bidders to confirm which joint finish grade applies where is preventing a real dispute.
- Fire watch, as its own line. Uniform or dedicated fire watch is separately priced by most bidders
  and is excluded by default by several.
- Billing portal and payment-application fees, as a percentage of contract value. Two bidders on
  different projects quoted the identical percentage, so it behaves as a market-standard adder.
- On renovation, an existing-conditions block: cutting and dropping existing members, reinforcement
  of existing beams and columns by shape and location, floor infills at existing openings by count
  and by level, cleaning of corroded existing steel, temporary shoring, and grout at new bearing.
  This block alone can run half the sheet.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Member sizes, shapes and connection types within one scope item. A stair is a stair; the channel
  size is an attribute.
- Individual embed plate counts within one condition. Enumerate the conditions, count within them.
- Bolts, washers, shims, expansion anchors and epoxy anchors. They belong to the item, and where they
  are excluded the bidder says so at the item.
- Coating film thickness, primer product and surface preparation within one finish class. The finish
  class is already an enumeration axis.
- Grid lines and individual member marks on structural work, unless the sheet is a renovation
  reinforcement schedule, where they are the content.
- Mobilization counts, deliveries and field measurement visits, except in aluminum supply packages,
  where they are priced inclusions and belong on the line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. On this trade
the test fires hardest in three places: anything that crosses the supply and install seam in section
five, anything on the roof, and anything with the word decorative, ornamental, aluminum or glass in
its name.

There is a second test that matters more here than elsewhere. If an item is furnished by this trade
and installed by another, it needs two rows, not one, and the two rows need different bidders'
answers in them. A single row for relieving angles will be answered yes by a fabricator who means it
will supply them and yes by a fabricator who means it will install them too, and the difference is
roughly a third of the cost.

Silence is a finding, and on this trade it is usually a documents finding rather than a bidder
finding. If several bidders exclude the same item citing the same missing detail, the scope line
should record that the detail is missing, and the item should go to a request for information
before it goes to leveling.

### The structural gap list

These are the recurring plugs: scope no bidder carries that a general contractor adds to every
number before comparing. Naming them on the scope list is doing the plugging in advance.

- Concrete fill at metal pan stair treads and landings. Excluded by every fabricator seen, and
  plugged identically against every bidder on one project.
- Thermal breaks at relieving angles and at balcony connections.
- Embeds as a block, when no bidder prices them.
- Installation of relieving angles and loose lintels, when the fabricator supplies only.
- Installation of prefabricated balconies and of aluminum railings, when the manufacturer supplies
  only.
- Installation of ballasted roof guardrail, which came from a roofing subcontractor's price.
- Hoisting, crane, conveyance, lifts and staging.
- Mock-up frames and mock-up pads.
- Access to elevator metals, which sits inside a shaft nobody has scaffolded.
- Generator and equipment dunnage.
- Roof anchors and fall protection.
- Sign supports and sign stanchions.
- Fire watch.
- Stamped calculations and shop drawings, where the bidder prices them as adds.
- Site and landscape metal: fencing, gates, gate hardware, tree guards, bollards, catenary posts.
- Demolition and temporary shoring on renovation work.
- Louver support steel, installation angles, clips and fasteners.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Very little of the above is pure boilerplate in this trade, which is the reverse of most. Repeat
bidders do reuse a general exclusions block, but the long numbered lists are written for the set,
and the tell is the citation: a scope-specific exclusion carries a sheet number and a reason, and a
general exclusion does not.

What is project signal:

- Any exclusion whose reason is a documents defect. No details, none shown, locations unclear,
  insufficient information, requires field verification, too many conflicting details. These are the
  highest-value sentences in the trade. On one project thirty-five numbered exclusions were almost
  entirely of this kind, and each one names the sheet.
- Any exclusion naming a conflict between two details. Bidders in this trade routinely state which of
  two conflicting details they priced, sometimes on a per-item basis. That is a documents problem
  surfaced by the market, and it belongs in a request for information rather than in a leveling
  adjustment.
- A stated departure between the stair plans and the rail standards sheet. One bidder repeated the
  same sentence under all seven stairs. Where the plan views and the details disagree about tread
  type or post type, every bidder has priced one of the two and none of them have priced the same
  one.
- An exclusion recording that an item shown in a rendering does not appear on the elevations.
- A disclosure that the fabricator or its erector is not certified to the level a specification
  commonly requires.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

- Access. Free and clear access for installation is the single most repeated qualification in the
  trade, stated per line by the enumerating bidders and as a blanket by the rest. Interior work with
  lifts and forklifts, level compacted ground for equipment, a shakeout area for material, and clear
  sequenced delivery are all named as conditions.
- Staging, and specifically who owns it. Relieving angle installation is priced on the assumption
  that it happens concurrently with the mason and rides the mason's staging; a boom lift, pump jacks
  or independent staging is a change. If the masonry sequence slips, the metals price moves.
- Shop primer is explicitly an impermanent coating. One bidder states it protects for a short
  exposure only; another states its standard shop paint on stairs holds for three months of exterior
  exposure and it will not repaint for concrete spillage, hoisting through the stairwell, or damage
  by other trades using the stairs. Stairs are the construction circulation of the building, and the
  bidder is telling you it will not pay for that.
- Material will not be ordered until approved shop drawings are received, and a timely turnaround is
  required to hold the schedule. Submittal turnaround is a schedule risk this trade names out loud.
- Delegated design boundaries. One bidder disclaims design responsibility for code compliance
  entirely and assigns it to the architect and engineer. Another includes calculations for stair
  connections but not for the structure around the stair, and excludes stamped shop drawings while
  including stamped calculations. Another states that any additional labor or material resulting from
  delegated design is not included, which means the calculation may find the assembly needs more
  steel and that steel is a change order.
- Connections not provided. On renovation work, bidders assume the connections that the drawings do
  not show, and say so per line. If the engineer later issues connection details heavier than
  assumed, that is a change.
- Quantities are estimated only and subject to confirmation. Stated flatly by the most quantified
  bidder in the corpus, which is worth remembering when reading its take-off as if it were a
  contract.
- Price validity. Seven days, ten days, fifteen days, thirty days. This trade holds price for a
  shorter window than any other, and multiple bidders cite steel and aluminum market volatility as
  the reason. One manufacturer excludes escalation entirely and tells the buyer to carry it.
- Tariffs. A live variable. One bidder priced a tariff add worth roughly a fifth of its base; others
  answered that most material is domestic and assumed no exposure. A bidder who includes custom
  duties in its number has offshore fabrication and a different exposure again.
- Attachment prerequisites imposed on other trades. Aluminum and railing suppliers state minimum
  concrete strength, minimum slab thickness, minimum anchor embedment, minimum edge and end distance,
  and minimum blocking thickness, as conditions of the warranty. A slab edge or a blocking detail
  that does not meet them is a redesign, and nothing on the architectural drawings will tell you.
- Sequencing prerequisites. Knife plates, ledgers and post shoes must be installed into blocking
  before the exterior finish goes on and before the lightweight concrete is poured. The balcony
  arrives later. Miss the window and the whole assembly is a change.
- Commercial terms as scope. One aluminum supplier attached a two-page commercial addendum: no
  automatic flow-down of prime contract terms, payment not conditioned on owner payment, termination
  for cause but not convenience, no assignment, entitlement to time and money for contractor-caused
  delay, no set-off or withholding, indemnity limited to proven negligence with the bidder choosing
  counsel, liability capped at the subcontract value, consequential and liquidated damages excluded,
  arbitration without fee shifting. Another excluded retainage in its exclusion list, where an
  estimator reading for scope will skim past it. These change what a number is worth as much as any
  scope line does.
- The priced breakdown may not be severable. One supplier's segment prices carry a note that the
  breakdown is informational only and valid only for purchase of the complete package. Extracting one
  segment for comparison is not permitted by the offer.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section that governs the trade. Misc metals is not primarily a scope question, it is a
custody question, and the drawings never answer it.

### The mode question, per work family

A single proposal routinely runs three modes at once: furnish and install on stairs and rails,
furnish only on embeds and relieving angles, and install only on somebody else's balconies. The
package-level mode is a fiction. Ask the mode per work family or the answers will not compare.

The trade has its own notation for this, and where a bidder uses it there is no ambiguity: a suffix
per scope block declaring fabricate and supply, fabricate prime and install, fabricate prime and
supply, fabricate galvanize and install, fabricate galvanize and supply, or fabricate color-galvanize
and install. Six modes, distinguished by both the custody boundary and the finish. Adopt that grain
on the scope sheet and the seam disappears as a problem.

### The seams that recur

- Relieving angles, shelf angles and loose lintels. Furnished by metals, installed by the mason,
  essentially always. Supplied in maximum lengths, hardware excluded, and where the metals contractor
  does install them it is riding the mason's staging on the mason's schedule. Where a thermal break
  bracket is specified, the bracket comes from a specialty manufacturer, the angle may come from that
  manufacturer or from a local fabricator depending on the condition, the anchorage and screws come
  from others, and the installation is the mason's. Four parties in one detail.
- Embeds, anchor bolts and leveling plates. Furnished by metals, set by the concrete trade, laid out
  by others. Every one of the three handoffs has been seen going wrong, and layout is the one nobody
  claims.
- Knife plates, seat plates, post shoes and ledgers for prefabricated balconies. Supplied by the
  balcony manufacturer, installed by others, into blocking provided by the carpenter, before the
  exterior finish and before the lightweight concrete. This is the tightest sequencing seam in the
  trade and it has three trades and a pour in it.
- Prefabricated balconies and aluminum railings. Manufacturers supply; an iron worker installs. On
  one project the estimator assembled the package by taking material from one company and
  installation from another and summing them, and neither company alone was the package. Rail
  installation ran roughly seventy percent of rail material, and balcony installation roughly forty
  percent of balcony material, so the install half is never a rounding error.
- Ballasted roof guardrail. The supplier furnishes; the roofer installs, because the rail bears on
  the roof assembly. Who supplies the pavers the ballast bears on is an open question on the leveling
  sheet.
- Stairs and the concrete trade. Pan stairs arrive empty. Concrete fill at treads and landings is
  excluded by every fabricator seen and is a plug. Footings and pads for stairs must be poured before
  erection.
- Stairs and the carpenter. Wall mounted rails need blocking, and blocking is excluded by every
  bidder. Wall preparation before installation and wall repair after are also excluded.
- Stairs and the painter. Shop primer is not a finish. The painter's own trade knowledge records
  field touch-up of shop primer as commonly excluded on the painting side too, which makes it a
  genuine two-sided gap rather than a routing question. Finish coat over galvanizing is a second one,
  and galvanizing needs different preparation than primer.
- Galvanizing itself. Whether an item is mill, shop primed, hot-dip galvanized, primer-galvanized or
  color-galvanized changes the price, the lead time and the painter's scope. Shop primer on galvanized
  items is a named exclusion. Color galvanizing and powder coating are alternatives, and a value
  engineering deduct for galvanized in lieu of powder coat is a standard offer.
- Louvers. The louver manufacturer supplies the louver and excludes the structural supports, the
  installation angles, the clips and the fasteners, and states a design load the surrounding
  construction is presumed to satisfy. Somebody has to make the support steel, and the louver
  schedule does not say who.
- Roof screens. The manufacturer supplies the frame and panels; installation is by others; the base
  supports attach to blocking by others which the manufacturer will not design; and the estimator
  splits the package into a frame line and a cladding line with a note to check the cladding against
  the siding package.
- Glass guardrails. Contested four ways. The fabricator may carry the posts and the glass, or the
  posts only with the glass by the glazier, or neither. A general contractor's own scope sheet asked
  the question directly: glass panel supply and install by the glazing contractor, steel supports by
  the metal fabricator?
- Elevator metals. Split three ways inside a single bid. Divider beams furnished and installed below
  a level and furnished only above, because access changes; hoist beams furnished only or excluded to
  the elevator supplier; sill angles carried or not; pit ladders and sump grates separately. Access
  into the shaft is a plug because nobody has scaffolded it.
- Hatches. Watertight sidewalk hatches for a utility company are a specialty purchase with a
  performance grade, not a fabricated item, and a supply-only quote at the highest grade came in at
  several times the plug.
- Fencing and gates. Bid by the aluminum supplier, present in the site package, annotated "assume
  with landscaping" on the metals sheet, and plugged anyway. Gate hardware splits again: hinges and
  magnetic latches included, panic hardware and electronic closures and access control excluded to
  the door hardware and electrical trades.
- Standpipe railings and pipe guards at standpipes. Fire protection's pipe, metals' rail, and no
  drawing locates them reliably. One bidder excluded them outright because they were not located in
  plan.
- Roof penetrations. Every guardrail post, catwalk leg and anchor that penetrates the roof is a
  flashing detail belonging to the roofer, and no metals proposal mentions it.
- Renovation demolition. Cutting, dropping and re-attaching existing members is carried by the metals
  contractor; disposal of the material is excluded to demolition; temporary shoring during the cut is
  excluded by nearly everyone and belongs to somebody.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What bidders actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The architectural stair and rail detail sheets, and the rail standards sheet. This is the primary
   document, and it is also the primary source of internal conflict. Where a stair plan view and the
   rail standards sheet disagree about tread type, post type or rail configuration, expect every
   bidder to have priced one of them without agreement.
2. The structural drawings, sheet by sheet, for clips, embeds, relieving angles, lintels, deck and
   framing. Bidders cite these more precisely than any other trade cites anything.
3. The elevations, for relieving angle and lintel take-off. Relieving angle footage is measured off
   elevations, level by level, and lintels are counted off the same elevations by opening.
4. The enlarged plans and details for elevator metals, pits and hatches.
5. The specification, for the galvanizing standard, the surface preparation grades, the ornamental
   joint finish grade, delegated design requirements and the basis-of-design manufacturer. Cited by
   the careful bidders, ignored by the rest.
6. The landscape drawings, when they carry site railings, fencing, tree guards or light posts. Read
   by some bidders and by nobody else.
7. The mechanical, electrical and plumbing drawings, for dunnage, screens and standpipe locations.
   Almost never read, which is why dunnage is always a plug.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Union or open shop labor, and prevailing wage applicability.
- Whether the item is furnished only or furnished and installed. The largest single variable in the
  trade and the one the drawings are silent on completely.
- Who sets the embeds and who lays them out.
- Whether the metals contractor gets the mason's staging for relieving angles, and when.
- Whether the stair concrete fill, the wall blocking and the wall repair after rail installation are
  carried elsewhere.
- Whether the aluminum package is a separate bid or inside this one.
- Whether the general contractor will accept a manufacturer's standard railing system in place of the
  drawn one, which is the difference between a fabricated price and a catalog price.
- Delegated design scope and whether calculations, shop drawings, or both need a stamp.
- Shop drawing turnaround time, which gates material ordering.
- Fabrication lead time. Steel fabricators rarely state it; specialty manufacturers state engineering
  at four to six weeks from a complete submittal, frames at two to three weeks and panels at four to
  six weeks after shop drawing approval, and aluminum at ten to twelve weeks after submittal and
  field-measure approval. On a fast schedule the aluminum package is a direct-buy decision.
- Field measurement responsibility and how many visits are needed. Aluminum suppliers price the
  visits.
- Mobilization count. Aluminum suppliers include a stated number and charge for more; on one project
  eight mobilizations were priced in, on another three.
- Whether a tower crane will be on site, and whether the courtyard is reachable by boom lift.
- Whether hoisting, lifts and staging are the general contractor's.
- Whether a mock-up is required, and whether the mock-up needs its own pad and frame.
- Fire watch, uniform or non-dedicated, and who pays.
- Tariff exposure and whether fabrication is domestic or offshore.
- Warranty duration. A two-year requirement against a one-year market default is worth a real deduct,
  and on a supply package the warranty was a priced line of its own.
- Insurance program. Whether an owner-controlled program applies, and the add if the bidder must use
  its own general liability.
- Bond, above a contract value threshold.
- Billing portal fees.
- Local residency and workforce policy compliance.
- Environmental product declarations, which some manufacturers do not offer at all.

Set-stage behavior. At design development the misc metals package is a rate, not a scope: an
estimator budgets it as a cost per square foot, and may apply two different rates to two parts of the
building because the density of misc metals varies by area type. Bidders at that stage quote railings
against a partial take-off and everything else is a plug. By permit and construction documents the
stair and rail details exist and the enumerated take-offs start arriving; the relieving angle and
embed blocks are the last to firm up because they live on the structural sheets and the elevations
rather than on a schedule. On renovation work the package never fully firms up, because half of it
depends on details the engineer of record has not issued and both the bidders and the estimator price
those as allowances against counted locations.

The most useful thing to know about this trade at any set stage is that the exclusion lists are the
survey. If four bidders exclude the same item citing the same missing detail, the detail is missing,
and that is worth more than four prices.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Risers, for stairs. The priced unit, and the count nobody publishes.
- Linear feet, for every kind of rail: guardrail, offset handrail at guardrail, wall mounted rail,
  cane detection rail, relieving angle, bent plate, fencing, catwalk rail. The dominant unit in the
  trade.
- Each, for gates, bollards, pipe guards, clips, sill angles, divider beams, lintels, hatches, sump
  covers, pit ladders, catenary posts and prefabricated balconies.
- Locations, for anything repeating that is not quite identical: canopies, embed conditions, pit
  covers, floor infills, slab openings.
- Square feet, for grating, deck, landing plate and screen cladding.
- Pounds, for structural framing, facade support steel, dunnage frames and, in aluminum packages, for
  the total material weight of the whole proposal.
- Tons and squares, when a structural package is folded in.
- Lot, for anything the bidder does not want to quantify.
- Percent of contract value, for billing portal fees, bond and insurance.
- The whole building, as the leveling denominator, in one of several forms.

That last one is worth its own paragraph. Across four projects the estimator normalized this package
four different ways: cost per gross square foot of building, cost per gross square foot net of an
excluded portion, cost per residential unit, and, for the sub-packages, cost per linear foot of
guardrail and cost per prefabricated balcony. Nothing about this trade fixes the denominator. The
sub-package denominators come off the drawings and are meaningful; the whole-building denominators
are a sanity check and nothing more, because the package's content varies enormously between
projects of the same size.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Package definition. Whether structural steel, the aluminum block, roof screens or roof anchors are
   inside or outside the number swings it by a factor of two or more. This dominates every price
   variable in the trade and it is a scope-sheet question, not a market question.
2. Furnish only versus furnish and install. Installation ran roughly forty percent of material on
   balconies, roughly seventy percent on aluminum railings, roughly forty-four percent on a supplied
   railing system, and half the furnish rate per foot on relieving angles.
3. Union versus open shop labor.
4. Finish. Mill, shop primer, hot-dip galvanized, primer-galvanized, color-galvanized and powder
   coated are a ladder, and the value engineering deducts move between rungs.
5. Stair construction. A fabricated metal pan stair, a checkerplate stair, a grating stair and a
   pre-engineered manufactured stair are four different prices for the same riser count; checkerplate
   ran roughly twelve percent above pan on one project.
6. Riser count and rail footage, which are the two quantities most often wrong.
7. Mounting method on aluminum rails. Stem mount ran roughly a quarter above fascia mount for the
   identical profile; mounting to pavers, to concrete and to wood blocking are three more prices.
8. Access and staging. Whether the fabricator rides another trade's staging, whether a tower crane
   exists, whether the shaft is scaffolded.
9. Delegated design and stamping scope.
10. Material market volatility and tariff exposure. Price validity in this trade is measured in days.
11. Renovation unknowns: shoring, cutting existing members, corroded steel, and details the engineer
    has not issued.
12. Warranty duration, insurance program, bond and portal fees.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest bid ran at about one and a half times on a renovation where the package was
tightly defined, about three times on two new-construction projects, and about seven and a half times
on a project where the ranking mixed full-package fabricators with single-product suppliers. That
last spread is not a price spread. Bidder counts per project run from about five to about twelve
rows, but rows are not companies: one supplier's two product configurations occupy two rows, one
supplier's two same-day options occupy two rows, one company's revision rounds occupy several, and a
two-company pairing occupies one. The real depth of a misc metals bid list is usually half what the
ranking suggests.

Two ranking traps are specific to this trade and both were live in the corpus. A stairs-and-rails-only
bidder ranks first on a full-package sheet at a quarter of the cost per square foot of the real bids,
and nothing in the ranking marks it as partial except a parenthetical someone typed into the bidder
name. A component supplier's quote addressed to all bidders is a product offer feeding whoever wins,
not a company competing for the package, and counting it inflates the apparent depth of the list.

One more convention worth knowing: an estimator does not compare base bids on this trade, and the
adjustments are not small. On one project the selected bidder's adjustments exceeded half of its base
bid; on another the adjustments against the low bidder were larger than the base. The base bid on
misc metals is a starting position. The scope list, and the plugs it forces you to name, is the
actual estimate.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on roughly thirty misc metals proposals and revision rounds, drawn from four
multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor, plus the
four matching estimator leveling sheets. It reads across the whole division rather than one token:
metal fabrications, stairs and rails, balconies and railings, ballasted roof guardrail, aluminum
railings, prefabricated balconies, roof screens, louvers and masonry support brackets, with the
structural steel package read only as a boundary. The proposals span design development, permit,
construction documents and conformed sets, and include one four-round negotiation ladder, several
revision pairs, and both new construction and one substantial renovation of an existing high-rise.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work in one market through one general
  contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors, institutional, healthcare, industrial,
  civil or public prevailing-wage work, where ornamental and architecturally exposed metal is a much
  larger share of the package.
- Only one project was a renovation, so the existing-conditions grain in section three, the
  structural-versus-misc boundary question, and the allowance-against-counted-locations pricing
  pattern all rest on that project alone. It is also the only project where a bidder enumerated
  reinforcement member by member, so the deepest grain example in the entry has a sample of one.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, who ended up carrying the plugs, or whether the supply and install pairings
  the estimator assembled actually held.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the access, staging and sequencing
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed, so treat it as untested here.
- No shop drawing or submittal round is in the corpus, so the delegated-design outcome, where the
  calculation finds the assembly needs more steel than the bid assumed, is a stated risk rather than
  an observed one.
- Architecturally exposed structural steel appears only as an exclusion. The corpus has not shown how
  it is priced or who bids it.
- Ornamental and historic metal appears once, as an extension to an existing brass rail on a
  renovation, and the only recorded difficulty was matching old and new finish. Nothing here covers
  ornamental metal as a real package.
- Stainless steel appears only inside aluminum railing quotes as a handrail material. The corpus has
  not shown a stainless package, a polishing grade schedule, or passivation as scope.
- Bike racks, mailbox surrounds and similar site specialties were bid in other divisions on every
  project and appear here only as metals exclusions, so their pull toward this package is asserted by
  the exclusion lists rather than observed in a bid.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the aluminum package and the steel package were bid by one
  company, so the entry cannot say whether that combination is available in this market or what it
  costs.
