# Rough carpentry

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the rough carpentry
package: wood framing, blocking, sheathing, and the panelized systems that increasingly carry them.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals, estimator leveling sheets, and one
negotiated subcontract. The entry speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is
carried by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real framers can bid it and a real estimator can
level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Section 061000 is the anchor and is what a negotiated subcontract names first. But this package is
never one section. A subcontract for it will list six or more: rough carpentry, sheathing, shop-
fabricated wood trusses, metal-plate-connected wood trusses, glued-laminated construction, and
then, without ceremony, the exterior windows section. That last one matters more than any of the
others, because the market has quietly made window installation part of rough carpentry.

The trade's real name in this market is the framing package, and its commercial shape forks three
ways before anything about the building matters.

The first fork is stick-built against panelized. Nearly every current bid is panelized: walls are
designed, engineered, fabricated and delivered as shop panels, and the field crew sets them. A
stick-built bid still exists and reads completely differently, with no shop drawing line, no
delivery line, and no submittal-phase obligations. When a bidder offers both, it will mark stick-
built labor as limited and panel labor as full, meaning it will stick-build what the panels cannot
reach. The panelized bid brings shop drawings, a design responsibility, a submittal review cycle,
and claim windows measured from delivery and from unbundling that a stick-built bid has no use for.

The second fork is furnish-and-install against labor-only. This one is the single most dangerous
thing about the trade for anyone reading a bid list, because a labor-only framing bid can be a
third of a turnkey bid and it will sit at the bottom of any ranking sorted on price. A labor-only
bidder excludes every dollar of material including fasteners and adhesives. There is usually one on
a bid list and sometimes two. Flag the mode before anything else.

The third fork is how much of the exterior envelope rides along. The market pulls three things into
this package by default, and the drawings will not tell you which are in:

- Window and exterior door installation, labor only, with the units furnished by the GC or a
  supplier. This is the strongest collapse attractor in the trade and it appears in nearly every
  proposal, sometimes inside the base, commonly as its own priced package, occasionally as an add-
  alternate. Where it appears, the flashing that goes with it comes too: sealant behind the flange,
  flexible flashing over the flange, pan flashing at the sill turning up the jambs.
- The air and vapour barrier at exterior wood walls. Carried by about half the market, and by the
  panelizers it is applied in the shop, on the panel, with field infill only at the joints. That
  makes barrier ownership a fabrication decision, not a site decision.
- Non-load-bearing interior partitions, when they are drawn as wood. This is the largest single
  detachable block in the trade and it can be a sixth of a base bid.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that this package pulls in even though it sits elsewhere in the
documents:

- Blocking, nailers, grounds and plywood backing required by every other trade. This is the
  package's defining obligation and its defining problem. Section 3 and section 5 are mostly about it.
- Craneage, hoisting, telehandlers and man lifts for the framing work. Ordinarily inside the base,
  and sometimes offered back as a deduct once the GC decides to supply a tower crane.
- Temporary safety rails, opening protection and temporary floor covers at chases and stairs, for
  the duration of framing.
- Temporary stairs below the podium, and temporary treads on the permanent stairs.
- Fire-rated gypsum coreboard at stairs and shaft liner, which sits on the drywall side of every
  boundary the framers otherwise draw and yet lands here often enough to need a line.
- Wood nailers on steel, and wood plates for steel beams, with the steel itself by others.
- Plywood backer panels for telecom, data and electrical rooms, though the fire-retardant-treated
  version of the same panel is commonly excluded.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like framing:

- Light-gauge metal framing in every form. Excluded by name in every proposal that mentions it, and
  it goes to drywall. When non-bearing partitions convert from wood to metal, the whole partition
  block moves with them.
- Balconies and decks, which leave in pieces rather than all at once. A framer will rough-frame a
  wood balcony and exclude the decking, the railing, the fascia, the sleepers, the sloped joists,
  the ledger flashing and every scrap of blocking for railing attachment. Prefabricated aluminium
  and steel balconies leave entirely and come back as an install-only alternate.
- Roof davits and their blocking. Excluded by nearly every bidder and priced back as an alternate,
  usually as two alternates once someone notices that blocking and installation are separable.
- Stair finishes: risers, treads, skirt boards, handrails, painting, rubber flooring. The framer
  builds a structure with temporary treads and stops.
- Structural steel, welding, drilling of steel, and every steel-to-steel connector. Excluded
  without exception.
- Insulation of any kind, except a narrow install-only carve-out for insulation in spaces the
  framing itself will make inaccessible.
- Firestopping and fire caulking, though fire blocking and draft stopping in wood are contested and
  land differently on different projects.

The renovation case is a different trade wearing the same name. On an existing building with no
wood structure there is no framing package and no bid list. What survives is a GC-carried
miscellaneous carpentry budget: a full-time project carpenter for the duration, blocking for new
windows, roof blocking, pipe portal framing, and the swing staging to reach the facade. Access
becomes the largest line in the package and carpentry becomes the smaller one.

## 2. How the market bids

This is one of the most structured proposal trades there is. Where a painter offers a number and a
bullet list, a framer offers a matrix.

The dominant form, used by most panelized bidders, is a table of work families crossed against two
independent columns, labor and material, each answered yes or no. A cover page carries the
project-level version, roughly fifteen to twenty rows: wall panels fabrication, wall panels site
installation, wall stick built, floor trusses, floor joists, floor engineered lumber, roof trusses,
roof rafters, demolition, window and door installation, flashing tapes, air barrier, roofing,
siding, decking, railings, taxes. Body pages carry the same structure per assembly, listing every
component with its species, grade, thickness and product: sill seal, bottom plate, top plate, studs,
jack stud assembly, headers, posts, sheathing. One variant uses four columns instead of two,
material-included, material-excluded, labor-included, labor-excluded, and runs to well over a
hundred rows.

The consequence is that this trade tells you its furnish-and-install boundary line by line, for
free, in a form a machine can read. Very few trades do. Use it.

Two other forms exist. A supplier-style bid organizes by package rather than assembly: wall panel
package, floor truss package, loose ship material for floor systems, roof truss package, loose ship
material for roof system, miscellaneous loose ship material, and field set. Every line begins
"supply and delivery of" or "installation of", so custody is stated per line rather than per column.
And a stick-built bid is a flat bulleted list with one price, closer to what other trades look like.

A caution on reading these matrices: the tick marks are frequently symbol-font glyphs that vanish
from extracted text while the row labels survive, so an extraction reads as a column of orphan
labels and a separate column of orphan yes and no values with no way to pair them. Render the page.
Several proposals in this trade are image-only or extract as character-shifted gibberish while
rendering perfectly, and at least one arrives as a saved email with the actual quote as five
untagged images inside it.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The basis set, by date, and increasingly by document family: architectural, structural,
  specification narrative, full specifications, addenda and bulletins, each ticked as received. That
  tick row is a documents-completeness signal a GC can read directly.
- Species and grade for studs and plates, sheathing thickness and rating by location, truss depth
  and spacing, and pressure-treated material where plates meet concrete. This is a materially
  specified trade and the proposals reflect it.
- Labor type, and often the specific wage zone or local. Nearly always present, because it is the
  largest price driver.
- Sales tax treatment, sometimes as a rate on the cover.
- Whether the price is turnkey.
- A material price validity window, which is short: commonly a week, sometimes five days, at the
  long end three weeks.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Quantities. Almost never. The exceptions are a hardware count and a stair riser count, each seen
  once.
- Any square-foot rate or unit price. The trade prices in lump sums even when it is enumerating a
  hundred components.
- Labor hours, unless the GC asks. When asked directly, several bidders will answer, and one will
  split framing hours from window installation hours. That makes this one of the few trades where
  an hours figure is obtainable, and it is worth asking for.
- Schedule duration, unless asked.
- Crew size.

Two proposal behaviors matter more than the rest. First, the exclusion list in this trade is a
documents report, not boilerplate. Framers exclude by naming the sheet and the detail, and often the
reason: "extent unclear, no cuts lead to detail", "locations not shown on plans", "no shear wall
locations shown on the referenced sheet". A single bid can carry eleven such exclusions. Read them
as a pre-written request-for-information list rather than as a scope boundary, because that is what
they are.

Second, boilerplate does travel, and here it travels between direct competitors more completely than
anywhere else. Two bidders on one list submitted substantively identical exclusion schedules and
clarification pages, sentence for sentence, including an unusual refusal to install windows on
buildings with a fluid-applied air barrier. Two panelizers on different projects share their terms
and conditions verbatim: the same material validity window, the same title-retention sentence, the
same site perimeter requirement, the same three claim windows. Leveling two such bids on their
exclusion lists produces no differentiation at all. Level on the priced structure instead, and note
that in this instance the two identical-looking documents described materially different products
because one carried non-bearing partitions in its base and the other alternated them out.

Revision habits are the richest in any trade here, because framing negotiates for months. A single
package can run seven rounds over three months. What actually happens across them:

- The base moves in both directions. A common shape is a large drop between the first and second
  round, sixteen percent or so, then a steady climb back as excluded items are negotiated in. A
  monotonic decline is not the norm.
- Scope text is often byte-identical across rounds. The movement is commercial, and where a bidder
  explains itself the explanation is commercial too: firm supplier quotes replacing square-foot
  budget numbers, waste optimisation, and mark-up reduction approaching buyout.
- The alternate list is where the real information lives. Alternates multiply round on round, split
  into finer pieces, get repriced, and migrate: an exclusion becomes an alternate, an alternate
  becomes base, a base item becomes a deduct. One combined davit alternate becomes a blocking
  alternate and an installation alternate at a lower combined price two rounds later.
- Whole packages leave. An air barrier package can sit in a bid for six rounds and vanish in the
  seventh, taking the total down while the framing price does not move. A total falling is not
  evidence of a concession.
- The commercial basis changes underneath the numbers. A tax basis flipping from exempt to included
  between two rounds means the round totals are measuring different things, and a naive delta
  reports a price movement that did not happen.
- Compliance quietly degrades. A late round can carry a deduct to shorten the warranty below what
  the specification requires, presented as a saving and labelled nowhere as a deviation.
- By the final round the grain has shifted from work family to drawing detail. The last round of a
  long ladder can carry five separate headline lines, each naming the structural or architectural
  detail it prices: sleeper curb blocking, generator blocking, privacy wall and railing blocking at
  the roof deck, catwalk railing, mock-up. That is the trade closing its blocking gap one detail at
  a time, late, at add pricing.

Two mechanical warnings about ordering rounds. Filename revision labels are unreliable: a file
labelled as the first revision can contain a document headed as the second, and a file labelled as
an early round can contain an attachment that cites a round three later, because the PDF is a bundle
assembled at some other moment. And a proposal often prices a set older than its own folder's stage,
stating so in a single cover field. Order by cited basis document, and read the document's own
header before trusting the filename.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Rough carpentry scope sheets have run 23, 37, 46 and 83 rows, and the GC-issued questionnaire behind
them has run 255. Target roughly sixty to ninety scope lines for a new-build multifamily framing
package, and expect the blocking block alone to want fifteen of them.

But row count is the wrong first question in this trade. The right one is grain, and the right grain
is the two-axis one the market already uses: each scope line needs a work family and an independent
labor-and-material answer. A line that says "roof trusses" without saying whether it is furnish,
install, or both is unbiddable, because a supplier-style bidder will read it one way and a field
framer the other, and both will be sincere.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Each wall family separately: exterior walls, interior bearing walls, interior shear walls,
  interior corridor walls, interior non-load-bearing partitions. Every structured proposal splits
  these, because the species, treatment and sheathing differ across them, and because the
  non-bearing family is detachable in a way the others are not.
- Non-load-bearing partitions, always as their own line, and always with the wood-or-metal question
  attached. This is the largest swing item in the trade. Removing them can take a sixth of a base
  bid out, and the same drawing condition can be assigned to framing in one building of a project
  and to drywall in the next.
- Each sheathing application separately: wall, shear wall, floor, roof flat, roof pitched, second
  layer at double-sided walls, parapet cap. The thickness and rating differ, the second layer is
  routinely excluded and priced as an add, and the sheathing product itself is a live commercial
  question the GC may also be asking.
- Each floor and roof framing system separately: trusses, engineered joists, dimensional joists,
  engineered beams and headers. Bidders answer these independently, and a bid can carry trusses and
  exclude joists.
- Every blocking type as its own row. This is the trade's most important enumeration and section 5
  gives the full list. At minimum: in-wall accessory blocking, full-height blocking at tubs and
  recessed cabinets, blocking outboard of the sheathing or barrier, roof blocking, roof edge
  blocking, curb and sleeper blocking for mechanical equipment, davit blocking, screen wall
  blocking, blocking around windows, blocking at window sills after installation, blocking for
  interior trim, blocking in metal-framed walls, fire blocking, and draft stopping. Each of these is
  answered differently by different bidders, which is precisely the test in the click test below.
- Window and exterior door installation, split at least three ways: units above the podium in wood
  walls, units at metal-framed levels, and the flashing package. Bidders price these separately and
  deduct them separately.
- Each flashing operation at openings separately: pan flashing at sill, flashing over the flange,
  sealant behind the flange, tape at the rough opening, interior backer rod and caulking, exterior
  backer rod and caulking, sill pans, back dams, expansion foam. Bidders include the first three and
  exclude most of the rest, individually. A single "window flashing" line will always be ambiguous.
- Stairs, one row per stair, keyed to its own drawing sheet, with the construction type stated.
  A project can have eight stairs of which half are precast or cast-in-place and out of this trade
  entirely. Then split the wood ones by component: stringers, landings, temporary treads, permanent
  treads, risers, handrail blocking, engineering and stamped shop drawings.
- Temporary work as its own block: temporary stairs below podium, temporary loading doors and their
  later removal and remobilization, temporary treads and their removal, temporary bracing, safety
  rails at floors, safety rails at roof, opening protection, temporary floor covers.
- Balconies and decks by type and count: wood-framed, inset, cantilevered, over low roof, and
  prefabricated. Each is answered differently and the counts are the leveling denominator.
- Hoisting split three ways: telehandler and lifts for own work, mobile crane, tower crane. These
  three land with three different parties and the third is usually a GC plug.
- Connection hardware by category: hold-downs with a count, anchor tiedown systems, uplift ties,
  hurricane clips, straps, hangers, epoxy anchors, wet-set anchors, sill anchor bolts, steel-to-wood
  connectors. Wet-set and cast-in-place anchors are excluded by essentially everyone and need to
  land somewhere.
- Air and vapour barrier split by location and application method: shop-applied on panels, field
  infill at joints, above podium, below podium, at garage separation walls, and fluid-applied at
  masonry. The application method changes who can bid it, as section 5 explains.
- Shop drawings, engineering and stamps as their own lines, separately for wall panels, trusses and
  stairs. Truss engineering in particular is a bounded product and a bidder will say so.
- Commercial rows: bond, insurance program participation, sales tax basis, warranty duration,
  project portal fees, residency or workforce compliance policies, labor hours. These are answered
  more often in this trade than in most, so asking is worthwhile.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Studs, plates, jacks, kings, headers and posts within one wall family collapse to that wall
  family's line. Every proposal lists them as components of a family, never as separate prices.
- Species, grade, treatment and spacing are attributes of a line, not lines.
- Room-by-room and unit-by-unit enumeration collapses. No document seen prices a room or a unit.
- Levels collapse to a range unless the framing system actually changes. Above-podium and
  below-podium is a real break and is enumerated everywhere; floor two through floor six is not.
- Individual truss, joist and beam counts collapse into their system line. Even the most quantified
  bid carries trusses as a package.
- Fasteners, nails, screws, adhesives and generic hardware collapse to one line. The named
  structural connectors do not.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In this trade
the test fires hardest on three things: every blocking type, the non-bearing partition family, and
every operation at a window opening. It also fires on the labor-material axis independently, which is
unusual: a line can pass the test on material and fail it on labor, and the scope row has to be able
to say so.

Silence is a finding here more than in most trades, because the trade's own proposal format makes
silence conspicuous. If a bidder's matrix has a row for a blocking type and that row is blank while
competitors answered it, that is a gap to surface, not an inclusion to assume.

### The structural gap list

These are the items no bidder carries, that a GC adds to every number before comparing. They recur
across every project in the corpus, and a scope line set that names them is doing the estimator's
plugging in advance.

1. A full-time project carpenter for the duration of the job. Plugged against every single bidder
   on every new-build project, sized in months and priced at an hourly rate. Nobody bids it.
2. Blocking outboard of the sheathing, the barrier, the gypsum or the roof membrane. Excluded by
   every framer and, per the siding side, excluded by the siding bidders too. See section 5.
3. Roof blocking and roof sheathing at mechanical equipment: curbs, sleepers, rafts, dunnage,
   isolators. Plugged in crew-weeks, not in square feet.
4. Davit blocking and davit installation, as two separate plugs.
5. Roof mechanical screen walls and line-set screen walls, including their insulation and thermal
   breaks, plugged per linear foot of wall.
6. Tower craneage, plugged per month off a crane vendor's own rate card.
7. Temporary stairs below the podium, and temporary loading doors with their later removal and
   remobilization.
8. Extra window blocking at sills, after window installation, to support interior trim.
9. Miscellaneous materials, fasteners and conveyance, as a lump.
10. Framing premiums at odd conditions: a crawlspace, a terrace step-down, a setback. Plugged per
    square foot of the affected area at a premium over the base rate.
11. Supports and blocking for brick relieving angles at masonry facades.
12. Nailers on steel beams.
13. Mock-up framing.
14. Safety rails at the roof, as distinct from safety rails at floors, which framers do carry.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate and it is heavily shared: repeat bidders reproduce their lists
verbatim between projects, and unrelated competitors reproduce each other's. Read boilerplate for the
trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, and this trade produces more of it than any other:

- An exclusion that names a sheet or a detail. Framers do this constantly, and it means the bidder
  opened that sheet and made a decision. A single bid can carry eleven.
- An exclusion carrying a stated documents reason: extent unclear, no cuts lead to the detail,
  locations not shown on the plans, no locations shown on the referenced sheet. These are the
  project's request-for-information list, pre-written by the market, and two different bidders will
  independently name the same missing detail.
- A disclosed deviation where the bidder tells you which side of a document conflict it priced. A
  bidder carrying seven-sixteenths sheathing where the architectural assemblies specify five-eighths,
  citing the structural plans by name, assembly by assembly, is the single most useful paragraph in
  the trade. It is a specification deviation and a documents conflict at the same time and it is
  never routed as an exclusion.
- A counter-question in an answered scope sheet: "please indicate where this is shown on the contract
  drawings", "we didn't find this sheet on the revision set, please re-send", "please clarify". These
  are the same signal in an interrogative mood.
- An assumption stated because the drawings did not settle it: shadow box window surrounds assumed
  factory applied, a masonry wall assumed to be a fire separation wall, truss chord sizing to be
  determined after design.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

Treat each as a constraint the scope must honor or price around.

- Material prices are held for days, not weeks. A week is typical, five days is common, three weeks
  is generous. Several bidders reserve the right to adjust for commodity price movement until a
  contract is signed, and one asks outright for an escalation clause in the contract. One bidder
  excludes material escalation by name, which pushes the whole risk back to the GC. This is a
  commodity trade and the price on the page has a shelf life.
- Truss pricing may be declared a budget in a footnote below a firm-looking lump sum.
- Truss engineering is bounded by an industry standard that splits responsibility between the
  project engineer, the contractor and the truss designer. The bidder certifies only that its
  trusses meet the loads printed on them. Confirming the overall building design is extra, and drift
  load recalculation during truss design can trigger extra charges after award.
- Blocking locations and dimensions must arrive before a stated milestone, which is either the start
  of wall shop drawings or the start of framing punch. Miss it and the blocking is out at any price,
  including full-height blocking a panelizer will not install in the field under any circumstances.
- Mechanical, electrical and plumbing coordination must be complete before wall panel construction
  starts. The panel shop is a hard schedule gate that stick-building does not have.
- Design changes after submittal approval are billed hourly for redesign, hourly for extra shop
  labor, and at cost for materials if panels or trusses are already in production.
- Claim windows are short and there are three of them: from delivery, from unbundling, and from
  installation. Defects reported late are not only unremedied, they are charged again where the same
  condition recurs at upper levels.
- Site access is a priced assumption. Panelizers require a perimeter clear enough for a telehandler
  and boom lift, commonly stated as twenty feet, and reserve the right to reprice without it.
- Safety rail maintenance transfers at initial punch of each floor. Before that the framer maintains
  them; after that the GC does; rails on exterior decks belong to nobody.
- Union against open shop, prevailing wage, federal wage requirements, and local residents jobs
  policies are all explicitly excluded unless named on the cover. Non-prevailing-wage is stated as a
  positive assertion by open-shop bidders.
- Payment terms are unusually assertive here. Title to delivered material is retained until paid in
  full, retainage is refused on material, retainage is demanded back at framing inspection rather
  than at occupancy, and work-stoppage-plus-lien is threatened on a stated day count.
- One bidder's own conditions make its deducts non-refundable, so its number can go up but not
  reliably down. Read the deduct language before assuming an alternate is symmetric.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### Blocking: the seam that matters most

Blocking is the trade's central problem and it deserves the most words, because it is the work every
other trade depends on, no drawing fully describes it, and its boundaries are drawn by exclusion
rather than by inclusion.

Blocking sorts into four zones by where it sits relative to the wall's layers, and each zone has a
different owner.

The first zone is inside the wood wall, and the framer owns it. On a panelized job most of it is
installed in the shop, in the panel, which means it must be drawn before the panels are drawn. The
market's own list of what is included here is remarkably consistent: kitchen cabinet blocking,
bathroom accessory and grab bar blocking, fire cabinet blocking, floor-to-floor fire stop blocking,
draft stop blocking in walls over eight feet, blocking for the horizontal edges of wall sheathing,
shear blocking per code, and blocking for mechanical and electrical panels. One bidder is specific
enough to give a rate: three rows in kitchens, one row in bathrooms. Handrail blocking at halls and
stairwells is in the same zone but is field-installed rather than shop-installed, which makes it the
one item in the list with a different schedule.

Full-height blocking is a subcategory with its own rule. Blocking and studs for tub and shower nail
fins, recessed medicine cabinets and fire extinguisher cabinet bays are included only if they are
drawn and dimensioned before wall shop drawings start, and one panelizer will not field-install
full-height blocking at all, at any price. If the locations arrive late, the work does not happen in
this package.

The second zone is inside the wood wall but for something the framer does not consider its business.
Blocking for interior trim, running trim, baseboard, crown, closet shelving, television mounts and
curtain rods is excluded by most bidders as a category. Beware of one particular contradiction: a
proposal can list interior trim blocking as included in its blocking table and exclude blocking for
trim in its miscellaneous table two pages later, in the same document. The priced assembly table is
the authoritative one, but the safe reading is that this scope needs its own row and its own answer.

The third zone is outboard of the sheathing, the barrier, the gypsum or the roof membrane, and this
is the real gap. Every framer excludes it. The exclusions are explicit and they name their reason:
blocking outside the barrier is excluded per the siding scope; blocking outside the weather barrier
or gypsum is excluded citing three details; all work after the barrier is excluded; blocking or other
framing installed above gypsum board, the barrier and the roof membrane is excluded; all blocking
over wall sheathing is excluded. That is five independent bidders across two projects drawing the
same line. The siding side of the corpus draws the same line from the other direction, so the work
sits between two packages that both point at each other. A GC estimator on one project carries a
dedicated scope row for exactly this condition, citing three architectural details, two of which
appear verbatim in a framer's own exclusion. The estimator knows, copies the market's own reference
into the scope sheet, and still gets no bidder to price it. This is the trade's defining unclaimed
scope and it needs its own row on every project.

The fourth zone is at the roof, and it is where the highest-value blocking hides. Curb and sleeper
blocking for mechanical equipment, dunnage and mechanical rafts, roof edge blocking at step-backs
and setbacks, parapet blocking, screen wall and windscreen blocking, davit blocking, generator
blocking, catwalk and railing blocking, and blocking for roof anchors. Roofers need curbs blocked
before they can flash them and they exclude the blocking; framers exclude most of it as not shown or
price it as an add. On the long negotiation this scope came back one detail at a time in the final
round as five separate priced lines. Plan for it as an enumerated block from the start.

Two more blocking boundaries close the picture. Blocking in metal-framed walls is excluded by every
wood framer that mentions it, and belongs to drywall, which means every specialty, accessory and
grab bar in a metal-stud wall needs a drywall-side answer. And blocking around windows, and extra
blocking at window sills after the windows are installed to support interior trim, is excluded by
most framers, questioned by the GC on its own scope sheet with an annotation wondering whether it
belongs to drywall, left unanswered by the entire bid list, appears on one bidder's post-negotiation
comeback list as an item to be added back, and finally appears as a numbered obligation in the
executed subcontract. That is the full life cycle of a scope gap, and it is worth carrying as a
named line from day one.

### Windows and exterior doors

The window seam is the second most consequential and it has a shape the drawings will not show.

The units are furnished by the GC or a supplier and installed by the framer, labor only, on nearly
every project. What the framer does: receive, unload, distribute, set, level, shim, fasten per the
manufacturer, set the flange in sealant, install pan flashing at the sill turning up the jambs,
install flashing over the flange, and integrate with the barrier. What the framer does not do:
hardware, screens, limit stops, final cleaning, field mulling, interior or exterior backer rod and
caulking beyond the flange, sill pans as a fabricated metal item, back dams, and expansion foam.
Each of those is a separate answer.

Three couplings decide whether this seam even exists on a given project:

- The barrier system governs it. Two bidders state flatly that they do not offer window installation
  on buildings with a fluid-applied air barrier system, and one adds that its window install and
  flashing alternates are struck from the proposal if the specification lands on one. A specification
  decision on the barrier therefore removes bidders from the window installation market entirely.
  If the barrier system is undecided on the set, the window installation scope is undecided too.
- Wall type governs it. Installation is routinely limited to exterior wood walls above the podium.
  Windows at metal-framed levels and below the podium are a separate priced add on nearly every bid.
- Window extensions are a third scope. A framer will install factory-supplied extensions and will
  exclude non-factory extensions and their blocking. Where a facade is masonry, field-applied
  extensions appear as their own estimator scope row measured in linear feet.

### Non-load-bearing partitions and the light-gauge boundary

The wood framer draws a hard line at light-gauge metal framing and drywall carries it. When the
drawings show non-bearing partitions as wood, the wood framer carries them and the boundary is
quiet. When the project value-engineers them to metal, the entire block moves to drywall, and the
size of that move is startling: bidders price the deduct at roughly twelve to sixteen percent of a
framing base. A GC will carry both a wood rate and a metal rate for the same partitions and record
the drywall contractor's number against the framers' columns to compare. On one two-building project
the same scope row was assigned to framing in one building and to drywall in the other.

This is the largest single commercial decision in the package and the drawings state it only as a
wall type. Carry the partition family as its own line with the wood-or-metal question attached, and
expect the answer to change during negotiation.

### The air and vapour barrier

Barrier ownership is genuinely unsettled and it moves. The same bidder, using the same proposal
template, carries the barrier inside its framing package on one project and excludes it on another.
About half the market carries it; the other half excludes it and it goes to a barrier contractor or
to siding.

Where a panelizer carries it, the barrier and the window-opening flashing are applied in the shop,
on the panel, with field infill only at the joints. That has a real consequence: the barrier's
quality and continuity become a fabrication matter, and the field crew is patching seams rather than
installing a system. It also means the barrier ships with the wall, so barrier delivery is panel
delivery.

Below-podium barrier, barrier at garage separation walls between wood and concrete, and fluid-applied
barrier at masonry are each excluded from the shop-applied scope and priced separately. And on the
long negotiation the whole barrier package left the framing bid in the final round after six rounds
inside it, which is a reminder that this seam is provisional right up to buyout.

### Other seams

- Trusses and engineered lumber. The framer furnishes and installs the trusses and furnishes shop
  drawings, but truss engineering is a bounded product: the truss designer certifies only that the
  trusses meet the loads printed on them, and confirming that the building design is adequate for
  those loads belongs to the project engineer. Sealed truss plans, third-party engineering, field
  inspection by the truss engineer of record, and professional liability coverage for the truss
  supplier are all excluded. If the specification requires any of them, nobody has priced it.
- Steel. The framer installs wood nailers on steel beams and wood plates for steel beams, and
  excludes furnishing, erecting, cutting, drilling and welding the steel, along with every
  steel-to-steel and steel-to-wood connector. Flitch beams must arrive pre-drilled and ready to
  install; the plates and bolts are somebody else's. Drilling through steel is excluded on both
  sides of the seam by both trades often enough to check.
- Concrete anchors. Wet-set anchors, cast-in-place anchor bolts, embedment plates and the building
  tie-down hardware set into the pour are excluded by essentially every framer. The framer installs
  the epoxy anchors and post-installed hardware but not the ones that had to go in before the pour.
  This is a real gap and it has a schedule dimension, because it must be resolved before the
  concrete is placed.
- Gypsum sheathing and coreboard. Interior gypsum sheathing at non-shaft sides, gypsum on shear
  walls, gypsum outboard of wood sheathing, and any taping or compound belong to drywall without
  argument. But fire-rated coreboard at stairs and shaft liner sit on the line and land in the
  framing package often enough to need a row, and when a framer does carry shaft liner it may carry
  it on three sides of a four-sided shaft.
- Hoisting. Three separate seams. The framer supplies telehandlers and man lifts for its own work
  without argument. Mobile crane usually sits in the framer's base and is offered back as a deduct
  once the GC decides to supply one. Tower crane is excluded by nearly everyone, is priced by the
  GC off a crane vendor's own rate card, and is quoted with its own internal structure: fixed costs
  for assembly, dismantle, freight, foundation design and base setting; cyclic costs for monthly
  rental plus a standard operator month of roughly one hundred seventy-four hours with overtime on
  top; and optional costs for climbing, anti-collision, third-party inspection and weekend work. If
  a framer's bid mentions sharing tower crane cost among the subcontractors that use it, that is a
  cost allocation question the scope sheet needs to answer.
- Finish carpentry. The seam is trim blocking, and it is one-sided: the framer excludes blocking for
  running trim and finish attachment, and the finish carpenter arrives expecting it. Sill blocking
  after window installation is the same seam in a different location.
- Roofing. Three-party: the mechanical trade sets the unit, carpentry blocks the curb, the roofer
  flashes it, and each of the three excludes the other two. Roof anchor supports and davits are the
  same shape and worse, because their locations are often not shown at all.
- Masonry. Blocking and posts supporting brick relieving angles are excluded and priced back as an
  add; the steel angles themselves belong to steel; and the wood blocking outboard of the gypsum
  stays excluded even inside the add. Furring at masonry walls and alignment between dissimilar wall
  types is a further unclaimed strip.
- Insulation. Almost entirely excluded, with one carve-out worth knowing: a negotiated subcontract
  can oblige the framer to install insulation, material by others, in spaces its own framing is
  about to make inaccessible. That obligation appears in no bid.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What framers actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The structural drawings. This is the primary document, and it is where the conflict lives.
   Framers cite structural plans for stud sizes, sheathing thickness, shear wall locations, hold-down
   locations, headers, posts and connection hardware, and several will price the structural
   requirement over the architectural one where the two disagree.
2. The architectural wall, floor and roof assemblies. This is where sheathing thicknesses, fire
   ratings and layer counts come from, and it routinely disagrees with the structural sheets. A
   bidder disclosing which side it priced, assembly by assembly, is doing you a favour.
3. Wall type schedules and partition plans, for the wood-or-metal question on non-bearing walls.
4. Architectural details, individually and by number. This trade reads details more than any other:
   davit blocking details, brick support details, roof anchor details, screen wall details, curb
   details, window extension details, air sealing details. Where a detail is missing or has no plan
   cuts leading to it, the scope disappears from every bid at once.
5. Elevations and window type designations, for the window count and the mulled-unit configuration.
6. The specification, for treated material, sheathing grades, fastener schedules and warranty
   duration.
7. A consultant's air-sealing detail package, where one exists. It is not part of the architectural
   set, it defines what the air sealing work actually is, and it can require blocking at conditions
   the architectural set never shows.

They do not price from a component takeoff. One bid in the corpus was priced off a takeoff the GC
supplied, and it is the only one. The normal method is area type plus assembly type plus judgment,
converted to a lump sum.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of these moves the number:

- Union against open shop. The largest driver by a wide margin, and on one project it sorts the
  ranking outright with no open-shop bidder exceeding the lowest union bidder.
- The wage zone or local, which is geography-driven and changes for the same bidder between towns.
- Prevailing wage, federal wage requirements, and local residents jobs policies, all excluded unless
  named.
- Whether the walls are panelized or stick-built, and if panelized, whether the barrier, the
  sheathing, the window openings and the blocking ride on the panel.
- Which barrier system the specification will land on, because a fluid-applied system removes some
  bidders from window installation entirely.
- Whether non-bearing partitions will end up wood or metal.
- Lumber, truss and hardware market movement between quote and contract, and whether the contract
  carries an escalation clause. Quote validity is measured in days.
- Whether the GC supplies a tower crane, and if so how its cost is allocated among the trades
  that use it.
- Perimeter access and grading for telehandlers and boom lifts.
- Whether power is available at the working floors, at what interval, and how soon after a floor is
  sheathed.
- Sequencing between barrier installation, blocking and window installation, which one bidder makes
  an explicit GC coordination obligation because it changes the work.
- Whether steel stairs will be installed before framing starts, because if not the framer needs
  temporary stairs and will charge for them.
- Whether blocking locations and dimensions will be delivered before wall shop drawings start.
  This is a hard gate: full-height blocking that misses it does not happen in this package.
- Whether mechanical coordination will be complete before panel fabrication starts.
- Warranty duration required, insurance program participation, bond requirement, and portal fees.
- The floor release and turnover sequence, which drives remobilization for loading doors, hoist
  openings and elevator frame-in.
- Whether the project requires certified-sustainable lumber, which every bidder excludes.

Set-stage behavior. At design development a proposal will price off a supplied takeoff or off
square-foot budget numbers and say so, and a bidder will later explain that its number fell because
firm panel and truss supplier quotes replaced those budgets. Treat a design-development framing
number as a rate carry, not a bid. By permit and construction-document sets the proposals switch to
named assemblies, named details, and disclosed deviations. Note that a bid's stated basis and its
folder's set stage disagree often enough to check every time: a proposal in a permit-set round can
be priced on a design-development set three months older and say so only in a cover field.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet of wood-framed area. This is the leveling currency and it is worth being precise about
  the denominator, because it is neither gross square footage nor unit count. It is the area of the
  wood-framed portion of the building, which excludes the podium, the garage and any concrete or
  masonry structure. Cross-project rate comparison is meaningless without it. Every new-build
  estimator sheet in the corpus normalizes on it.
- Dwelling units, but only on a renovation where there is no wood framing to measure.
- Square feet of wall or partition area, for the non-bearing partition plug specifically.
- Linear feet, for screen walls and for swing staging.
- Each, for hold-downs, balconies by type, windows and exterior doors, temporary loading doors, pipe
  portals, stairs, and connection hardware.
- Risers, for stairs, seen once and useful.
- Hours, for the project carpenter plug, for blocking crews sized in crew-weeks, and for hourly
  change-order rates by role.
- Months, for tower crane and for the project carpenter duration.
- Lump sum for essentially everything a bidder prices.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, quoted at two to two and a half percent.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Labor type, union against open shop. On one project the three union bidders were the three most
   expensive and no open-shop bidder exceeded the cheapest union one. Open-shop hourly rates for
   carpenters, foremen and project managers run roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the union
   equivalents where both are stated.
2. Whether the bid is furnish-and-install or labor-only. A labor-only bid can be a third of a
   turnkey bid for the same physical work. This is a mode difference, not a price difference, and
   comparing across it is the trade's most common leveling error.
3. Whether non-bearing partitions are in, and in wood or metal. Worth roughly an eighth to a sixth
   of a base bid.
4. Total wood-framed area, and how much of the building is wood rather than podium, garage or
   masonry.
5. Lumber, truss and hardware commodity pricing at the moment of quote, and whether escalation risk
   sits with the bidder or the GC.
6. Whether window installation and its flashing are in. Commonly a sixth to a fifth of a framing
   base when carried, and it is the single largest detachable labor block after partitions.
7. Whether the air barrier is in, and whether it is shop-applied or field-applied.
8. Craneage, and specifically whether a tower crane is required and who carries it. A tower crane
   over a typical framing duration can approach a tenth of the framing base on its own.
9. Sheathing thickness, rating and product. Upgrading to a taped or fire-rated system, adding a
   second layer, or upgrading a floor panel at corridors and loading zones are each priced as
   discrete adds.
10. Fire-retardant treatment of studs, plates and sheathing, which is carried by some bidders as
    standard and excluded by others by name.
11. Blocking scope actually required against blocking scope drawn, which is the trade's largest
    hidden variable and the one this entry exists to warn about.
12. Access: perimeter clearance, staging, swing staging on a renovation, and available power.
13. Temporary work: stairs, loading doors, safety rails, remobilizations.
14. Bond, insurance program participation, warranty duration and portal fees.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within one project, cost per square foot of
wood-framed area among turnkey bidders spreads by a factor of about two to two and a half. Including
a labor-only bid the same list spreads by a factor of three and a half to seven. Bid lists run from
four bidders to thirteen. Across projects, a small two-building project drew four bidders whose
adjusted rates converged to within about a quarter of each other, while the two large projects drew
eleven and thirteen bidders with far wider spreads. Small and simple produces agreement; large and
detailed produces noise.

The most important pricing convention in this trade is that a base bid means very little on its own.
The estimator compares base bid plus adjustments, and the adjustments are enormous: on one project
they ranged from about five percent of base to about one hundred thirteen percent of base across
four bidders on a single trade, and the largest single adjustment on another project exceeded half
the base bid it was applied to. That is a different order of magnitude from most trades, and it is
driven by the fact that bidders package this scope so differently. A scope line set that pins the
labor-material axis, the partition question, the window question, the barrier question and the
blocking zones is doing most of that adjustment work in advance.

One more convention worth knowing. When a GC bid form asks this trade to break its number into labor,
lumber and trusses, it gets nothing. On one project every one of seven requested breakout rows came
back empty from all eleven bidders. Labor hours, by contrast, will be answered when asked directly:
several bidders will give a total and one will split framing from window installation. Ask for hours,
not for a cost breakdown.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This entry is calibrated on about two dozen rough carpentry proposals spanning four projects, four
estimator leveling sheets, one crane vendor rate card filed among the bids, and one negotiated
subcontract scope exhibit carrying the subcontractor's own redline comments. Three of the four
projects are new-build multifamily residential over a podium or slab, in one regional market, all
bid by one general contractor; the fourth is a renovation of an existing building with no wood
framing at all, which is why it appears here mainly as a contrast. The proposals span design
development, permit, construction-document and conformed sets. One package was followed end to end
through seven negotiation rounds over three months, which is where most of section 2 comes from. The
bidder pool includes several companies appearing on more than one project, which is what makes the
boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable, and it turned up two direct
competitors filing substantively the same document.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from wood-framed multifamily residential in one market through one general
  contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial, institutional, healthcare, industrial, or public
  prevailing-wage work, and nothing is tested against single-family or townhouse production framing,
  where the packaging conventions are likely to differ substantially.
- Only one negotiated subcontract is in the corpus, so the claim that the sill-blocking gap and the
  insulation-in-inaccessible-spaces obligation get bought at the subcontract stage rests on that
  single document. No change order history is in the corpus at all, so the claim that the material
  escalation and sequencing qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals'
  own language rather than observed in the field.
- The corpus contains no mass timber, cross-laminated timber, or heavy timber work, and no
  structural insulated panel systems. It contains no volumetric modular construction, which would
  change this package's boundaries fundamentally.
- Only one proposal was priced off a supplied takeoff and only one carried a material quantity list,
  so every quantity convention in section 7 that touches component counts rests on very thin
  evidence. Unit prices for framing work do not appear anywhere in the corpus; every rate in the
  cost record comes from the estimator's own plug assumptions rather than from a bidder.
- The corpus does not show what happens when the wood-or-metal partition decision is made before bid
  rather than during negotiation. On every project here it moved after bids were in.
- The corpus does not show a project where the air barrier was fluid-applied from the start, so the
  claim that a fluid-applied specification removes bidders from window installation rests on two
  bidders' stated policy rather than on an observed bid list.
- The corpus does not resolve where blocking outboard of the barrier is actually bought. It shows
  every framer excluding it, the siding side excluding it, the estimator carrying a scope row for it,
  and no bidder pricing it. What happens next is not in these documents.
- Nothing here is tested against a set where the structural and architectural assemblies agree.
  Every project in the corpus had at least one disclosed conflict between them, so the advice to
  capture which side a bidder priced may be over-weighted for cleaner document sets.
