# Flooring

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the flooring package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real flooring contractors can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The anchor is 09 6000, Flooring. Under it the market recognizes distinct sections that bidders name
and price separately: resilient flooring and accessories, carpeting, wood flooring, and tile. Some
bidders still write the older five-digit section numbers, which map to the same four families. Two
further sections sit at the edges and behave differently: resinous flooring, which is nearly always a
separate specialty bid, and gypsum underlayment or gypcrete, which is a separate trade entirely and
is discussed in section 5.

The package boundary is not stable, and this is the first thing to establish on any project. Four
shapes all occur, and more than one of them will usually appear inside a single bid list:

- Soft goods and hard goods together with ceramic and stone tile, bid as one number. Common, and it
  is the shape that produces the biggest proposals in the corpus.
- Finish flooring only, with ceramic tile and all its transitions explicitly excluded. Equally
  common, and the exclusion is usually the first line of the qualification list.
- A tile-first bidder that also carries resilient, carpet and sometimes interior thin brick. Occurs
  often enough that the trade token on a file tells you nothing about what the bid covers.
- A material supplier bidding supply and installation as two separately priced packages, or supply
  only.

Because the boundary moves, the estimator levels flooring and tile on separate sheets and splits any
bundled bid before ranking it. A scope list that does not draw the same line will be unrankable
against half the market. Where a bidder bundles, it will often quote insurance, bond and portal fees
separately for the ceramic half and the resilient half of its own number, which is the clearest signal
that the two halves are commercially distinct even inside one company.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or section:

- Wall base of every kind. Resilient cove base, sculpted profile base and rubber base come with the
  flooring package by default. Wood and MDF base does not, and is excluded by most bidders and picked
  up by carpentry, except when a supplier bids the whole finish package and offers MDF base as its own
  supply and install line.
- Wall tile, shower and tub surrounds, and kitchen backsplash, whenever the bidder is carrying tile
  at all. Wall tile is often the largest single number in a bundled flooring bid.
- Transitions, saddles, thresholds, reducers and edge trims. Carried by most bidders, sometimes
  enumerated one row per detail callout, and excluded outright by a minority. Unit entry thresholds
  specifically are contested: some carry them in the base, some exclude them by name, some price them
  as an add.
- Walk-off and entry mats. Usually carried, sometimes labor only over owner-supplied goods.
- Acoustic and sound underlayment. Discussed at length in section 5 because it is the largest single
  gap in the trade.
- Attic stock and maintenance stock. Carried by some as a percentage line, shown at zero by others,
  excluded outright by a few.
- Floor protection. Split roughly evenly between base bid, priced alternate and flat exclusion.
- Interior thin brick veneer at amenity walls. Priced by some tile-carrying bidders, excluded by
  others on the same project.
- Turf, rubber tile at fitness, and rubber stair treads and risers. Carried consistently when they
  appear.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like flooring:

- Resinous and epoxy flooring, and sealed or densified concrete. This is the trade's most contested
  boundary and it has its own subsection in section 5.
- Gypsum underlayment and gypcrete. A separate bid list with separate bidders, though one company
  appears on both sides.
- Moisture mitigation and vapor control systems. Excluded by nearly every bidder seen, and priced as
  an alternate by a few.
- Self-leveling and heavy floor prep. Excluded by every finish flooring bidder seen; the most any of
  them include is one skim coat or one hour of prep.
- Shower pans. Supplied and installed by others in most tile-carrying bids; the tile bidder tiles to
  the pan and may offer install-only as an alternate.
- Tile backer board and cement board. Excluded and offered only as an alternate.
- Solid surface, stone slab, pedestal pavers, elevator cab flooring, fireplace surrounds, reception
  desk concrete and acoustical wall panels. Named and excluded by the bidders that read the finish
  schedule closely.

## 2. How the market bids

Flooring is a mark-driven trade, and the discriminating fact about any proposal is not how many
numbers it carries but what the numbers are keyed to. Four enumeration grains occur:

- By finish schedule mark. The dominant grain. The proposal lists each mark, its product, and usually
  a quantity, grouped by area. This is what most quantified bids do and it is the grain estimator
  sheets adopt.
- By material family. Resilient, carpet, base, rubber, wood, tile, each one number. The second most
  common shape and the default for the fast bidders.
- By unit type. Rare and valuable: each plan type with its unit count and its square footage, main
  living separated from bathrooms. One bid in the corpus does this, and it is the only document that
  supplies the per-unit-type denominator everyone else's number implies.
- One number for the whole scope, with no grain at all. The degenerate case, seen on single-family
  scopes such as unit wood flooring.

Quantities are far more available in this trade than in most. A majority of proposals state square
footages, linear footages, square yards or counts against their marks, and a meaningful minority state
unit prices as well. That does not mean the quantities agree. Bidders' own square footages for the
same rooms differ by tens of percent, and one specialty bidder's quantity for the same two rooms moved
by three fifths between two rounds of its own proposal. Read the quantity basis out of each proposal;
never assume a shared one.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The named product per mark, usually with manufacturer, collection, colour code and size. Nearly
  always, and often with vendor part numbers.
- The drawing set date it priced from, and frequently the specific finish sheets, unit finish
  schedule and interior-design finish schedule by number.
- Sales tax treatment, either included in the number or shown as its own line.
- Regular working hours, defined by clock time, with premium and double time defined.
- An assumption block covering free use of hoists and elevators, locking on-site storage, water,
  electricity, lighting and cleanup to a central location or a general contractor's dumpster.
- Labor type, union or open shop, stated by many, and recorded by an estimator for every bidder
  whether stated or not.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- The subfloor condition it assumes, beyond a generic requirement that floors arrive smooth, level,
  dry and clean. Only a minority state a numeric flatness tolerance or name the industry standard for
  concrete floor evaluation.
- Whether acoustic underlayment is in or out, unless asked. Silence here is the most expensive silence
  in the trade.
- Attic stock, unless the specification is quoted back.
- Warranty duration. Where stated it is one year on installation workmanship, occasionally two on
  labor, and the carve-outs (product defects, moisture failures, subfloor movement, damage by others)
  are spelled out only rarely.
- Insurance basis, bond cost and project-portal fees. Supplied on request by email, usually as a
  percentage of contract for the bond.
- Mockup and moisture-test costs. A minority price them as named unit costs, which is exactly what
  lets an estimator plug them on everyone else.

Freight and tariff behave as first-class commercial lines here, more so than in most finish trades.
Freight appears as its own priced line in several proposals, in one case a separate line per material
vendor. Tariff appears three ways: a clause disclaiming escalation between award and material order, a
shortened price validity window stated as sixty days because of new tariffs, and, from an importing
supplier, a statement on the summary page that prices are based on current tariff rates as of the
quote date and subject to change with future government action. An importing supplier will also carry
staged payment terms with a large deposit before production, container unloading pushed to the buyer,
and monthly warehousing rates per container if the project slips.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs:

- A revision that moves no money in the base and exists only to attach a substitution menu. Common.
  Treat a revision label as no evidence that base scope changed.
- A revision that moves price on some material families and not others, with no reason documented and
  sometimes the same proposal date and number as the prior round, so nothing inside the document marks
  it as a revision at all.
- A revision that adds a large scope block previously excluded, most often acoustic underlayment. This
  is the one that matters, and the added block can be worth a fifth of the original total.
- A revision that restructures the package: tile dropped, mode changed from bundled to flooring only,
  prep and moisture and protection broken out from prose into priced alternates. The number falls
  sharply and none of the fall is a price reduction.
- A revision rebased on a later drawing set, where categories previously shown at zero become priced.
  The earlier number was never a like-for-like bid.

A filename that says a document is a value-engineering version may instead be the same proposal
re-issued against a different quantity basis, and a proposal filed twice under sibling names may be
one document, not two rounds. Open the file.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Flooring scope sheets in real leveling have run 38, 38, 49 and 49 rows, of which roughly 24 to 33 are
actual scope rows and the rest are allowance, alternate, value-engineering and qualification blocks.
Target roughly forty to sixty scope lines for a full multifamily flooring package, and expect a
separate sheet of comparable size for tile if tile is a separate package. Fewer than about twenty-five
and the sheet cannot separate a bidder that carries underlayment, prep and protection from one that
carries none of them, which is the difference that decides the award.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Area, as the top-level axis: residential units, corridors, amenity and front of house, back of
  house, egress and back-of-house stairs, garage, lobby and vestibule, retail. Every estimator sheet
  uses this axis first and most proposals adopt it.
- Every named finish mark, within its area. Each resilient plank mark, each carpet mark, each rubber
  mark, each wood mark, each vinyl composite mark, each turf mark, each base mark, each transition and
  trim mark, each threshold and saddle mark, each concrete and resinous mark. This is the grain the
  quantified proposals and the leveling sheets both use, and a mark range is a legitimate way to write
  a line only when the whole range shares one product and one installation.
- Base separately from flooring, and each base mark separately, because base is where the seams are:
  resilient base, sculpted profile base, metal base, tile base and wood base are answered differently
  by different bidders and some exclude base within units while carrying it in common areas.
- Transitions and thresholds as their own group, broken at least into unit entry thresholds, interior
  transitions and reducers, and tile edge trim. Unit entry thresholds in particular are answered three
  different ways across a single bid list.
- Acoustic and sound underlayment, its own line, named by product and by where it applies, and split
  by area if the drawings apply it selectively. Never a sub-attribute of a flooring line.
- Floor preparation, split into at least four lines because bidders answer them separately: minor prep
  or skim coat, self-leveling over concrete, prep at unit entry thresholds, and prep over gypcrete.
- Moisture testing and moisture mitigation as two separate lines, and mitigation split by substrate
  where the drawings distinguish slab on grade from elevated cast-in-place slab. The two substrates
  carry very different exposures.
- Gypcrete sealing, its own line, with the furnish and the install sides named separately.
- Floor protection, its own line, and worth splitting by finish type because bidders name a different
  protection product for resilient, for tile and for carpet.
- Waterproofing and crack suppression membrane, split into floor and wall, and split again by where
  the schedule applies it, because one bidder may carry it only at a specific bathroom group per a
  drawing note.
- Attic stock and maintenance stock, its own line, with the specified percentage stated.
- Mockups and moisture tests, their own lines, because a few bidders price them and the rest need a
  plug.
- Hoisting, mobilization and cleanup, their own lines. One bidder prices hoisting as a distinct
  number, another charges a stated fee per mobilization for delays caused by others, and a third
  carries a mobilization line inside every area subtotal.
- Commercial terms as scope rows: sales tax, labor type, owner-controlled insurance program versus
  conventional general liability, warranty duration, payment and performance bond, project-portal fee.
  Every estimator sheet carries these as rows and levels on them.
- Wall tile, floor tile, backsplash and shower and tub surrounds, each its own line with its stated
  height, whenever tile is in the package. The height of the surround, and whether it runs to the
  ceiling, is worth its own alternate.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Room-by-room enumeration collapses to area plus mark. No document in the corpus prices a single
  residential room, though a bidder will name individual amenity rooms when the mark changes.
- Levels collapse into a level range unless the finish or the substrate actually changes. Where a
  substrate changes at a level line, as when the lower levels are concrete and the upper levels are
  gypcrete over wood framing, that break is real and both sides need their own prep and moisture rows.
- Adhesive, thinset, grout, caulk, patching compound, weld rod and fasteners are attributes of a line,
  not lines. One bidder prices each of them as a row, but everyone else buries them, so enumerating
  them makes the sheet incomparable.
- Individual unit counts collapse into a unit-type line or a whole-units line. The unit count is the
  denominator, not a row.
- Colour, thickness, wear layer and installation method are attributes of a mark, not marks, but they
  are load-bearing attributes: state them on the line, because they are the whole substance of the
  value-engineering exercise described in section 7.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
flooring, the test fires hardest on the seams in section 5 and on every substrate-preparation item,
which is why prep gets four rows rather than one.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. Watch for the mark that appears on a proposal with
no price against it. That is neither an inclusion nor an exclusion; it is a bidder telling you it saw
the mark and did not price it, and it will read as included to anyone skimming the total. A bid that
lists sealed concrete and epoxy marks at zero is not carrying them.

### The trade's structural gap list

These are the plugs an estimator adds to every bidder before comparing, because the market
systematically will not carry them. A scope list that names all of them is doing the plugging in
advance:

1. Floor leveling and self-leveling over concrete. Carried on every bidder as a square-foot allowance
   on one project, and it is the largest single plug in the trade.
2. Floor preparation, whether budgeted per unit, per floor or per square foot.
3. Moisture testing and moisture mitigation, separately, and separately by substrate.
4. Acoustic and sound underlayment.
5. Gypcrete sealing, install side.
6. Floor protection.
7. Mockups.
8. Attic stock.
9. Hoisting.
10. General liability if no owner-controlled program applies, payment and performance bond, and the
    project-portal fee.
11. Resinous flooring and sealed concrete, which usually route to another sub rather than being
    plugged.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the trade that must pick the work up, and if
no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| Excluded by the flooring contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Subfloor preparation and leveling (excluded by every finish flooring bidder seen; a skim coat or one hour of prep is the most any include) | General contractor, or a plug |
| Moisture testing and moisture mitigation or pH correction (nearly every bidder) | General contractor, an independent testing agency, or unassigned |
| Protection of finish flooring (about half; the other half price it as an alternate or carry it) | General contractor, or a plug |
| Final cleaning, washing, waxing, sealing and vacuuming of the finished floor (nearly every bidder) | General contractor or final cleaning |
| Ceramic tile and all associated transitions and trims (about half of bidders, being the flooring-only half) | The tile package |
| Sealed concrete and resinous flooring with their trims and transitions (commonly) | The resinous specialty, or painting; see section 5 |
| Sound or acoustic mat and the whole subfloor assembly (commonly) | The underlayment trade, or unassigned and expensive |
| Gypcrete sealer, supply and install (some bidders exclude both; others install with the sealer furnished by the gypcrete contractor) | Gypcrete contractor furnishes; installer contested |
| Underlayment, primers and tile backer board (commonly) | Frequently unassigned; backer board offered as an alternate |
| Wood base and base within units (some bidders) | Carpentry, or the finish supplier |
| Metal wall base (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned |
| Preformed resilient base corners, with field-formed corners only (a repeat bidder, on every project) | Absorbed, or a quality concession |
| Shower pans, supply and install (most tile-carrying bidders) | Plumbing or the pan manufacturer |
| Demolition, removal of existing flooring, adhesives, curing compounds and contaminants (commonly) | General contractor or demolition |
| Expansion and control joint treatments (a minority) | Frequently unassigned |
| Entry and walk-off mats (a minority) | Frequently unassigned |
| Unit entry thresholds (a minority; others carry them, others price them as an add) | Contested |
| Elevator cab flooring (one bidder names it) | Elevator contractor |
| Interior thin brick veneer (some tile-carrying bidders exclude it; others price it) | Contested inside this package |
| Named schedule marks the bidder will not do: fireplace surrounds, reception desk concrete, solid surface backsplashes, decorative metal base, elevator metal, pedestal pavers, acoustical wall panels, fiberglass reinforced panels, wood wallcoverings and mouldings (one bidder names eleven) | Millwork, stone, specialties, or unassigned |
| Night and weekend work, premium and overtime (nearly every bidder) | Priced as an extra |
| Dumpster and debris removal beyond a central location (commonly) | General contractor |
| Prevailing wage rates (some bidders, always as a negative) | Not applicable, or repriced |
| Moving furniture and fixtures, handling of fixtures (commonly) | General contractor |
| Layout and surveying of substrate for leveling or drainage (a minority) | Frequently unassigned |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

A repeat bidder reproduces the same paragraph block on every project and every round, byte for byte.
In this corpus one bidder's standing terms are identical across four projects and three set stages, so
their presence carries no project information at all. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the
project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. A bidder excluding carpet at back-of-house
  stairs because none is called out there is reporting a reading of the finish plan, and an estimator
  may carry the exclusion rather than plug it.
- An exclusion naming a specific finish mark, sheet or detail number. When a bidder writes out marks
  it will not do, it read the schedule and found something outside its scope.
- A qualification that names a schedule note. A bidder carrying waterproofing only at one bathroom
  group per a plan note has read further into the documents than the bidders who carried it everywhere
  or nowhere.
- A stated substitute product carried in the base without being asked. This is a deviation disclosure,
  not an alternate, and it is discussed below.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a trash room with no finishes, a vestibule
  pending finish selection, an air-handler closet qualified to the unit spec.

### Deviations, which are neither exclusions nor alternates

The highest-value paragraph in a flooring proposal is often the one disclosing that the base bid is not
on the specified product. Three forms occur and all are common enough to look for:

- The base bid is priced on the bidder's own brand and the specified product is offered only as a
  priced add. On one project this add was worth a large fraction of the unit flooring number, so the
  apparently low bidder was low because it had quietly changed the product.
- A named substitute is disclosed in a single line, as when a bidder states which manufacturer and line
  it carried for a vinyl composite tile mark.
- The bid is a manufacturer's own product offered as an alternate to the specified flooring, stated in
  the first sentence, so the entire proposal is a deviation.

An importing supplier may deviate on material type rather than brand, pricing engineered wood where
the schedule shows a resilient plank, or MDF base where the schedule shows a resilient base. Read the
product description on every line against the schedule; do not read only the total.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts a lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Floors are received flat, clean, dry and free of debris and old adhesive. Universal, and it is the
  qualification that hands every prep dispute back to the general contractor.
- A stated flatness tolerance, a quarter inch in ten feet in the one bid that names one. Rare and
  worth asking for, because without it the previous line is unenforceable in both directions.
- The distinction between flat and level, which one bidder states outright: self-leveling does not
  mean the floor will be level, it is defined as flat. This single sentence decides who pays for
  ramping to a level plane.
- Slab testing performed by the customer to the named vapor-emission and relative-humidity standards,
  with written results delivered before start, including a list of any sealers already applied to the
  slab. Some bidders make this a condition; a carpet-industry standard requiring the owner or general
  contractor to submit a written moisture and alkalinity report is cited by others.
- New concrete not to be over-troweled or burnished, with burnished slabs drawing additional prep
  cost. A direct constraint on the concrete contractor that no drawing carries.
- Free use of hoists, elevators, locking on-site storage, water, electricity and adequate lighting.
  Near-universal.
- Finish lighting installed and functional to reflect finished room conditions, and the HVAC system
  operational before flooring install. Stated by a minority and both are schedule constraints hidden
  in a materials note.
- Additional floor preparation billed at a stated rate per man hour plus materials. Common enough that
  the hourly rate is worth capturing.
- Price validity, usually thirty days, shortened to sixty days explicitly because of tariffs by one
  bidder and stated with a manufacturer-increase pass-through by another.
- Material deposits billed in the following requisition, or, from an importing supplier, a staged
  payment schedule with a large deposit before production starts.
- Union or open shop, and on one project a bidder states that all ceramic and stone tile on floors and
  walls will be installed by a named floorcoverers local of the carpenters council, which is a
  jurisdiction statement rather than a wage statement.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The subfloor seam, which is where this trade loses money

Flooring is a finish applied to somebody else's substrate, and almost every dispute in the trade lives
at that handoff. The assembly under the finish is built by two or three other trades, and no flooring
bidder carries any of it:

- Gypsum underlayment or gypcrete is a separate trade with its own bid list. Flooring bidders exclude
  the subfloor assembly wholesale, naming gypcrete and sound mat together.
- Gypcrete sealing is the seam with a name. The pattern the market has settled on is that the gypcrete
  contractor furnishes the sealer and the flooring contractor installs it, immediately before its own
  work, sometimes stated as twenty-four hours prior. Two bidders on one project both priced it that
  way, as a labor-only add; a third excluded both supply and install. It appears on the estimator's
  sheet as its own row on more than one project. Because it is a labor-only add on a large square
  footage, it moves real money and no drawing shows it.
- Moisture is the second named seam. Flooring excludes mitigation and prices it as an alternate at
  best; the exposure differs between slab on grade and elevated cast-in-place slab and an estimator
  will carry a plug for each. One bidder refuses to install over a named moisture-mitigating concrete
  admixture at all, which makes a concrete-mix decision into a flooring exclusion.
- Slab flatness and finish. The bidder receives the floor flat, not level, and a burnished or
  over-troweled slab costs extra. This is a concrete-trade decision with a flooring price attached.
- Contaminants. Old adhesive, patch, curing compounds, paint and joint compound must be mechanically
  removed by others before the flooring contractor starts.

### Acoustic underlayment, the largest single gap

Sound underlayment sits between the subfloor and the finish and belongs to nobody by default. In the
corpus it appears in every possible commercial position within a single set of projects: excluded
outright by a bidder that later added it as its own priced category worth roughly a fifth of its
original total; carried under all flooring except a mark that has an attached pad, by a bidder that
said so in one sentence; offered as an alternate at a specific level only; excluded on an estimator's
sheet with a standing note that the mat between the gypcrete and the finish is not in this contract;
and named as an exclusion by product. It is also the item the estimator most often has to price twice,
recording two different bidders' worths for it and plugging the higher.

Give it its own line, name the product and the thickness, name the areas, and ask explicitly. A
flooring bid silent on acoustic underlayment is not comparable to one that carries it.

### Sealed concrete and resinous flooring, contested with painting

This is the boundary the painting package describes from the other side. Painting's file records
concrete floor sealer and epoxy floor coatings at back-of-house rooms as items some painters carry,
more exclude, and an estimator noted as carried in the flooring package instead. The flooring corpus
shows the same contest from this side, resolved four different ways within one bid list:

- One flooring bidder carries both sealed concrete and the resinous mark in its base bid.
- Another lists both marks as rows with a zero price, which reads as seen but not carried.
- A third excludes all resinous flooring and sealed concrete together with their trims and
  transitions, in one sentence.
- A fourth excludes concrete finish marks by name in a list of eleven marks it will not do.

On the estimator's side, the rows for sealed concrete and epoxy floor carry a routing value pointing at
another sub, and on one project the resinous row was moved out of the flooring tab into its own
package mid-evaluation. So the answer is that it is a genuinely separate specialty that sometimes
collapses into flooring and sometimes into painting, and the scope list has to name it explicitly and
say where it goes rather than leaving it inside either package.

The specialty bidders themselves are a distinct market with distinct habits, and this matters because
they price conditions rather than scope:

- The whole document may be a conditions sheet with no scope description at all.
- The quantity basis is stated in the header by some and inferred by others, and the same rooms drew
  square footages that differed by more than half across four bidders. Cove base height differed too,
  four inches from one bidder and six from another, on the same rooms. An estimator normalizes by
  writing each bidder's quantity beside its number and computing a rate per square foot.
- Prep is shot blasting or diamond grinding, and it is included. Patching is not: the general
  contractor is told to carry a patching allowance tracked on time and material. Joint fill is quoted
  per linear foot separately.
- Mobilization is priced per mobilization, and additional phases are extra.
- Temporary power is a scope item. Specific circuits are demanded for prep and mixing equipment, and
  connection and disconnection are the general contractor's.
- Slab cure time of twenty-eight days, minimum slab temperature of fifty-five degrees with heat
  supplied by others, and a trade-free work area for the duration of installation and cure.
- Moisture thresholds are stated numerically against named test methods, which the finish flooring
  bidders never do.
- Protection during cure is the general contractor's problem, and damage from foot traffic or spills
  during cure is not the applicator's cost. Some specialty bidders include protection in their base,
  which the finish flooring bidders almost never do.
- A cancellation restocking charge, commonly around a fifth of material cost, and materials charged if
  the job is postponed inside a short window of the agreed start.

### Other seams

- Base and painting. Where a supplier furnishes MDF or pine base primed, somebody has to finish it,
  and that is the painter. Where the base is resilient or rubber it is a flooring product and nobody
  paints it. A scope list that says only "base" leaves the finishing question open.
- Base and carpentry. Wood base is excluded by most flooring bidders and belongs to carpentry, but one
  bidder excludes base within units while carrying it in common areas, and another quotes millwork base
  in unit bathrooms as part of a flooring add. Name the base by area and by material.
- Owner-furnished goods. Carpet is the family this happens to. When the owner furnishes, the
  configuration changes shape: the bidder prices labor and adhesive only, and the estimator's sheet
  grows a row for receive, inventory and store. A deduct for the owner-furnished option may be offered
  against a base bid that assumed supply and install, so the two are directly comparable only if that
  deduct is captured.
- Tile and the wet trades. The tile-carrying bidder tiles to a shower pan supplied and installed by
  others, waterproofs to a named membrane, and may exclude shower niches on the ground that none are
  specified. Backer board is an alternate. Where devices and fixtures do not sit flush on an uneven
  tile, one bidder warns of extra labor to be discussed at layout, which is a plumbing and electrical
  coordination cost landing in the tile line.
- Flooring under casework and appliances. One estimator sheet asks whether flooring runs under all
  casework and millwork, and one supplier states its unit scope includes the kitchen under appliances
  but not under cabinets. That distinction is invisible on a finish plan and worth several thousand
  square feet on a large project.
- Elevator cabs. Excluded explicitly by one bidder and unmentioned by everyone else, which makes it a
  likely gap.
- Interior thin brick. Priced inside the flooring and tile bid by one bidder, excluded by two others on
  the same project, and value-engineered to ceramic tile by a fourth. Beware of the same item being
  claimed by two trades or by neither.
- Stair finishes. Treads, risers, tactile warning strips, landings, nosings and stringer material are
  answered inconsistently: one bidder carries treads but excludes the tactile strip, another prices
  stringer material as an add, a third did not carry stair finishes at all and quoted a rate per tread
  when asked.
- Hoisting and access. Priced as its own line by one bidder and assumed free by most. On a tall
  building with a single hoist this is a real number.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

This is the section the drawings cannot cover, and it is the reason a scope line has to carry more than
a surface and a quantity.

What flooring contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The finish schedule and the finish legend, unit and common area kept separate. This is the primary
   document. Every mark comes from here, and most proposals restate the marks with their products.
   Some bidders bind their own marked-up finish plans into the proposal.
2. The unit count and the unit mix. Every estimator sheet normalizes every bid to a cost per
   residential unit and ranks on it, so unit count is the leveling currency of this trade. One supplier
   computes its own average cost per unit for every package inside its proposal. If the set does not
   make the unit count and the unit-type mix unambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable.
3. The transitions and details sheet, for thresholds, saddles, reducers, edge trim and mud-set details.
   One bidder enumerates a row per detail callout and another excludes a specific mud-set detail by
   number.
4. Area and level organization, for the unit, corridor, amenity and back-of-house segmentation.
5. The specification, for coat systems on resinous work, for attic stock percentages, and for
   protection requirements cited by paragraph number.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- What is under the finish, and who built it. Gypcrete over wood framing on the upper levels and
  concrete slab below is the normal multifamily case, and the two demand different prep, different
  moisture treatment and different sealing.
- Whether the slab will arrive flat, and to what tolerance, and whether it will be burnished.
- Whether moisture testing has been done, by whom, and what the results were.
- Whether acoustic underlayment is required, at what thickness, and in which areas.
- Whether floor protection is required, for how long, and over which finishes.
- Union versus open shop, which the estimator tracks on every bidder. On one project every bidder was
  open shop and the field carried no spread at all; on others it is the largest structural difference
  between bids.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies. Base bids are commonly instructed to assume
  one, with an add alternate for conventional general liability if the owner does not provide it, and
  one supplier shows it as a credit rather than an add.
- Bond requirement and whether the bond runs through the warranty period. Quoted as a percentage of
  contract, in the range of one and a half to two percent where stated.
- Warranty duration. One year on installation workmanship is the market default; two years is
  sometimes the base with no cost difference, and beyond two years costs more.
- The project-portal or payment-application fee, quoted as a small percentage of contract with a cap,
  plus a flat amount per sub-tier sub.
- Whether the material is imported, and what the tariff exposure is. This is a first-class commercial
  question in this trade, not a footnote.
- Lead times, split into production weeks and freight weeks by importing suppliers, commonly four to
  six weeks each, with production starting only after approved fabrication drawings and a deposit.
- Whether attic stock is required and at what percentage.
- Whether a mockup unit is expected.
- The floor release sequence and whether the area can be closed to other trades during install and
  cure.
- Site access, elevator and hoist availability, and locking on-site storage.

Set-stage behavior. At design development the trade prices against material allowances rather than
products: a whole tile package quoted as an allowance per square foot for floor tile and per linear
foot for base, against marks whose product is to be determined, is the design-development default and
appears from more than one bidder. Value-engineering schedules at that stage are written as allowance
reductions naming a thickness and a wear layer rather than a product. By permit and construction
documents the same bidders switch to named products, part numbers and counted quantities. Treat a
design-development flooring line as an allowance carry, not a quantity, and capture the allowance rate
because it is the thing that will be renegotiated.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet for resilient, wood, rubber, tile, turf and protection. The dominant unit, and the one
  most unit prices are quoted in.
- Square yards for carpet, both broadloom and tile. Carpet is quoted in square yards while the drawings
  and the general contractor think in square feet, and a bid may state carpet in either.
- Linear feet for base, transitions, reducers, edge trim, cove base and joint fill.
- Each for thresholds, saddles, stair treads, showers, tubs, unit entries, air-handler closets and
  walk-off mats.
- Each per residential unit for prep allowances, thresholds and backsplashes.
- Residential units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator sheet.
- Percent of contract value for bond and for the project-portal fee.
- Percent of material for attic stock, stated as one, two or three percent depending on the
  specification and the family.
- Percent waste, stated per line by importing suppliers: around five percent in repetitive unit work
  and ten to fifteen percent in common areas and tile.
- Coverage per material kit, on resinous work, where the system is priced by how far a kit goes rather
  than by finished square foot.
- Hours, for additional floor preparation and time-and-material work, at a stated rate per man hour.
- Bags and hours per unit, or bags and hours per floor, as the basis for a prep budget.

Cost drivers, and in this trade the commercial-shape questions outrank the price variables:

1. Where the package boundary is drawn. Whether tile is in or out changes the number by more than any
   product decision, and a bid that dropped tile between rounds fell by roughly two fifths without any
   price moving.
2. Whether prep, leveling, moisture mitigation, acoustic underlayment, gypcrete sealing and protection
   are in the base or excluded. Taken together these are worth a large fraction of a base bid: on one
   project the self-leveling alternate alone was about eighteen percent of the flooring base and the
   moisture-mitigation alternate about nine percent, and on another the acoustic underlayment was about
   a fifth.
3. Supply versus install. Where a supplier splits them, the two halves are separately rankable and the
   supply half alone will look like a winning bid until someone notices half the scope is missing.
4. The unit flooring product: material type, thickness and wear layer. Substituting a resilient plank
   for engineered wood removed roughly four fifths of the unit flooring cost on a wood-floored project.
   Within resilient, dropping the wear layer from eight mil to six is the standard first value-
   engineering move and is defended on the ground that six is standard for rental and eight is
   over-specified for non-owner-occupied units.
5. Whether the specified product is actually being carried. See the deviations discussion in section 4.
6. Total finished area, driven by unit count and by how much amenity and common area the project
   carries.
7. Labor type, union versus open shop, tracked on every bidder in every leveling.
8. Tariff and freight exposure on imported material.
9. Wall tile height, and whether surrounds run full height to the ceiling and onto the face of the
   tub. These are the standard tile add alternates and each is worth real money.
10. Substrate mix, and how much of the floor is concrete rather than gypcrete.
11. Attic stock percentage.
12. Off hours, overtime, mobilization count and phasing.
13. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between lowest
and highest cost per residential unit has run about 1.36 times, about 1.7 times, about 2.1 times and
about 2.7 times, and it widens further when a supply-only supplier is left in the ranking. Bidder
counts per project run from six to eight, which is a much narrower list than the painting trade
carries. The selected sub was not the low bidder on any of the four projects. Cost per unit is not
comparable across projects unless the package boundary is the same: the same denominator produced
figures roughly twice as high on projects where tile and base were bundled in as on one where flooring
was levelled alone.

One more pricing convention worth knowing: an estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base
bid plus adjustments, where adjustments are plugs added to every bidder to cover what the market
systematically will not carry, and in this trade those adjustments have run from nothing to roughly
forty-five percent of a base bid. The plug list is the eleven items at the end of section 3. An
estimator also carries a leveling reserve visibly, entering a floor-leveling allowance at a rate per
square foot on every bidder and then crediting part of it back on a second line, so the reserve stays
on the sheet without being double counted.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly forty flooring-package proposals plus four estimator leveling
sheets and one separate specialty leveling tab, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one
regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The proposals span design development, permit,
construction documents and conformed sets. Several bidders appear on more than one project, which is
what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable, and the set
includes seven same-bidder revision pairs or triples, one full superseded bid round, and five
boundary-specialty bids for resinous flooring, epoxy and sealed concrete.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back-of-house space, in one
  market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors,
  institutional, healthcare, hospitality, education, industrial or public prevailing-wage work.
- No project in the corpus was a renovation of an existing floor, so demolition, take-up of existing
  flooring, adhesive and contaminant removal and asbestos abatement appear only as exclusions and never
  as priced scope. The whole existing-conditions block is untested.
- The gypsum underlayment and gypcrete package sits in the corpus but was read only where it touches
  this seam. The furnish-and-install split on the sealer, and who actually carries the leveling
  reserve, rest on what the flooring bidders and the estimator say about it rather than on the
  underlayment bids themselves.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is present, so nothing here shows which exclusions survived
  negotiation, whether the deviations disclosed in the base bids were accepted, or how the acoustic
  underlayment and gypcrete sealing questions actually resolved.
- No change order history is present. The claim that the prep, flatness and sequencing qualifications
  convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed in the
  field, so treat it as untested here.
- The corpus has not shown terrazzo, poured seamless flooring outside the resinous specialty, raised
  access flooring, athletic or sports flooring beyond a fitness rubber tile and a turf roll, static
  control flooring, or exterior pedestal paver systems, all of which attach to this package in other
  building types.
- The corpus has not shown a project with no acoustic requirement at all, so it does not reveal what
  the package looks like when the underlayment question does not arise.
- The corpus has not shown carpet stretched over pad, only carpet tile and one broadloom
  value-engineering option, so the pad, tackless and seaming conventions of broadloom work are absent.
- Attic stock percentages are visible only where a bidder quoted the specification back; the corpus
  does not reveal what a typical specification actually requires.
