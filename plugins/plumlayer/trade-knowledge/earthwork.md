# Earthwork and Sitework

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the earthwork and
sitework package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling
sheets. This file speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the
words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. This is the package where the drawings show you the least of
what the number is made of. The single largest variable in an earthwork bid is what class of material
the soil turns out to be and where it is allowed to go, and no drawing states either one.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Division 31 is the anchor and the package is commonly called earthwork or sitework interchangeably by
the same bidders on the same projects. The trade token in a filing system is close to meaningless
here: the same company, on the same job, gets filed as earthwork, sitework, site, export, test pits
and utility depending on which document arrived. Establish what a document actually prices by opening
it, never by its label.

The package that reaches the market is normally one number covering everything from the erosion
control sock to the top course of asphalt. Inside that one number sit at least a dozen operations
that other markets buy separately: erosion control, clearing and grubbing, site demolition, cut and
cap of existing services at the street, bulk excavation, the earthwork balance, foundation excavation
and backfill, excavation and backfill for the under-slab trades, aggregate bases, site drainage,
sewer, water, gas, electric and telecom duct bank, site lighting bases, curbing, paving, striping and
signage. Which of those the bidder actually carries varies enormously and is the main reason two
numbers on the same job differ.

Two packaging regimes exist under one division, and the first thing to establish is which one you are
scoping.

Greenfield or open-site work is the ordinary case: the earthwork balance is the spine of the bid, the
number is dominated by cut, fill, export and import, and the enumeration follows scope families that
look like the civil drawing set. Bidders can carry a hundred quantified lines.

Constrained urban work is a different trade wearing the same name. There is no earthwork balance to
speak of because there is nowhere to put anything, the number is dominated by the street work and the
seams with support of excavation, underpinning and demolition, and a bidder can price the whole thing
on one page. The organizing question stops being how much dirt moves and becomes who touches the
excavation, in what order, and what holds the neighbor up while it happens.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another spec section:

- Site utilities in their entirety, including sewer, water, drain, gas, electric and telecom duct
  bank. This is the default and it is why an earthwork proposal reads like three trades stapled
  together.
- Cut and cap of existing services at the street main. The demolition package excludes this almost
  universally and it lands here, priced by service, with crew days, police detail days and municipal
  permit allowances.
- Paving, curbing, pavement markings and traffic signage. Usually carried, occasionally split out to
  a paving specialist, and the striping is the piece most likely to fall out on its own.
- Precast drainage and sewer structures, transformer pads, switchgear pads and light pole bases.
  Bought as material from a precaster and set by this package.
- Local dewatering. Almost always included, and almost always bounded to open sump pumping with
  onsite discharge, which is a much smaller thing than the word suggests. See section 5.
- Erosion control, construction entrance, street sweeping and dust control.
- Construction management plan hardware on urban sites: jersey barrier, fence on top of barrier,
  gates, impact attenuators, temporary striping and signage. Sometimes carried with a stated
  allowance, sometimes excluded wholesale.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like earthwork:

- Support of excavation. Bid by engineered-foundation specialists as its own package, and excluded by
  most earthwork bidders. A minority of larger sitework contractors carry it inside their base bid,
  and when they do their number is not comparable to anyone else's until it is stripped out.
- Ground improvement, meaning aggregate piers and similar. Its own bidder pool, its own package, and
  its own normalizing denominator. Earthwork's role is support work, and even that is contested.
- Underpinning. Excluded by nearly everyone. It comes back from the same specialists who do support
  of excavation, or occasionally as a separately priced line from an earthwork bidder who has
  retained an engineer.
- Engineered dewatering. Wellpoints, deep wells, groundwater treatment and the discharge permit are a
  separate specialty package. Everything past a sump pump leaves.
- Soil transport and disposal, where the volume justifies it. A soil export broker sells trucking and
  tipping as a rate card and takes no earthwork risk at all.
- Hazardous material handling, soil testing and characterization, and licensed environmental
  professional services. Excluded by every bidder seen without exception.
- Site concrete, meaning sidewalks, pads, flatwork and cast-in-place site structures. Almost every
  earthwork bidder prepares the base and excludes the concrete. Where the package boundary sits can
  be set by union jurisdiction rather than by the drawings.
- Landscaping, planting soils, loam placement and irrigation. Earthwork strips and sometimes
  stockpiles the topsoil, and a landscaper places it.
- Hardscape pavers and specialty stone. Bought from a hardscape specialist by product, colour and
  laying pattern. Earthwork provides the base under it and typically excludes the setting bed.

## 2. How the market bids

There is no default proposal shape in this trade. The same package attracts documents that are three
orders of magnitude apart in detail, and the shape a bidder chooses is itself a strong signal about
how the number was built. Five shapes recur.

The one-page flat list. A short scope in dashes, one number, one dense exclusion paragraph, and a few
lines of house commercial terms. Common on constrained urban work and from utility-focused
contractors. It reads as a small scope and can be a large one.

The scope-family narrative. A page or two of paragraphs headed by scope family, one number, then a
long itemized exclusion list. The most common shape in the trade. Quantities appear only where the
bidder wants to disclose an assumption.

The numbered operation list. Thirty to fifty numbered operations, each a sentence, then a long
exclusion list. The clearest of the prose shapes because the seam is usually stated inside the
sentence rather than left to the exclusions.

The quantified line list. Roughly a hundred priced lines in scope families, every line with a quantity
and a unit. The most valuable document this trade produces and the one that makes a leveling sheet
possible. Expect it from a minority of bidders.

The internal cost estimate. Some bidders submit their own resource-level build-up: named equipment
models with hourly rates, labor classifications with hourly rates, materials with unit costs, crew
hours per operation, tax, and separate markup percentages on materials and subcontracts. This is the
richest document in the trade and it is offered without apparent hesitation.

A sixth shape is not a proposal at all. Where the general contractor issues a numbered scope sheet,
some bidders answer it row by row in a separate column instead of describing their scope, and the
returned sheet is the real bid. Diff any returned sheet against the issued one, because the row
numbers a bidder skipped are its exclusions and they are invisible otherwise.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The basis document, nearly always, by name and date, and often enumerated sheet by sheet across
  civil, structural, plumbing and electrical. Earthwork bidders cite their basis more precisely than
  most trades because their scope is spread across four disciplines.
- The soil classification assumed for export, nearly always, and usually the named receiving facility
  as well. This is the trade's defining disclosure and section 6 explains why.
- Whether dewatering is local sump only.
- The liquid asphalt base price used, wherever paving is in the scope, together with an escalation
  formula.
- An obstruction threshold, stated as a volume, above which ledge and boulders leave the scope.
- Where the utilities stop relative to the building.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- The export or import quantity. Some bidders state it and it is the single most useful number they
  can give you. One bidder's own boilerplate carries a fill-in-the-blank line reading that the base
  contract includes loading, transportation and disposal of a stated number of cubic yards at a stated
  conversion factor equalling a stated number of tons, and the blanks were left empty. The template
  knows the question matters.
- Crew size, durations or mobilization counts, unless a scope sheet asks. Paving mobilization counts
  are the exception and are commonly stated because they carry a real cost.
- Labor basis, union or open shop. Stated in a minority of proposals, and an estimator records it for
  every bidder regardless, because it is the second largest price driver in the trade.
- Bond cost. Nearly always excluded, occasionally offered as a stated percentage of contract value.
- Anything about the vertical datum. See section 6.

Exclusion behavior is heavy and highly templated. Thirty to forty exclusion lines is normal, and a
large share of any list travels verbatim between unrelated competitors. The obstruction threshold
travels as a phrase, the winter conditions line travels, and the list of things by others under a
duct bank travels. Read the boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project. Where a bidder repeats
the same exclusion twice in consecutive lines, which happens, it is pasting blocks rather than
writing.

The priced breakout beats the printed exclusion list here as elsewhere. Proposals routinely exclude
in the boilerplate what they price in the scope: one one-page bid begins with cut and cap of existing
sewer and water and ends by excluding cut and caps of any services. Another excludes bollards and
guard rail while carrying a priced bollard line in the same document. Never level off an exclusion
list alone.

Revision habits are unusually varied in this trade and the mechanism matters more than the movement:

- Quantity-reduction ladders are the most important shape and the most dangerous. Across three rounds
  a bid can fall by about a fifth with the scope families, line descriptions and units all unchanged
  while the assumed quantities drop: crew days for foundation preparation down by a quarter, the
  structural fill layer under a slab halved, allowance days for under-slab trades trimmed. No unit
  rate visibly moves. The bid came down by assuming less work, not by charging less for the same
  work, and if the leveling sheet then adopts those reduced quantities as its scope rows, the
  assumption has become the project's definition of the scope.
- Silent reprice, where the narrative proposal is byte-identical between rounds, the exclusions are
  identical, even the estimate date on the backup is unchanged, and only the number moves. Movements
  of two fifths occur. All the real content sits in a quantity backup that most readers never open.
- Reconfirmation, where a bidder asked to reprice against a superseding set re-issues its previous
  number and says so. A re-dated bid is not evidence that anybody re-read the documents. Watch also
  for a round that reverts to an older, lower number after an interim round had corrected it upward.
- Scope entry as a price rise, which is the honest revision and looks bad on a total. A conformed-set
  round rising by about a fifth can be entirely explained line by line: footings added by the new
  set, an operation that was an explicit exclusion in the earlier round becoming included scope, and
  a new pit shoring item appearing. Read the exclusion lists across rounds, not just the totals,
  because an exclusion that quietly disappears has left the paper and stayed in the number.
- Allowance migration. A stated dollar allowance carried in one round for an undefined item can
  become a routed exclusion in the next, on the stated ground that it belongs to another trade, with
  nothing in the totals to show it moved.
- Alternate-only revisions in response to an addendum, where the base does not move. Note that
  inserting a new alternate at the top of a list renumbers everything below it, so alternate two in
  one round and alternate two in the next can be different scopes with the same label.
- Row-level correction in rate cards, where two facility rows swap their rates with each other, the
  quote number advances by one day, and a total-level diff sees nothing at all.
- Comeback absorption, where a base rises slightly to take in one item the general contractor asked
  about by email.

The revision is frequently not a document. Bidders confirm scope, disclose additional items, and
answer questions by email, and those sentences are the real revision. Order rounds by the basis
document each cites, never by the filename or the letterhead date, and expect files whose folder set
stage disagrees with the set the bidder actually priced. A general contractor discovering mid-round
that a bidder had priced a superseded set is a normal event.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets in this trade have run from about ninety rows on a small project to more than three
hundred on a constrained urban one, with a hundred and sixty rows typical for a full site package.
Target a hundred and fifty rows for a greenfield site with a building, and expect a constrained urban
site to need more, most of it about seams and street work rather than about dirt.

There is a structural warning specific to this trade. On more than one project the leveling sheet's
scope rows are not the estimator's own list at all: they are the selected bidder's proposal lines,
quantities included, pasted in as the sheet's taxonomy. A sheet built that way cannot ask a question
that bidder did not answer, and it silently adopts that bidder's assumed quantities as the project's
scope. Build the scope list before the bids arrive, or the low bid will build it for you.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- The earthwork balance, decomposed. Not one excavation line but the full set of movements: strip and
  export topsoil, strip and stockpile topsoil for reuse, cut and export unsuitable or fill material,
  cut and stockpile natural material for reuse, fill to subgrade reusing stockpiled material in
  landscape areas, fill to subgrade reusing material under hardscape and building, and import
  structural fill. Bidders who quantify their bids enumerate exactly this way, and the difference
  between two bidders is almost always which movements they assumed rather than what they charge per
  cubic yard. A single site cut and fill line is unanswerable.
- Export, split by disposal classification tier, with a quantity against each tier. This is the most
  important enumeration in the trade and section 7 explains the instrument.
- Import, split by material and destination: structural fill for backfill, gravel base under slabs,
  gravel base under pavement, aggregate base under walks and pavers, crushed stone under other
  concrete, sand bedding, loam. The thicknesses differ under each and so do the material specs.
- Each cut and cap at the street, by service, counted. Water, fire service, sewer, drain, gas,
  electric and telecom are all different scopes with different owners, and the electric and telecom
  ones commonly are not this trade's at all. Count the locations and name the street.
- Each new service into the building, by service, with the stopping point stated. See section 5.
- Excavation and backfill for each under-slab trade separately: plumbing, electrical, telecom.
  Bidders price them as separate allowances, often in crew days, and cap them at different maximum
  depths.
- Each drainage or sewer structure type, counted: drain manholes by diameter, sewer manholes by
  average vertical feet, catch basins with and without sump, area drains, access basins, water
  quality units, outlet control structures, trench drain by length. These are the counted comparables
  in the package and the counts diverge between bidders more than the rates do.
- Each infiltration or detention system separately, with pipe diameter, length, surrounding stone and
  process gravel as their own lines. These systems are large enough to be their own subtotal.
- Duct bank by service and by encasement condition. Concrete-encased primary electric, concrete-
  encased telecom, direct-buried site lighting conduit and sand-bedded gas are four different unit
  costs, and who supplies conduit, spacers, pull strings and grounding is a separate question again.
- Precast pads and bases, counted and separated: transformer pads, switchgear pads, transclosure
  pads, light pole bases, light bollard bases, handholes, manholes. Some are furnished and installed,
  some are excavate-and-backfill only, and one bidder can do both on the same site.
- Pavement by course, with mobilization counts stated. Binder and top are separately mobilized and a
  bidder assuming one mobilization each has priced a different job from one assuming two.
- Curbing by type, radius condition and whether the curb is new, reset or salvaged. Salvage and store
  existing curb is its own line and so is reinstalling it.
- Erosion control by element: perimeter sock or silt fence by length, inlet protection counted,
  construction entrance counted, wheel wash, street sweeping while on site, and a per diem rate for
  periods when the contractor is off site but the controls remain.
- Dewatering, split three ways: local sump with onsite discharge, engineered dewatering, and the
  permitting and compliance sampling that goes with the second. See section 5.
- Support of excavation, ground improvement and underpinning, each on its own line even when they are
  excluded, and each with a companion support line for what the earthwork contractor must do for that
  contractor. Pre-trenching, the working pad, spoils handling and cut-off disposal are the support
  scope and they are routinely excluded by both sides.
- Obstruction and ledge, with the threshold and the instrument stated. See section 4.
- Winter conditions, as its own line. Excluded by nearly every bidder seen, in nearly identical
  words, and it is a real cost on any schedule crossing a winter.
- Permits and fees, split by kind: street opening, trench, street bond, utility authority inspection
  and signoff, sidewalk deposit, curb cut, water and sewer connection or tap fees, water usage
  mitigation, roadway repair, parking sign. Bidders take these in different combinations and several
  include a permit while excluding its fee or its deposit.
- Police details, separated from fire details, and separated again by whose work they cover. A bidder
  including details for its own utility work while excluding details for soil export and import is
  normal, and both belong on the sheet.
- Monitoring, split by kind: vibration, settlement, total station, air, dust, noise. Excluded by
  almost everyone.
- Preconstruction survey of adjacent properties, and layout, separately. Layout for own work from
  controls provided by others is commonly included; registered surveyor and control layout is
  commonly excluded.
- Testing ownership, split: compaction and density testing, material testing, soil characterization
  and screening, chemical and analytical testing, and pipe pressure testing and chlorination. The
  last is usually the bidder's and the rest usually are not.
- As-builts, split by whether they are stamped or certified. Nearly every bidder provides drawings
  and nearly every bidder excludes the stamp.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Equipment, crews and means. Whether the cut is done with a large excavator or a dozer is the
  bidder's business. The internal estimates that expose it are exposing their build-up, not offering
  a scope choice.
- Individual pipe fittings, bends, thrust blocks, marking tape and restraint hardware. These collapse
  into the pipe run they serve. Even the resource-level estimates that price them individually
  subtotal them into a utility.
- Trench spoil disposal separately from the utility run. It rides with the run unless the material
  classification changes.
- Individual soil movements below the balance categories. The balance categories above are the grain;
  double handling within them is the bidder's problem unless it is being priced as a concession.
- Room-level or grid-level enumeration of foundation excavation. Foundation excavation collapses to
  perimeter, interior, courtyard, garage and pits, which is how bidders and estimators both cut it.
- Erosion control maintenance separately from installation, unless a per diem instrument is being
  asked for.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In earthwork the
test fires hardest on the seams in section 5 and on every assumed quantity in section 6, because in
this trade the assumption is the price.

There is a second test worth applying, specific to this trade: for every quantity, ask what document
established it. Almost none of them come from the drawings. Cut and fill volumes come from the
bidder's own takeoff of two surfaces, export tonnage from a conversion factor the bidder chose,
disposal classification from a laboratory result that may not exist yet, and existing conditions from
a site visit and a set of assumptions. If a scope row states a quantity without stating whose
quantity it is, two bidders will answer it against two different numbers and neither will say so.

Silence is a finding here more than in most trades, and the most consequential silences are numeric.
A bid that states no export quantity has not carried less export than one that states a quantity; it
has carried an unknown quantity, and it will be right or wrong by the truckload.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate and a good deal of it circulates between unrelated competitors
word for word. The obstruction threshold phrase in particular is a trade fixture rather than a
company position. Read it for the trade map.

What is project signal, because it was written for this site and appears nowhere else in that
bidder's other proposals:

- A stated quantity. Any bidder that names an export tonnage, a site lighting length, a stated cut
  volume or a number of days it has assumed for an allowance has told you what its number is made of,
  and a competitor that states nothing has not offered a lower price for the same thing.
- A named disposal classification with a named receiving facility. Bidders that name both have
  actually priced a route.
- An elevation assumption. When a bidder states what it assumed the elevation of an unseen existing
  slab to be, or states the offset between the architectural datum and the civil datum, it has read
  both sets and found they do not agree.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. Foundation drainage excluded on the stated
  ground that the scope is not shown, or handholes priced against invented dimensions because no
  detail exists, are documents defects reported by the market.
- A disagreement with the design. A bidder attaching a marked-up drawing section to its bid showing
  that the designed utility routing cannot be built if existing duct banks are to remain live, and
  recommending a redesign, is doing the coordination review for you. Route it upstream as a request
  for information rather than absorbing it at leveling.
- An exclusion of work another trade claims. A bidder excluding under-slab plumbing earthwork inside
  the building footprint as work claimed by the plumber, while competitors carry it, is telling you
  the package boundary is contested and not that it is cheap.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number. Violating them converts the lump sum into a change order.

- The soil classification assumed for export. Universally stated in some form and it is the single
  largest condition in the trade. Several bidders go further and require a written letter of clean
  from the owner, stating the soil is unregulated and classified for unrestricted reuse, before they
  will export at all.
- The assumed export or import quantity, where stated. Where it is not stated it is still an
  assumption, just an undisclosed one.
- A maximum depth on the excavation and backfill carried for the under-slab trades, stated separately
  for electrical and plumbing and differing between them. Anything deeper leaves the scope.
- An obstruction threshold, stated as a volume in trench and a larger volume in open excavation.
  Below the threshold the bidder culls and disposes; above it, the work is extra. A minority price
  the culling at unit prices, which is the instrument to ask for.
- Liquid asphalt escalation, quoted as a base price per ton with a per-ton surcharge for every
  one-dollar-per-ton increase in the index. Nearly universal wherever paving is carried, and the base
  price differs between bidders on the same job, so the same escalation clause is measured against
  different starting points. Some bidders also state a diesel base price per gallon and pass fuel
  surcharges through at cost plus markup.
- Paving mobilization counts, stated as a number of binder mobilizations and a number of top-course
  mobilizations, with additional mobilizations priced.
- Personal protective equipment level assumed, normally the lowest, with any site-specific upgrade
  and any regulated-soil health and safety plan priced as an extra.
- Working hours, normally weekdays within a stated window, with premium time excluded.
- Access assumptions on urban sites: restricted truck routes reserved as a repricing trigger, road
  plates and trench boxes as needed, and a stated number of means of egress into the excavation.
- Where the utilities stop. See section 5.
- Commercial terms that change what the number is worth. Bidders reserve equitable adjustment for
  tariffs, duties, taxes, trade restrictions, supply chain disruption, labor shortages and regulatory
  change, expressly as a material condition of the bid. Soil disposal is invoiced with no retainage
  held by some bidders. Prices are commonly valid for fifteen or thirty days, which is short. Payment
  portal fees are quoted as a percentage of contract value with a cap plus a flat fee per sub-tier
  subcontractor.
- Site conditions priced from a visual observation, with the proposal made binding only subject to a
  site visit to agree conditions, means, methods and design assumptions.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This trade has almost no furnish component in the ordinary sense and an enormous custody component.
The recurring question is where the earthwork stops relative to somebody else's object.

### The building service seam

Every new service into the building stops somewhere, and where it stops is stated in the proposal in
plain words if it is stated at all. Three answers recur: five feet from the foundation, ten feet off
the face of the foundation wall, or inside the building stubbed a stated distance above finish slab
with a flange connection. Water and fire protection commonly go further in than sewer and drain, on
the same bid. Some bidders take different services to different points. A general contractor scope
sheet that asks whether the bidder brings services into the building or stops ten feet out, and gets
a question mark back, has found the seam and not closed it.

Related and separate: who breaks into the existing structures. Coring an existing sewer or drain
manhole is usually the earthwork contractor's. Breaking into a live electric or telecom manhole is
usually not, and is excluded by name on both sides.

### The support of excavation seam

The three-party version of this seam is where urban earthwork loses money.

The support contractor requires a cleared alignment, a stable working pad at a stated elevation, and
free and clear access with stated overhead and horizontal clearances. It prices none of these. The
earthwork contractor prices pre-trenching to a stated depth and excludes anything deeper or anything
requiring specialized equipment. Between those two positions sits a gap whose size nobody knows until
the pre-trench is dug.

Obstructions sit in the same gap but are excluded on two different instruments. Earthwork excludes
them by volume threshold. Support of excavation excludes them at any depth and publishes an hourly
crew rate for penetrating them, with the express statement that penetration rates are unpredictable
and that elements terminated early or needing reconstruction are extra. Neither party is carrying the
risk, and the rate exists so the risk can be billed rather than borne.

Two further items in this seam:

- Internal bracing interferes with the permanent concrete. Where a support system needs rakers,
  walers or internal struts, they occupy the space the foundation walls go into, and removing them is
  a separately stated cost that can be a meaningful share of the support package. Ask for it as a
  priced line.
- One-sided forming. Where support of excavation is installed tight to the foundation line, the
  concrete is formed against it, and the earthwork or support bidder will state an offset it has
  assumed and a horizontal deflection it expects. Any additional concrete resulting from that
  deflection is stated as somebody else's, which means the concrete package inherits a quantity
  nobody has counted. One bidder formally objected to the specification paragraph on this point and
  substituted its own deflection assumption.

### The ground improvement seam

Where aggregate piers or similar are installed, the earthwork contractor is expected to cut the site
to a stable working elevation, contain and remove the spoils, and place a load transfer pad of
imported crushed stone wrapped in geotextile on top of the elements before the footings go on. Some
bidders price all of that, some price a lump sum for support only, and some exclude the whole thing
including pre-trenching, obstruction removal, daily support crew, cut-offs and spoils disposal. A
scope line reading support the ground improvement contractor will be answered five different ways.

The sequencing question inside this seam is rarely asked and always matters: whether the ground
improvement design accounted for the open-cut foundation excavations, and for the trenching for
under-slab plumbing and site utilities that happen after the elements are installed. One bidder made
that assumption explicit and reserved the right to reprice if the design changed.

### The under-slab trades seam

The earthwork contractor excavates, beds and backfills for under-slab plumbing, electric and telecom;
the trades supply and install the pipe and conduit. Three things go wrong. The earthwork bidder prices
it as an allowance in crew days rather than against a quantity. The allowance is capped at a maximum
depth, different for each trade. And on some projects the plumber claims the work inside the building
footprint, so one earthwork bidder excludes it as claimed by the plumber while competitors carry it.
Ask which, and ask at what depth.

### The utility company seam

Electric and telecom are the most contested services on any site, and the boundary is not the same in
two places. The utility provider commonly brings service to the property line and does the street
work, so the earthwork contractor excavates and backfills on site only, forms and encases the duct
bank, sets the manholes and precast pads, and excludes conduit, spacers, pull strings, sweeps and
grounding. Whether the street portion is earthwork's, the electrician's or the utility's is a
project-by-project answer, and one scope sheet carries it as a priced add alternate for exactly that
reason. Utility company back charges are excluded by name.

Light pole and bollard bases split in the same way and often differently from each other in one bid:
a bidder can furnish and install precast pole bases while excavating and backfilling only for bollard
bases whose castings come from someone else, with anchor bolts, conduit sweeps, grounding and clock
diagrams by others in both cases.

### The dewatering seam

The word dewatering covers three different scopes and a scope line saying dewatering is unanswerable.

Local sump dewatering means a couple of pumps discharging to an onsite recharge area within a short
distance of the excavation during a normal shift. That is what nearly every earthwork bidder includes
and several define in exactly those terms. It carries no permit, no treatment and no sampling.

Engineered dewatering means a wellpoint or deep well system installed around the excavation perimeter,
sized from a geotechnical report's hydraulic conductivity data. It is a separate specialty package and
it is priced as a triplet per system: mobilization and installation, monthly rental, and
demobilization. The duration is an assumption, commonly stated as a number of months, and the rental
is where the money is.

Water treatment is a third thing again, priced as a base treatment train at a stated flow capacity
plus separate contingency trains for pH adjustment, carbon and metals, each carrying its own full
mobilization, monthly rental and demobilization. Those contingencies exist because groundwater
analytical data usually does not exist at bid time, so the bidder prices for a chemistry it has not
seen. Media removal and disposal, tank cleaning, power to the pumps and winterization are excluded.
The regulatory wrapper is its own set of lines: discharge permit preparation, pre-characterization
sampling, first-week compliance sampling, recurring sampling events, monthly data submittals to the
regulator, and a notice of termination.

Treat any pre-permit dewatering number as a duration-times-rate estimate against an unknown chemistry,
and expect the general contractor to plug the whole engineered package.

### The demolition seam

Demolition draws its line at the building footprint and this package picks up everything outside it.
Three items cross the line and each needs its own row.

Cut and cap at the street main leaves demolition and arrives here. On a leveling sheet the same
figure can appear as a plug against every demolition bidder, taken from the earthwork side's own
priced line, because nobody in that package prices it.

The disposition of demolition debris changes this package's quantity. Where a demolition bidder plans
to keep crushed masonry and concrete on site as a rubble buttress or a berm supporting basement walls
that remain, the earthwork contractor is relying on that berm being there, in that material, and one
earthwork bidder specified it as clean crushed concrete or processed gravel placed by others.
Different demolition assumptions produce different earthwork scopes and neither bid says so.

Backfilling the existing basement is a distinct decision with an unusual answer set: infill with
processed concrete to a stated elevation so support of excavation can be installed, import fill, or
reuse of the demolition material. The three cost very different amounts.

### The site concrete seam

Nearly every earthwork bidder prepares the base and excludes all cast-in-place work, including mud
mats, lean concrete, sidewalks, ramps, detectable warning pads, housekeeping pads and the base slab
under a detention system. The site concrete then comes back from a separate bidder, sometimes an
excavation company bidding under a Division 3 token, priced with each element split into preparation,
place and finish, concrete supply and reinforcing as separate rows.

Where this boundary falls can be set by labor jurisdiction rather than by the work. A general
contractor asking a site bidder how much concrete sits inside its number, because it may have to work
with the unions on that scope and does not want to disturb the site package, is setting the package
boundary from the labor agreement.

### The precast supply seam

Drainage and sewer structures are bought as material from a precaster, and that single object has
three parties on it. The precaster furnishes and delivers, with a stated free offloading window per
delivery and hourly charges beyond it, full loads only, jobsite accessible to a long truck and trailer
under its own power, and delivery windows constrained by oversize load regulations. The earthwork
contractor offloads and sets, except that structures above a stated diameter are delivered and
unloaded and set by others again. Frames, grates, miscellaneous hardware, coatings and waterproofing
are by others. Buoyancy and design calculations for the structures are a separately priced engineering
line on the supply side and an explicit add-cost exclusion on the earthwork side, so the calculation
that decides whether the structures float has no owner.

Payment terms on precast run from the earlier of delivery or a stated period after manufacture, not
from installation, and any approved and produced structure later removed from scope is invoiced and
delivered anyway. Submittal drawings are scheduled only on receipt of a signed order, so the lead time
starts at award and not at bid.

### Other seams

- Topsoil. Earthwork strips it and either exports it as clean material or stockpiles it. Screening it
  to a specification, amending it, importing loam and placing it belong to the landscaper, and several
  bidders exclude screening and amending by name while offering screening as its own priced operation.
- Existing pavement. Where it is pulverized in place and stockpiled for reuse as base, it is a
  material source rather than a waste stream, and the alternate to do it can be a net credit once the
  imported subbase it replaces is deleted.
- The stormwater filter or treatment insert. Contested between this package and plumbing, and one
  bidder carried it as a stated allowance in one round and routed it to the plumber in the next.
- Existing utility crossings. Where new utilities cross live duct banks, vacuum excavation, temporary
  support and flow fill backfill are a real scope that one bidder in a field will carry and the others
  will not mention.
- The elevated deck or basement roof. Where landscape or hardscape sits over structure, the earthwork
  bidder excludes work on it entirely and the growing medium, pavers and drainage above the membrane
  become somebody else's package.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

This is the largest section in this trade, because the subject of the work is the ground, and the
drawings describe the finished condition of a site whose existing condition nobody has measured.

What earthwork contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The civil drawing set, and specifically the utility plan, the grading plan and the details. This is
   the primary document and bidders cite it sheet by sheet.
2. The geotechnical or foundation engineering report. It supplies the soil profile, groundwater
   elevations and depths, hydraulic conductivity where slug testing was done, and the bearing
   recommendations that set the excavation depths. It is cited nearly as often as the drawings and it
   is the only document that says anything about what is underground.
3. The structural foundation plan, for footing sizes, bottom-of-footing elevations, pit depths and mat
   thicknesses. Bidders read structural sheets closely and quote footing elevations back.
4. A site visit. Cited constantly on constrained sites, and several proposals are made binding only
   subject to one.
5. The plumbing and electrical under-slab plans, for the excavation and backfill allowances.
6. The construction management plan or logistics plan, on urban sites, which is where the barrier,
   fencing, attenuator and temporary striping scope comes from.
7. The general contractor's issued scope sheet, which on urban work is frequently the real scope
   definition and is answered row by row.
8. The specification, cited rarely and quoted from almost never.
9. Existing utility authority records, permit plans and signoff charts, which enumerate the services
   better than the drawings do.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of these moves the number:

- The soil classification. This is the largest single driver in the trade and nothing on the drawings
  says it. Soil leaves a site under a regulatory tier established by laboratory analysis, and the
  tiers are separated by chemistry thresholds, not by anything visible. Across one export broker's
  rate card the highest tier costs roughly seven times the lowest per ton for the same cubic yard of
  dirt. Two tiers at the same contaminant concentration can price differently purely on whether the
  material is classified as remediation waste, which is a regulatory history question rather than a
  chemistry one. Every bidder states what it assumed, and every bidder assumes the cheapest tier
  unless told otherwise. If the classification is wrong, every number on the table is wrong by the
  same multiple.
- Where the soil is going, and whether the receiving facility will take it. Facilities have physical
  acceptance criteria as well as chemical ones: some will not take organics, topsoil, loam, clays,
  silts or wet non-compactable material at any price. Material bound for the nearest facilities must
  be field screened at a stated frequency to verify it stays under a threshold. Facility approval
  packages carry review fees that scale with volume, and per-sample fees beyond a stated count.
- Whether the bidder has somewhere to put the material. This is the trade's hidden competitive
  variable and it comes from the market rather than from the bid. A contractor with a fill site that
  will accept clean material has a structural advantage over one that must pay to truck and tip, and
  competitors say so about each other in writing. Where one bidder is far below a tightly grouped
  field, the explanation is usually either missing scope or a home for the export, and those are very
  different findings.
- The export and import quantities. Derived from the bidder's own takeoff of existing and proposed
  surfaces, and from a cubic-yard-to-ton conversion factor the bidder chooses. Two bidders quantifying
  the same site produce different volumes, and the general contractor's own quantity form asking for
  cut and fill and utility spoils can come back with those two rows left as to be determined while the
  easier rows are answered.
- The earthwork balance. Whether material can be reused on site at all, whether natural sand and
  gravel can be stockpiled and put back under hardscape and building areas, and whether subsoil can go
  into landscape areas, decides whether the job exports twenty thousand cubic yards or five thousand.
  A bidder can price the entire balance decision as one add alternate: export the cut and import fill
  for the backfill instead of reusing it, worth over a tenth of a base bid.
- Ledge and bedrock. The borings say how deep the rock is and nothing says how much of it is in the
  way, whether it can be ripped or must be hammered or blasted, or whether blasting is permitted on
  that site at all. Where it is live, it is handled by round-figure allowances on both the bidder side
  and the estimator side, which means two allowances for one unknown. Note that ledge can be cheaper
  to dispose of than the surrounding soil, because rock exports as clean unregulated material while
  urban fill does not.
- Obstructions. Old foundations, abandoned utilities, timbers, buried concrete and boulders. Excluded
  above a threshold by nearly everyone and priced by nobody.
- The groundwater elevation on the day of the work, as against the day of the borings. Monitoring well
  data taken over years shows several feet of variation, and dewatering flow estimates are stated as
  possibly two to three times higher at startup, after rainfall and snowmelt, and through preferential
  pathways nobody found.
- The groundwater chemistry, which usually has not been sampled at bid time, which is why the
  treatment package is priced as contingencies.
- The vertical datum. On more than one project the architectural and structural drawings and the civil
  site drawings use different vertical datums, and a bidder has to assume the offset between them and
  state it. When a bidder writes down what it assumed elevation zero on the architectural plans equals
  on the civil plans, it has found a documents conflict that will otherwise be discovered in the
  field. Ask for it explicitly.
- What is actually under the existing slab or behind the existing wall. Bidders assume the elevation
  of an unseen basement slab, the depth and construction of an abutting building's footing, and the
  presence or absence of a bond breaker. Test pits exist to answer exactly these questions, are bought
  as a tiny standalone package priced in crew hours, and can be priced with the test pit logs
  excluded, which is the deliverable that makes them useful.
- Labor basis, union or open shop. The second largest driver after classification. Where a bid list
  carries a single union bidder among open shop competitors, that bidder tends to sit at the top of
  the ranking, and part of the gap is labor and part is scope breadth, so do not attribute it all to
  either.
- Whether the work crosses a winter. Excluded by nearly everyone, so the general contractor carries it.
- Access and truck routing. A restricted truck route is a stated repricing trigger, and on an urban
  site the number of road plates, trench boxes, police detail days and street occupancy permits is
  driven by the municipality rather than by the drawings.
- Who supplies water for dust control, and whether a hydrant meter, permit and backflow preventer
  exist. Dust control is normally priced assuming somebody else supplies the water.
- Utility company scheduling. Some connections require a main shutdown coordinated by the water
  department, and where several project connections must be made under one shutdown, the sequence is a
  constraint on the whole site program.
- Whether the general contractor supplies survey controls, baselines and benchmarks, and updated CAD
  files when the design changes. Most bidders carry layout for their own work from controls provided
  by others and exclude the surveying that establishes them.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders openly carry allowances for items whose type is not
yet decided, name the assumption, and say they will revise. By permit and conformed sets the same
bidders switch to enumerating footings, pits and structures against sheet numbers. The most common
set-stage failure is not a bidder pricing badly but a bidder pricing the wrong set: a proposal filed
in a current-round folder can be priced off a superseded set, and the discovery is usually accidental.
Order rounds by the basis document each cites.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Cubic yards for soil movement, in place. The primary unit of the trade and the one every balance is
  written in.
- Tons for export and disposal, because that is what the receiving facility charges in. The conversion
  between the two is the bidder's assumption and it is almost never stated.
- Tons for imported aggregate, structural fill and asphalt.
- Cubic yards for imported stone and gravel under slabs and pavements, and for controlled density and
  flow fill.
- Square yards for pavement, subgrade preparation and pulverization; square feet for fine grading and
  for slab preparation.
- Linear feet for pipe by diameter, duct bank by service, curbing by type, erosion control, safety
  rail, support of excavation, underpinning and site lighting conduit.
- Square feet for support of excavation area, which is length times height of cut and is the unit an
  estimator will plug against.
- Each for structures, and vertical feet for manhole risers, so a manhole is priced as a base each
  plus risers per vertical foot plus a flat top each.
- Each per location for cut and cap, and each per service for new connections.
- Crew days for foundation preparation, slab preparation, and the excavation and backfill allowances
  for the under-slab trades. This is the trade's characteristic soft unit and it is where quantity
  reductions between rounds hide.
- Acres for tree clearing and grubbing.
- Hours for crews, equipment, police details, street sweeping and time-and-material work.
- Months for dewatering system rental, monitoring equipment rental and temporary works.
- Per diem or per visit for periods when the contractor is off site but its controls remain.
- Gallons for tack coat, and gallons for the diesel base price in an escalation clause.
- Percent of contract value for bonds and for payment portal fees.

Normalizing denominators an estimator will use, and they are not interchangeable:

- Cost per site square foot, on projects where the site is the subject. Confirm what the site area
  actually includes, because it can be the whole parcel or the disturbed area.
- Cost per residential unit, on multifamily projects, and this is what a leveling sheet will rank on
  even for a site trade.
- Cost per square foot of support of excavation, for that sibling package, which is sometimes
  normalized per unit instead in the same workbook. The same scope measured two ways in one document
  is a real occurrence, so name the denominator on every rate.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Soil classification and disposal tier. The largest driver by a wide margin, and it is a laboratory
   result, not a drawing fact.
2. Whether the bidder has a home for the export. Not a cost at all in the ordinary sense, and capable
   of moving a bid below a tightly grouped field on its own.
3. The earthwork balance, meaning how much material can be reused on site rather than exported and
   replaced.
4. Package breadth. In this trade scope breadth outranks price as a cause of ranking movement, and the
   biggest single component is whether support of excavation and dewatering sit inside the number.
5. Labor basis, union or open shop.
6. The assumed quantities behind soft units, especially crew days for foundation and slab preparation
   and the under-slab trade allowances.
7. Ledge, obstructions and unsuitable material, all of which are excluded and therefore all of which
   are the general contractor's.
8. Dewatering duration and whether treatment is required.
9. Street work volume: permits, police details, road plates, patching and restoration.
10. Import material quantity and specification, especially the thickness of structural fill under slabs.
11. Paving quantity, course thickness and mobilization count, plus asphalt escalation exposure.
12. Winter conditions and schedule.
13. Commercial terms: bond, escalation reservations, retainage on disposal, validity windows.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. On a full site package with a healthy bid list, low to
high commonly runs between two and two and a half times on cost per site square foot, with five or six
bidders. That is a wide range for a trade this quantifiable, and it decomposes cleanly: the top of the
range is usually a bidder carrying real scope others left out and often on a different labor basis, and
the middle of the range is usually two or three bidders within a few percent of each other. A bidder
that is far below a tight cluster is the one to interrogate, and the two explanations the market itself
offers are missing scope or a disposal advantage. Where the packages are not comparable at all, the
spread is meaningless: a ranking mixing a full-package bidder, a utilities-only bidder and a specialty
dewatering quote can show a tenfold range that measures nothing but breadth. Confirm what each column
contains before ranking anything.

Support of excavation and ground improvement disperse differently. Ground improvement, where the
scope is defined by a designer, can land within a quarter across three bidders. Support of excavation
routinely runs three or four times low to high on stated lengths within a few percent of each other,
because the method and the risk position differ rather than the quantity.

Two instruments worth asking for explicitly, because a minority of bidders offer them and they convert
the trade's biggest unknowns into known unit costs:

- A disposal rate card by classification tier and receiving facility, with the trucking rate and the
  tipping rate shown separately. The trucking rate tracks how many round trips a truck can make in a
  day, so distance is priced through trip count rather than mileage, and a facility further away can
  be cheaper if its tipping rate is low enough. Ask for the whole card, not one rate, because the
  classification is not yet known.
- Obstruction and over-excavation unit prices, so that the exclusion everybody carries has a price
  attached to it before it is needed rather than after.

Finally, an estimator does not compare base bids in this trade either. It compares base bid plus plugs.
The plugs to expect are the entire engineered dewatering and treatment package with its permitting and
compliance sampling; support of excavation; underpinning; ground improvement and the support work for
it; monitoring of all kinds with mobilization and monthly rental; preconstruction survey; ledge and
rock excavation; over-excavation and unsuitable material; obstruction removal; hazardous material
handling and soil characterization; winter conditions; police and fire details; permit fees and
deposits the bidders excluded; site concrete and flatwork; temporary fencing, barriers and
construction-management-plan hardware; striping and signage; bollards and site furnishings; and stamped
as-builts. A scope line set that names all of them is doing the estimator's plugging for it.

Two cautions on plugs specific to this trade. Plug rates are commonly sourced from a competitor's own
priced line for the same scope, so a plug can be a real quote sitting in a plug cell, and it is worth
knowing which. And on a well-quantified site package the leveling sheet may carry no plugs at all,
because the bidders enumerated everything, which is not the same as there being no gaps: the gaps have
simply moved into the assumed quantities.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly thirty earthwork and sitework proposals plus a set of boundary
records from the support-of-excavation, ground-improvement, dewatering, soil-export, precast-structure,
site-concrete, curbing, paving and hardscape packages, together with four estimator leveling sheets,
drawn from four projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The projects cover
three genuinely different site problems: two constrained urban infill sites with abutting structures,
deep basements and heavy street work; one large suburban greenfield site with a real earthwork balance
and a parking garage on ground improvements; and one coastal urban site with shallow bedrock. Proposals
span design development, permit, construction document and conformed rounds, and several bidders appear
on more than one project, which is what makes the boilerplate versus project signal distinction in
section 4 testable. Multiple same-bidder revision ladders are represented, including one running three
rounds and one running four across two set stages.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here is one market, one general contractor and four projects. The regulatory soil
  classification regime that dominates section 6 is jurisdictional, and while the shape of the
  instrument, a tiered ladder from unregulated through progressively more restricted destinations, is
  general, the tier names, the thresholds and the facility economics are local. Nothing here is tested
  against public bidding, highway or heavy civil work, industrial sites, or any market where earthwork
  is bid to an owner directly.
- No project involved a contaminated site actually being remediated. Every bid assumes clean or
  lightly regulated soil and prices the alternative as a schedule. What happens when the assumption
  fails is described from the exclusions rather than observed.
- Only one project had ledge as a live question, so the rock excavation material rests on it alone,
  and no priced blasting operation appears anywhere, only allowances.
- Only one project ran a full engineered dewatering and treatment package, so the dewatering pricing
  structure in sections 5 and 7 rests on one specialty bidder's schedule and the estimator's copy of
  it.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is present, so nothing here shows which exclusions survived
  negotiation and which were bought out.
- No change order history is present. The claim that assumed quantities, depth caps and obstruction
  thresholds convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- No project shows what was actually found underground. Every bidder states dimensions, elevations and
  classifications it could not verify, and no document records the outcome.
- Support of excavation, ground improvement, underpinning and engineered dewatering are deliberately
  boundary reads here, not the trades themselves. Their design regimes, means and methods, and the
  engineering that governs them are outside what these records support, and each deserves its own
  file.
- The corpus has not shown a site with a significant phasing or occupied-operations constraint, nor
  one where the earthwork was bought in packages rather than as one number.
- The corpus has not shown a project where a diversion or reuse target was enforced on the exported
  material, so who owns that reporting is unknown.

This file carries no structural gap list. The first corpus round did not draw one out for this
trade; a later round writes it where the corpus supports one.
