# Painting

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the painting package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. It speaks
in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves. Where
real proposals have not shown something, it says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real painters can bid it and a real estimator can
level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

### Establish bidder kind before anything else

Roughly one painting bid in five comes from a company whose core business is not production
painting, and this holds across most projects rather than being a curiosity of a thin list. The kinds
seen bidding a painting package: painting contractors; painting and wallcovering contractors;
multi-division finishes houses selling drywall construction, acoustical ceilings, decorative
painting, gilding, upholstered wall panels and wood finishing alongside painting; multi-trade
remediation, demolition and labor-supply houses; building-maintenance and building-services
contractors; a franchise that subcontracts its field labor and prints a franchisee profit;
general construction companies; a metal framing and drywall contractor; a specialty wallcovering
vendor; and, at least once, a stone fabricator bidding well outside its trade.

Bidder kind explains the top of a price range better than scope does. The most expensive columns in a
painting ranking are usually the bidders whose core business is something else, priced on a different
labor basis or covering more than the package. Establish kind before comparing anything, and treat a
bid list as a mixed pool by default.

Certifications sit beside bidder kind and shape the bid list before price does. About one painting
proposal in ten asserts one or more: minority-owned, women-owned, disadvantaged, veteran-owned,
sexual-orientation-and-gender-identity owned, union shop, a lead-safe renovator certification, a
location in a federally designated economically distressed zone. They arrive as a letterhead banner
on every page, in a footer, in a transmittal email signature a reader working from the priced page
alone never sees, or as certificates bundled behind the proposal. Dates are usually absent, and where
a certificate is bundled it can expire inside the construction period, be issued by a neighboring
state, or omit a required insurance line. Two signature blocks of one company in one email thread can
claim different certifications. Where a participation goal is a bid condition, expect it to be priced;
a bidder has also been seen excluding municipal workforce participation requirements by name.

### The section number identifies nothing

Painting and Coating is the section a proposal names when it names one, and up to five different
numbers name this package on a single project. Bidders and the estimator maintain independent section
vocabularies and neither reconciles to the other: a bidder can cite a five-digit number on one round
and a six-digit number on the next, and the same office's two workbooks can file the same trade under
two different numbers. The number tracks whoever last typed it, not the scope. Route on the trade
token and the product description.

### What the package is

On multifamily new construction the package as bid is painting and wall coverings, not painting
alone. Wallcovering collapses into the painting package by default and is carried by the same bidder:
most proposals price or exclude it as part of their own scope, and a distinct wallcovering section
number is cited by bidders very rarely, though estimator sheets use it as a routing label.

That default is a multifamily default, and building type and set stage break it. On office core and
shell, on office-to-residential conversions, on hotel work and at schematic design, silence is as
common an answer as a price: bidders neither carry wallcovering nor exclude it, the finish schedule
may carry marks nobody prices, and the estimator may open no wallcovering row at all. Where the set
carries no wallcovering marks yet, one pool can answer three incompatible ways: a blank department
left unfilled on the bidder's own printed form, an invented coverage fraction of the amenity wall
area priced as labor and material, and plain silence. A package tab titled painting and wall
coverings is not evidence the scope exists.

Wallcovering is detachable rather than merged, in every shape section 5 lists.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another
division or another spec section:

- Wallcovering and wall protection, as above. Wall protection is the weaker half: corner guards,
  crash rails and non-wallcovering protection trims appear rarely and have been seen routed out of
  painting to a wall protection trade by name.
- Concrete floor sealer at back-of-house rooms. Genuinely contested. Carried in the base by some
  bidders and excluded by name by others in the same pool, plugged onto painting as a leveling
  allowance on some sheets and noted as flooring's on others. Both directions occur inside one market.
- Epoxy floor coatings at bike rooms, pet rooms and similar. Contested and actively priced, not an
  occasional carry: base-bid marks, quantified alternates, unit rates, and a resinous floor system
  written into the painting specification section itself. Which trade owns it is decided by which
  product the specification names, not by the trade.
- Painted MDF or wood closet and linen shelving. Contested three ways: carried in the base, carried
  with a deduct alternate to remove it, and excluded as casework and closet shelving. It is the
  trade's most reliably detachable alternate.
- Caulking and sealants at painted joints. Carried by about half of bidders, always bounded to
  painted-to-painted surfaces, and increasingly bounded to a stated maximum joint width as well.
- Line striping at garage and parking. Mostly excluded, and a few bidders price parking markings as a
  named add alternate, itemized by striping element with a coat count. A base-bid-only read of such a
  bid misses it. On the estimator side the row is naked as often as it is plugged: it exists, no
  bidder answers it, and no value is attached.
- Garage interior surfaces. Where a sheet asks for them, the garage is bid as its own named line and
  is a small, cheap, separable segment. What actually leaves is the garage perimeter, the garage
  ceiling and the striping.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like painting:

- Custom murals and hand-applied decorative finishes. Named and excluded where they exist, priced by
  nobody. The market that does this work sits inside the painting bid list rather than outside it:
  bidders whose own letterhead sells gilding, marbleizing and decorative painting still exclude it.
- Limewash and other manufacturer-proprietary decorative wall finishes. Rare, and carried and
  excluded on the same project, which is a scope gap rather than a convention.
- Intumescent and fire-rated coatings. Excluded by name by roughly a quarter of all proposals and
  carried by almost none. Where it is carried the shapes are labor with the material excluded, a rate
  gated on approval of the assembly and the required dry film thickness, and an addendum-priced
  coating over another trade's sprayed insulation. Section 4 carries where it goes. Note that it is
  excluded by name on projects where no drawing calls for it, so much of that exclusion is reflex
  rather than a reading, and a scope line plugging it by default can be adding money for scope nobody
  drew.
- Electrostatic coating at elevator doors. The carve-out is the process, not the element. The
  electrostatic or factory-quality refinishing process is excluded and carried by nobody, while
  elevator doors, frames and the wood trim at the framed opening are painted in an ordinary acrylic
  system by several bidders, especially in office work. Body-filler repair of the same doors is
  excluded.
- Metallic and specialty architectural paints at elevator surrounds, guard rails and miscellaneous
  metals. Carried unprompted by some bidders, including proprietary metallic and mineral silicate
  systems priced as their own phase. It does not need an estimator's scope sheet to arrive.
- Textile, acoustic, felt and sound-panel systems. Pulled toward painting because they sit on the
  finish schedule beside paint marks, then reassigned by the estimator, and the destination varies by
  project: to the drywall or gypsum-board package by section number, to a specialty panel vendor
  ranked inside the painting sheet, or to a sound-absorbing-panels tab of its own. Competing bidders
  can classify the identical mark oppositely, as a vinyl wall covering by one and an acoustical wall
  panel by another. Assume nothing about the destination and ask.

### Conversions add a packaging axis

An office-to-residential or historic conversion adds a scope family with no counterpart on new
construction: historic treatment. It appears as its own specification section on a scope sheet, as an
exclusion family of half a dozen lines each cited to a sheet or a general note, as a
required-confirmation item before contract, and as repaint of historic doors, elevator surrounds,
existing masonry and existing stair treads inside a base bid. It brings convectors and radiators,
brass storefronts, photoluminescent egress markings, commercial tenant space priced as a warm white
box, and substrate repair where existing wallcovering comes off. None of it exists on a new-build
multifamily set.

## 2. How the market bids

The single lump sum is the most common shape and it is not the majority shape. Just over a third of
proposals state one number with no internal breakout; about as many state two to four; and close to a
third state more than four. Expect a segmented bid at least as often as a lump.

Segmentation axes, in rough order of how often they carry the split: scope family (interior,
exterior, wallcovering); area; commercial (subtotal, taxable material, tax, labor against material);
building, wherever a multi-building or multi-block solicitation exists, because the estimator
solicits by building and the bidders answer in the shape they were asked; element; phase; level; and
unit type. Element and building segmentation are live, not absent. The commercial axis is not a scope
axis at all and is treated in section 7.

Segmentation depth is not takeoff depth, and this is the trap the shape sets. A level-by-level
breakdown can be generated from a handful of independent figures by fixed whole-number fractions and
then split labor to material at one fixed ratio on every row; a per-floor breakout can carry
identical labor, material and total figures on every residential level. Thirty printed rows can
carry seven independent estimates. Reading them as thirty market observations reads one house's
arithmetic as judgment.

Measured quantities are more common than the trade's reputation suggests: about one proposal in five
carries unit pricing, and full measured takeoffs occur in several shapes, including a takeoff-platform
export organized by drawing sheet and then by room, an open-book cost model printing coverage rates
per gallon and a markup ladder, a franchise estimating workbook, and a takeoff marked up on the
architect's own plan sheets. A takeoff can also arrive with every price column blank.

Quantification does not predict rank in either direction, and where it predicts anything it predicts
expense: quantified bids sit at both ends of rankings and usually in the top third by price. A fully
quantified table is not evidence of a competitive number and is not evidence of a defined boundary
either, since the most quantified proposal in a pool can carry no exclusions at all.

What a proposal typically will state:

- A list of included surfaces, phrased either as surfaces (walls, ceilings, doors and frames, base,
  trim, stairs) or as coating systems per substrate (substrate, primer coats and product class,
  finish coats and sheen). The coating-system framing is common and is the only framing that can be
  audited against the specification, which is a reason to ask for it.
- A paint manufacturer and product grade, commonly. Contractor grade and a three-coat system are the
  defaults.
- Coat counts, where the proposal is written in coating-system form. One primer coat plus two finish
  coats is written out in most such documents.
- A labor type, in about half of proposals.

What a proposal typically will not state, or will state unreliably:

- A basis document. About a quarter of proposals name no set, no date and no spec section at all.
  Others name it wrongly: a superseded set months old, a year that does not occur in the
  solicitation, two sets in one sentence, three mutually inconsistent bases in one document, a set
  dated after the letterhead date. Do not order or compare rounds on a cited basis without checking
  it, and expect a bid pool to be pricing several different sets with nothing on the leveling sheet
  saying so.
- A go-back and punch allowance. Rare, and where stated it is far more often a count of visits than a
  count of hours: a single punch-list walk-through, one mobilization per unit, punch limited to the
  bidder's own deficiencies, or turnover punch folded into a scope narrative. An hours-per-unit basis
  from a bidder is exceptional.
- A mock-up unit. Never stated by a bidder. Estimators carry it as a scope row on a minority of
  sheets and it is answered by nobody.
- Quantities. Present in a minority, but not almost absent: a stated dwelling-unit count sits on the
  face of a substantial minority of proposals and is often corroborated independently by several
  bidders on the same set.

Three commercial facts do not need an estimator's email to surface, though the trade is often told
they do. Warranty duration is stated unprompted by a minority of bidders, at one year, sometimes with
named failure modes, a claim window and a stated remedy. Bond and insurance arrive unprompted on
occasion, as a priced add or a stated rate. And prevailing wage is stated as a negative in every
market except a prevailing-wage one, where bidders state it positively and the estimator records it
as a first-class labor class beside union and open shop.

Exclusion behavior splits the market and the split matters more than proposal shape. About a quarter
of proposals carry no exclusion list of any kind. Of those that do, most run under ten lines; a list
of ten to twenty is the top fifth of the market, not its typical case. A bidder with no exclusion
list is not offering broader scope, it is leaving every boundary open.

What that absence does not do is predict price. A missing exclusion list appears at both ends of
rankings: on the low bid and selected subcontractor on some projects, on the highest bid at more than
twice the winner on others. What predicts a low number is missing scope, not a missing list. And the
plug does not reliably follow either: an estimator has been seen applying its caulking plug to the
columns that carried exclusion lists and withholding it from the two that carried none, one of which
was selected.

Revision habits, across same-bidder pairs:

- Scope moves in most pairs. A price-only revision with the scope text unchanged to the character
  happens, and it is the minority shape. Where a revision does hold its scope, it holds its
  descriptions, its units and its quantities and moves only a rate.
- Movements are usually modest, roughly a tenth to a quarter in either direction. A minority are very
  large, including drops of more than half and of about three fifths. A reason is almost never
  documented anywhere in the paper.
- Direction is not one-way. Scope entering at a price rise is the commonest single mechanism; scope
  entering at an unchanged total, scope entering at a price decline, and scope leaving while the
  price rises all occur.
- The trigger is a new set, an addendum, a value-engineering request, a descope call, a scope request
  issued to the whole pool, or a rejection disclosure from the estimator. A returned scope sheet is
  almost never the trigger, because bidders almost never return one.
- A revision label can be an addendum response filed in an addendum folder. Do not assume a revision
  is a negotiation artifact by default; read what it answers.
- The revision is frequently not a document at all. Whole bids exist only as figures in an email
  body; a requested breakout can exist only in email and never reach the transmitted proposal; a
  round's submission can be the previous round's proposal re-transmitted behind a covering email; and
  a general contractor may re-solicit on a later set and accept a written no-change confirmation as
  the revised bid.

A newer shape worth naming. Some proposals now carry the register and structure of a generative
drafting tool rather than a house template: headed area blocks over a lump sum, a four-line exclusion
list, section openers that are instructions to the drafter left in the text, and a stated basis that
the unit prices were the ones given. The consequence for a reader is that scope narrative and price
derivation have come apart. A long, fluent, well-organized painting proposal is no longer evidence
that the bidder read the set. The tell is whether the document cites a sheet, a mark or a detail
rather than a category.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Build the grain from the bidders' documents, not from the estimator's sheets. Across many unrelated
projects in one market the bidders agree with each other far more than the sheets do: sills and
aprons in with the sash out, one primer and two finish coats, caulking bounded to painted-to-painted
surfaces, exterior reduced to hollow metal doors and frames, prevailing wage stated as a negative,
contractor-grade product as the pricing basis. Each of those is written by companies that share no
template. The sheets, by contrast, are one office's evolving checklist: their row sets, their
organizing axes, their plug lists, their denominators and their section numbers all change from
project to project while the market around them does not.

There is no row-count target. Painting scope sheets run from zero rows of content to more than one
hundred and fifty, with a median around twenty. Sheets carrying forty to eighty rows of content are
roughly a quarter of what exists. Two of the deepest sheets seen carry no bidder answer at all in any
column, and one of the thinnest produced the tightest spread in its market.

So row count is the wrong measure, and answered columns is the right one. Count how many bidder
columns carry any scope-row answer, not how many rows exist. A fifty-five-row sheet with zero answers
and a thirteen-row sheet with one answered column are opposite instruments and row count reports them
backwards. The binding constraint is whether anyone answered.

A thin sheet still fails the way the trade expects, and the failure is worse than a wide spread. Where
the two boundaries that decide the money, wallcovering and exterior, are written as bare questions
that nobody answers, the pool spreads more than four times across the priced documents on file. Where
the sheet asks nothing at all, the spread comes back narrow, which is a tight-but-false spread and not
market agreement. Interpret a spread with its cause before treating it as an input.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Area or room type, wherever the coating system or the access changes: residential units, corridors,
  lobby and amenity, back of house, stairwells and stair enclosures, garage or parking, basement and
  sub-basement, mezzanine, penthouse, retail or commercial tenant space, exterior. This is the primary
  organizing axis in the proposals. On the sheets it competes with work family, element, finish mark,
  building, phase and bare specification section number, so do not expect a sheet to be organized this
  way.
- Substrate, wherever it changes the coating system: gypsum board, concrete masonry, cast-in-place or
  exposed concrete, hollow metal, wood, metal stair assemblies, exposed structure and deck, exposed
  mechanical and piping, existing plaster. These appear as separate priced rows in a quantified
  proposal and as separate coating-system bullets elsewhere.
- Every named finish mark in the finish schedule that is not a plain paint: each wallcovering mark,
  each wall protection mark, each specialty coating mark, each sealed or coated floor mark, each
  decorative or mural mark. Mark ranges seen run to twenty-six paint marks on a project and to
  twenty-two paint marks alongside eight wallcovering marks on another. About a third to a half of
  proposals cite scope by mark, and sheets that carry a scope grid are commonly two rows in three
  finish marks.
- Doors, frames and trim as their own group, separate from walls and ceilings, and split again by
  wood against hollow metal because the coating systems differ. All six sides on wood doors is stated
  by bidders across many unrelated projects and is asked by no estimator sheet, which makes it a
  question the scope line has to put rather than one the sheet will.
- Windows, in at least four states, which is why a single windows line is always ambiguous. Sills and
  aprons included with the window and sash excluded in one sentence is the commonest. Sill only, on
  the stated ground that the jambs are gypsum board returns, is next. Windows, sills and trim excluded
  outright occurs. And an estimator can resolve sills and aprons out of painting entirely on the same
  gypsum-return reasoning, with the sheet's own row title written as a question.
- Caulking and sealants, broken by joint condition: door trim to wall, window to wall, base to wall,
  counter and backsplash to wall, stair stringer to wall, painted to painted generally. Proposals
  enumerate four to six named conditions where they enumerate at all. Carry the stated maximum joint
  width on the line: bidders cap the joint at an eighth or a quarter of an inch, and a caulking line
  with no stated maximum gap is not a priceable line in this market.
- Preparation and general conditions as their own block: nail-hole filling, general prep, light
  sanding and crack filling, filling dimples in hollow metal frames, protection of adjacent surfaces,
  priming, hoisting and lift access for own work, cleanup to dumpster, mock-up unit, project-portal or
  payment fees, insurances. This block exists on the sheets that carry a scope grid and almost never
  in proposals.
- Go-backs and punch, its own line, with an explicit basis stated on the line. See section 6 for why
  the basis has to be named rather than assumed.
- Mobilization count. Painting is sold with a stated number of mobilizations more often than the trade
  is credited for: one mobilization in the price with a rate for each additional, one mobilization per
  released area, and a mobilization count attached to a dryfall system. Rates for an additional
  mobilization vary by a factor of about five between bidders, so the count belongs on the line.
- Existing-conditions work on renovation and conversion projects: repaint of existing-to-remain doors
  and frames, refinish or restain of existing millwork, repaint of existing windows, patch-and-paint
  of existing plaster, remove and reinstall of existing cornice and moulding, existing masonry at
  basement and sub-basement, existing handrails, substrate repair where existing wallcovering is
  removed. This block runs six rows on a light renovation and about forty on a phased healthcare
  project, and each row tends to be answered differently by different bidders. It lives in the
  proposals as often as on the sheet, and on a conversion the sheet may carry none of it.
- Historic treatment, on any conversion of a building with historic fabric, per section 1.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Walls, ceilings, soffits, columns and bulkheads within one area collapse to one line in most
  documents. Where a sheet does split them into separate element rows, the bidders answer none of
  them, which is the strongest confirmation the rule has.
- Coat count, primer product, finish product and sheen are attributes of a line, not lines. They vary
  by substrate, which is already an enumeration axis. Carry them as attributes and read them, though:
  they are where an undisclosed grade ladder hides, and they are the only place a coat-count shortfall
  against the specification is visible.
- Individual door, frame or column counts usually collapse into a doors-and-frames line. The exception
  is real and recurring: a bidder pricing per unit type or per level will carry doors and frames as
  separate each-priced rows inside each type, and a takeoff can count door area in square feet and
  frame length in linear feet as separate rows.
- Room-by-room enumeration collapses to room type on new construction. It does not collapse on a
  renovation phase, where a bidder can price every room by number with its own measured area and its
  own extension, and the estimator's own sheet can list the same rooms by number with their wall,
  ceiling, trim and door codes.
- Levels collapse into a level range unless the coating scope actually changes. A bidder pricing
  levels three to six as one line while pricing levels one and two separately is correctly tracking
  where the finish changes; a bidder pricing every level separately is usually tracking phasing rather
  than finish.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
painting, the test fires hardest on the seams in section 5 and on the specialty coatings in section 1.

Silence is the third outcome and it is a finding, not an inclusion. Where an item is one that some
bidders carry and others exclude, a proposal saying nothing about it is a scope gap to surface. Two
shapes of silence need naming because a parser inverts both. A bidder can answer its own template's
wallcovering block with a not-applicable rather than an exclusion, on a set carrying a mark range,
and be the low bid. And a bidder's own printed form can carry a priced department left entirely
blank, so the stated total covers none of that scope and the document nowhere says so. An empty box
is neither an exclusion nor a zero.

### The structural gap list

Scope that has to be added to every number before comparing, in three parts. This is an instruction
for what a scope line set should ask, not a description of what estimators actually do: about a third
of painting leveling sheets apply no plug to anyone, and where plugs exist they are commonly applied
to some columns and withheld from others, frequently the cheapest.

What no bidder carries, so the estimator must:

- Exterior painting. The largest and most reliable gap in the trade. Excluded by name in nearly half
  of all proposals, silent in a third more, and carried by fewer than a quarter. Where it
  survives it is narrow: an entry door and frame, exterior hollow metal doors and frames, a roof-deck
  guardrail, bollards, garage columns. It reaches buyout named on several rows with a note that no bid
  defines complete limits and a plug on none, it is written as a bare question row nobody answers, and
  where a plug does exist it is applied asymmetrically or set above every bidder's own price for the
  same area.
- Go-backs and punch. No bidder carries an hours-per-unit allowance. The row exists on about a third
  of leveling sheets and touches about half of projects, a plug actually reaches a column on about a
  quarter of projects, and the row draws a bidder answer very rarely.
- Wallcovering termination trims, fry reglets and outside corner trims. Carried by nobody anywhere.
  Named by roughly one in ten of the documents that price wallcovering, so most of the market does not
  know they exist. A counterparty is named only occasionally, and drywall is that counterparty when
  someone writes it down; the same house template can carry the routing clause in one solicitation and
  drop it in the next, which strands the work. A leveling sheet rarely carries a row for them at all,
  and where it does the row can be an open question asking whether painting or Division 10 furnishes
  and installs the trim. They must be installed before the wallcovering goes on, which makes an
  unassigned trim a schedule problem as well as a money one.
- The garage perimeter, ceiling and floors. Excluded by most bidders and carried by nobody unless a
  scope row names the garage, in which case bidders price it as their own line and it is a small,
  cheap segment. No other package contends for it, so the contest is inside the painting pool rather
  than between packages: it turns on whether the sheet asked. A bidder can also price the garage
  labor only, which leaves the garage material with nobody.
- Garage line striping and parking markings. Excluded by name and carried only as an occasional add
  alternate. Where the estimator plugs it, the basis is a rate per parking stall or a rate per square
  foot bundled with basement painting.
- Intumescent and fire-rated coatings, in the ordinary case. See the answer in section 4.
- The unbounded caulking families: caulking to unpainted surfaces or where only one side is painted,
  fire caulking, acoustical sealant, sanitary and plumbing-fixture caulking, Division 10 specialty
  caulking, acoustic grid to wall, and all exterior caulking. Painted-to-painted caulking is carried
  by about half of bidders; none of these is carried by anyone. The gap is the boundary, not the item.
- Photoluminescent egress markings, on any building that has them.
- Temporary heat and temporary enclosure to reach the temperature and humidity the coatings need.
  Excluded by name by a few bidders and unmentioned by everyone else, so it lands on the general
  contractor by default.
- Substrate repair and skim coating where existing wallcovering has been removed, on a conversion.
  Drywall-side work created by demolition, on a surface the painter is contracted to finish, carried
  by no package.

What is contested between packages, so the estimator must decide who has it rather than plug it:

- Concrete floor sealer and epoxy floors, against flooring. Carried by roughly as many bidders as
  exclude it. The same scope row can run in opposite directions across one pool, with a deduct to
  remove it from one base and an add to put it into another at the same order of magnitude and
  opposite sign, so summing or comparing that row across a bid list is meaningless.
- Level-five skim coating, wall patching and broad substrate correction, against drywall. See section
  5.
- Acoustic, textile, felt and FRP panels, against drywall, specialties and a specialty panel vendor.
  Destination varies by project and competing bidders classify the same mark oppositely.
- Exposed pipe, duct, conduit, standpipe and deck. Contested rather than systematically uncarried,
  and carried by rather more bidders than exclude it. It arrives as a plain inclusion, as a
  conditional inclusion tied to whether the finish schedule marks the ceiling, deferred to time and
  material, or refused by specification article. The hazard is that a pool can still line up almost
  entirely against it: a request-for-information answer has been seen directing the contractor to
  carry exposed pipe and conduit while most of that pool excluded mechanical painting by name and
  the sheet carried no plug.
- Wall protection, corner guards and crash rails, against a wall protection trade and specialties.
- Elevator door and frame metal, against the elevator trade. Painted as ordinary metal in office work
  and routed out as a specialty refinish elsewhere.
- Painted MDF and wood closet shelving, against millwork and casework.

What the estimator carries outside every package, so no subcontract holds it:

- Repairs, patching, cleaning and protection at existing structures and finishes to remain, on
  conversion and renovation work, carried as a general contractor allowance on the owner-facing
  breakdown. On this kind of work the patching money is not a boundary between two trades; it sits
  above both.
- An intumescent coatings allowance, held by the general contractor and stated in writing to the
  owner side as needing more information before a subcontractor can price it, at a small fraction of
  what a painter charges for the same scope.
- Repaint and repair at an existing exterior structure, likewise as an allowance.
- The model or mock-up unit, which can be carried once at project level across all trades rather than
  against the painting package.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the trade that must pick the work up, and
if no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap. Read the counterparty column with care: on
most projects the painter names no counterparty at all, and the named trade below is where the work
goes when someone writes it down.

| Excluded by the painter | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Custom millwork, casework, built-ins, amenity millwork (most bidders) | Millwork contractor, prefinished at the shop |
| Staining, clear and natural finishes; prefinished doors and trim (most bidders, and not universally: a selected sub can carry staining of custom millwork walls, wood ceilings, tambour panelling and veneer columns inside its base) | Millwork or door supplier |

### Boilerplate versus project signal, and the authorship check

Most of the table above is boilerplate. Repeat bidders reproduce the same list on every project, and
repeat bidders are most of a bid list in one market: a handful of companies appear on the majority of
projects, and their exclusion and qualification blocks travel intact for years.

Before promoting anything in a bid list to a market convention, check authorship rather than
letterhead. Whole scope-note blocks, qualification paragraphs, cost-summary forms and unit adder rates
travel word for word between differently named companies with different addresses, telephone numbers
and email domains. A purchased commercial proposal template circulates with its vendor's copyright
line and its fields left blank. And the same operation files under two names across projects, with
one president, one office telephone and one address, disclosed nowhere in either document and visible
only in the estimator's own ranking column label, which carries both names. At least two such
operations run through one market. A convention counted by distinct company name is not a convention;
count it by distinct authorship.

Boilerplate is also not inert, and the edits are where the project signal is. A repeat bidder's
apparently identical house template can carry real per-project changes: a window line moved from
sill, apron and casing to sill only with the reason given; a spray-and-sequencing paragraph and a
metal-defect disclaimer added; a wood baseboard line flipped from excluded to included; a
counterparty clause dropped from a termination-trim exclusion, so the same sentence routes the work
in one solicitation and strands it in the next. Diff a repeat bidder's list against its own list on
another project, not against its competitors.

One structural point about which list to diff. For a repeat bidder the exclusion and qualification
blocks are frequently stable to the character across three rounds and a full year while every scope
change lives in the inclusion list. Diffing exclusions on such a bidder finds nothing. Diff the
inclusion list.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- A named finish mark excluded by number. Where a bidder writes out specific mark numbers and refuses
  them, it read the finish schedule and found something it will not do. Common enough to expect and
  the highest-value form.
- An exclusion justified by reference to a specific sheet, detail or general note. On a conversion an
  exclusion list of about twenty lines, each cited that way, is a documents-gap report and is
  RFI-grade in its entirety.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings, while the estimator's sheet keys the same scope
  to a sheet and a mark. When a bidder and the general contractor disagree about whether scope exists
  on the set, that is a documents problem and worth an RFI.
- An exclusion justified by the room finish schedule saying those rooms take no paint. This reads like
  a refusal and is a correct reading of the documents; only the cited schedule tells the two apart.
- An exclusion whose stated reason is the bidder's own uncertainty about the documents, such as a wall
  base refused on the ground that it looks prefinished. That is an RFI written as an exclusion.
- A specification article refused verbatim. A bidder can quote a dozen specification articles by
  section, part and paragraph and refuse each one, which collectively deletes the whole mechanical and
  electrical painting scope, the exterior schedule, the high-performance schedule, the clear-finish
  standard and the touch-up obligation after other trades. That list is a specification audit, every
  entry is RFI-grade, and it removes a whole specified scope family without ever looking like scope
  movement.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a penthouse, a sub-basement, a retail shell,
  a precast garage, named tenant floors, a second building, an existing structure to remain.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Spray application is assumed and the price is explicitly conditioned on being allowed to spray and
  on logical sequencing with other trades; out-of-sequence work is chargeable. The most widely stated
  qualification in the trade.
- Doors and frames sprayed with the finish coat before the finish flooring goes in, with no masking of
  floors to spray doors.
- Trim, doors and woodwork arrive factory primed; if they do not, extra charge. And the shop primer
  must be compatible with the specified finish system, which a primer merely being present does not
  satisfy.
- The drywall substrate is the painter's problem only to the level the specification requires. Bidders
  disclaim responsibility for drywall quality control, for repairing defective metal, for cleaning
  welds and poor factory priming, and for concrete spillage left by others. Some add a
  notify-and-proceed clause: the painter tells the general contractor the surface is unacceptable, and
  proceeding on the general contractor's direction transfers the repair cost.
- Standard prep is bounded quantitatively, and the full instrument is a house rule rather than a
  market one: a defect no larger than a coin, a stated percentage of any one wall or surface above
  which the prep leaves the lump sum, and a published hourly rate beyond it. It comes from a handful
  of operations and travels with them across projects and across years, the sentence unchanged and
  only the rate moving, so the rate is a project variable rather than a house constant. Its absence
  from a proposal is not evidence that bidder priced prep any differently. Where the full instrument
  is absent the market bounds prep by a gap dimension, a miter condition, or a stated hour of labor
  instead.
- Coating standard stated as one primer coat plus two finish coats to a named industry standard or a
  named painting-industry practice standard. A bidder can also refuse that standard by specification
  article.
- Material pricing held for a stated number of days from the proposal date, from thirty to one hundred
  and eighty, or to a calendar date. Watch for a validity date that does not exist on the calendar and
  for one already expired at the time of quoting; both occur, from house templates.
- Material escalation at a stated percentage per year, carried inside the price rather than as an
  adder. The mirror also occurs, with escalation stated as unknown and expressly not included. Both
  totals read as a lump sum and nothing on the face distinguishes them.
- Markup stated on the face: a combined overhead and profit percentage, or a full ladder of a labor
  buffer, an overhead percentage on direct cost and a profit percentage on cost plus overhead.
- A proposal that is also a contract form, with signature blocks for both parties, so accepting the
  price and executing the subcontract are one act. The reverse clause also occurs, stating that an
  executed subcontract governs over the proposal.
- A numbered section of required confirmations before contract, which is a bidder shipping its own RFI
  list as part of the offer. Read it as an RFI list.
- A payment-delay work-stoppage clause, reserving the right to stop work if payment runs past a stated
  number of days.

### Labor regime is an axis to record, never a rank rule

Union against open shop is not reliably the largest price driver in this trade and must not be treated
as one. It brackets both ends of some rankings, with the lowest column and the highest columns all
union and the middle all open shop. On prevailing-wage work the prevailing-wage bidders can occupy the
low end and the union bidders the high end. On several bid lists every bidder in the pool is open shop,
so the axis discriminates nothing at all. And a bidder can state a prevailing-wage rate in one round
and a non-prevailing-wage rate in the next at an identical price on identical scope, which no leveling
on a labor field survives.

Record the axis for every bidder, note that the estimator will record one whether the proposal states
it or not, and note that the recorded value is sometimes contrary to the bidder's own words. An
explicit union-against-open-shop delta line for painting is rare; where it appears it is a note against
a single column rather than a sheet-wide instrument. Where a whole workbook is built two ways for two
labor regimes, the painting tab can be identical in every cell including the labor type row.

The axis has more than two values. Union, open shop and prevailing wage all appear as first-class
recorded classes, and a residential against commercial rate classification is a fourth axis underneath
them that no two-way field expresses.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### Wallcovering: the seam that matters most

Wallcovering appears in six distinct commercial shapes, and the drawings will not tell you which one
applies:

1. Painter furnishes and installs. The most common shape by a wide margin.
2. Painter installs only, material furnished by the owner or by a supplier. Where this happens the
   three scope lines it should bring, receiving, inventorying, storing and re-delivering the
   owner-furnished goods, providing the quantities the owner must order, and providing all adhesives,
   are almost never stated by the install-only bidder. Put them on the sheet yourself.
3. Painter offers it as a detachable alternate, priced separately.
4. Material and installation carried as two separately priced rows by the same painter, so furnish and
   install are separately purchasable.
5. A per-mark, per-location menu offered as a decomposition of a single wallcovering line, for the
   owner to pick from.
6. A separate specialty wallcovering vendor bids it directly. On a minority of projects, and where it
   happens the painting bidders are not necessarily silent: they may price the same marks in parallel.

Two further variations sit inside a single proposal rather than across a bid list. Mode can vary by
mark, with most marks furnish-and-install and two named marks install-only in one bullet list. And a
priced wallcovering segment can be split-basis, with some marks priced from real products and others
carried at a stated allowance per linear yard because the schedule shows them as to be determined.
Nothing on the face of a wallcovering segment says which marks are priced and which are carried, and
the sheets never ask.

The specialty-vendor case exposes the real dependencies, because a vendor prices them as conditions
rather than mentioning them as courtesies. The list below is one shop's standing conditions, seen on
several unrelated projects in near-identical words, and not a demonstrated market convention: the
same operation stands behind every sighting, so read it as what a specialty vendor can require
rather than as what every vendor requires. It is still the sharpest statement of the dependencies
anywhere in the trade, and a painter's silence on any of it is a gap rather than an answer:

- The substrate must be sanded smooth, dust-free and primed by others with a named wallcovering
  primer. The painter's ordinary primer choice can void the wallcovering warranty.
- Low-VOC and zero-VOC primers require a minimum twenty-eight-day cure before wallcovering can be
  applied. That is a schedule constraint hidden inside a materials note and it belongs on the scope
  line.
- Wall and ceiling mounted devices and fixtures must be removed and reinstalled by others.
- The vendor's priced scope is defined by its own shop drawing, not by the contract documents, and it
  warns the general contractor in writing to compare that drawing against its own takeoff before
  submitting final pricing. Two definitions of one scope have to be reconciled at leveling, and the
  values in circulation for one identical mark range have been seen to span twice.
- The shop drawing is the architect's own bid-set sheets re-issued with color-coded markups, one
  sheet per mark per level, and legend colors can be near-identical. A monochrome print loses the
  scope boundary silently and completely, and those sheets can extract to zero characters, so a
  text-only read loses them entirely.
- Marks can live on a reflected ceiling plan and appear on no enlarged finish plan. Wallcovering is a
  ceiling scope as well as a wall scope; open the ceiling drawings for this trade.
- Two schedule marks can resolve to one manufacturer product under two names.

The same shop varies its own list between projects: sequencing conditions with a stated cost increase
if breached, named marks installed before millwork, mailboxes, kickplates and door hardware, appear
on some of its quotes and on others it prices one mobilization and lists coordination failure as an
extra-charge trigger instead. Ask for the conditions; do not assume the list.

Wallcovering material units do not match the drawings. Linear yards is the supply chain's unit, square
feet is how the drawings and the general contractor think, and the square yard also appears as a
schedule allowance unit, so three incompatible units circulate. A finish schedule can state most marks
per square yard and one per square foot at the same figure, which moves that mark's allowance by about
nine times. The conversion between area and yardage needs the roll width and the pattern repeat, and a
bidder that states both is doing the estimator's work; a quantity stated in yards may also be stated
before a waste add of about fifteen percent, so the rate per stated yard and the rate per delivered
yard differ by that much.

### Other seams

- Doors and frames. They arrive factory primed from the door supplier; the painter applies the finish,
  and on wood doors finishes all six sides. Stated explicitly by bidders across many unrelated
  projects and asked by no estimator sheet.
- Concrete floor sealer and epoxy floors. Genuinely contested between painting and flooring, and the
  contest is live in both directions inside one pool.
- FRP, textile, felt and acoustic panels. Pulled toward painting off the finish schedule and assigned
  elsewhere by the estimator, to drywall, to a gypsum-board package by section number, or to a
  specialty panel vendor. Two rankings of one workbook can each route the same marks to the other, so
  the pair is priced by nobody while both tabs believe it is covered.
- Exterior cladding. The dominant exterior variable is whether siding, trim, fascia and panels arrive
  prefinished or are field painted, and it swings the painting number harder than anything else on the
  projects that have it. Field-painting the cladding can be worth about a tenth of a whole base bid,
  can exceed the entire rest of the exterior scope, and can be carried as an add alternate by one
  bidder and inside the base by another on the same list. A bidder's own value-engineering suggestion
  to switch to a prefinished cladding product can take about a seventh out of its number. On projects
  with no exterior cladding scope the driver is absent rather than small.
- Interior wood base. The same prefinished-against-field-painted lever applies inside the building.
  Deleting field-painted wood base in units and corridors in favor of prefinished base can be worth
  more than a tenth of a whole offer, which makes it the largest single value-engineering item in the
  trade and converts a painting scope into a millwork purchase.
- Exposed structure, deck and mechanical. Sometimes painted with a dryfall system, sometimes excluded
  outright, in roughly equal measure. Priced as a procedure rather than as a surface by some bidders,
  with dust removal, a solvent wipe of the mechanical and a single dryfall coat, sold in one color and
  sheen with a stated mobilization count and a sprinkler-head and miscellaneous-item covering
  assumption. Read a procedure stated in identical steps across projects as a bidder's house method
  rather than as a market convention, and check the letterhead before treating two sightings as two
  opinions.
- Millwork joints. Bidders assume the carpenter delivers tight, true miters that do not need caulk to
  fill, and some write the assumption as a set of demands on the carpenter with a dimensional bound of
  about an eighth of an inch. If the carpentry scope does not carry that, it lands back on the painter
  as extra work. This is the clearest cross-package assumption pair in the trade: several documents
  assume the miters and others exclude the corrective work by name with a threshold.
- Electrical, media and load panels. Expected prefinished; painting them is extra. An estimator sheet
  can require the opposite, with wireways, access panels, grills and electrical panels painted to match
  the adjacent surface.
- Painting before installation. A painter can paint exterior exhaust and louvers on the ground before
  the installing trade sets them, which is a shop-finish seam performed in the field and fits no
  custody mode cleanly.
- Priming for another package's product. A painter can prime a deck so a different trade can apply a
  sprayed insulation over it, with the next round replacing that prime-only line with an intumescent
  coating over the same insulation. Neither furnish nor install describes it.
- Lifts, staging, rigging and hoisting. Not settled, and the market answers at least five ways: lift
  costs excluded outright; lifts included for the painter's own work only; all rigging, hoisting,
  staging, ladders and lifts included; exterior staging provided by others with shared costs
  excluded; and the lift priced as its own add alternate or as a stated duration of supply. An
  operation has been seen giving two of those answers on two projects two days apart, so it is not
  even a house position. Ask for the answer rather than assuming a default.
- Removal and reinstallation of an exterior railing, assumed by others before and after the painter,
  with no package named.
- Plaster on renovation work. The painter's line is paint only, with patching, skim coating and
  fire-rating restoration belonging to the plaster or drywall contractor. Where an estimator carries a
  block of plaster rows the bidders answer all of them as paint only. On a conversion the pattern is
  more often a single large general contractor allowance instead of a row block.

### Drywall

The boundary with drywall is contested in both directions and it can collapse entirely.

- Preparation, patching and skim coating. Nearly every painting document that addresses this boundary
  excludes the drywall side by name: skim coating, wall patching, plastering, all taping and joint
  compound to level finishes, extensive drywall repair beyond minor touch-up, abrasive or excessive
  prep, sanding or mitering uneven joints, and drywall, masonry, concrete and wood patching or repair.
  Against that, painters routinely carry the light end as an inclusion: nail-hole filling, minor crack
  and dimple filling, sanding between coats, and patching of small damages before the finish coat. A
  minority go further and carry inspect-and-patch with joint compound, tape and sanding, bounded to a
  stated hour of labor.
- The boundary is the delivered finish level, not the activity. Some bidders draw it that way, excluding
  patching or a named level of finish, or excluding taping and skim coating joint compound to all level
  finishes, while others invert it and price standard preparation consistent with the specified drywall
  finish level. The painter's price is a function of what the drywall contractor delivers. Ask for it
  both ways, though, because most painting documents never name a finish level and a line written only
  as one may come back unanswered. A line written as a defect size and a percentage of surface is a
  question this market has been seen to answer in its own words, with an hourly rate beyond the
  threshold.
- The seam can collapse, and painting's own documents show it collapsing. An accounting-platform
  estimate can carry a drywall line and an interior painting line under one estimate number, with the
  painting line about a quarter of the combined total, and that painting line can reach the painting
  ranking as its lowest full-scope column with no painting proposal behind it in the bid folder at
  all. A metal framing and drywall contractor can bid the painting package outright and rank third of
  fifteen, carrying its drywall house terms into the painting bid and excluding all work at existing
  gypsum board and framing. Multi-division finishes houses selling drywall construction and painting
  from one firm draw the line differently from project to project. Any sheet that ranks such a bid
  against painting-only bids is ranking two different scopes.
- Common ownership does not merge the packages. A bidder that sells both drywall and painting on its
  own letterhead can bid painting alone and exclude patching and level-five finish by name, and a
  painting company can disclose a partnership with a named drywall company, in which case the
  exclusion routes to a sister firm rather than to a third party. That changes what the exclusion is
  worth without changing what it says.
- The estimator draws the boundary the other way. Scope rows obliging the painter to touch up at
  drywall, or to carry final touch-up painting with patching of minor damage to all gypsum before
  painting, appear on the majority of sheets that carry an operation block, and the bidders ranked
  against those rows exclude wall patching in their own documents. No plug reconciles them. That is a
  live cross-package assumption pair, not a settled seam.
- Wallcovering termination trims, fry reglets and corner trims. Carried by nobody, named by roughly one
  in ten of the documents that price wallcovering, routed to drywall only where someone writes it down,
  and excluded by name by drywall bidders too where the drywall side has been read. Put them on their
  own line with the counterparty named, because the market's own sentence is unstable.
- Level five at wallcovering locations is the finish the wallcovering installer's warranty depends on,
  and it is a drywall row wherever a sheet carries one. A deduct that removes the wallcovering also
  removes the reason level five and the trims exist, and no drywall column moves.
- Acoustic wallcovering marks can be routed to the gypsum-board package by section number by the
  estimator, while the drywall tab's own bidder cell says the marks sit with the painter. At the
  estimator's own rate a pair of such marks can be worth about a tenth of the painting package, and
  neither ranking surfaces the hole.
- On a renovation or conversion, patching of existing is excluded by the drywall bidder and by the
  painter both, and the money most often ends up as a general contractor allowance above both packages.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What painters actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The finish schedule and the room finish schedule. The primary document. Marks come from here, and
   most proposals cite marks or the schedule directly; a bidder can use the mark range itself as its
   entire scope statement. Estimator sheets paste the finish schedule and its coating notes in as
   scope rows verbatim.
2. The interior design package. On several projects this, and not the architectural set, is the
   governing document: bidders cite interior design sheets by number as their basis, and sheets paste
   interior design sheet references in as rows. Treat renderings and interior design sheets as contract
   documents. Be warned that the architectural schedule and the interior design schedule can name two
   different paint manufacturers on the same project, which a bidder resolves by pricing both under an
   or-approved-equal.
3. The dwelling or occupancy unit count. Every leveling sheet that ranks anything normalizes to a cost
   per unit and ranks on it, and a whole negotiation can be conducted by both sides in that currency.
   See the warning below.
4. The door schedule, for the doors-and-frames line and the wood-against-hollow-metal split.
5. Area and level organization, for the corridor, amenity, back-of-house and garage segmentation.
6. The specification, mostly for coat counts, the coating standard and the paint manufacturer. Bidders
   cite the section number and rarely quote anything from it, with the exception noted in section 4.

The normal method is area type plus unit count plus judgment. A room-by-room takeoff is unusual and
occurs mainly on renovation phases.

### The unit rate is the currency and it is a mismatched denominator

This is the most important warning in the section. Every sheet that ranks divides a whole-package
number by a residential unit count, while the numbers being divided cover basements, sub-basements,
mezzanines, stairs, garages, clubhouses, pumphouses, amenity, retail and commercial tenant space and
exterior work that no unit count measures. Where the coverage question is recorded at all, the
denominator fails to cover its numerator on about three sheets in four. A bidder has stated the problem
in writing, publishing its unit count, its residential unit area and its gross building area and adding
that none of them represents total paintable surface.

Four further ways the denominator breaks, each of which invalidates a comparison a reader would
otherwise make:

- It changes with building type. A hotel normalizes on keys, not dwelling units, so a cross-project
  rate that reads both as units compares a guestroom to an apartment. An office base building has no
  units at all, and a sheet built from another project's template can divide every bid by a residential
  unit count that does not describe the building and still order the ranking on the resulting rate.
- It does not cover the numerator on phased work. A three-phase total divided by one phase's unit count
  names its source correctly and is still wrong, and the same error can run through a whole-project cost
  overview for every division.
- It is contested inside one pool and inside one workbook. Bidders state different counts on one set, a
  bidder can change its own count between rounds two weeks apart, a ranking can normalize on a value
  none of them states, and a go-back plug can use a different denominator from the ranking in the same
  file. The ranking proceeds anyway.
- It is not homogeneous within one project. On a mixed tower and townhouse project, unrelated bidders
  price townhouse units at roughly one and three quarters to three and a half times their own tower
  rate, consistently, while the estimator's own premium is under a half again and the ranking divides
  every column by the combined count. A large, real intra-project rate difference is averaged away.

Where the set carries a unit schedule, bidder unit counts converge rather than diverge, and unrelated
bidders have been seen to enumerate the residential mix from the drawings and agree exactly in every
unit type. That convergence is the condition under which the unit denominator is safe.

### Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number

- Labor regime: union, open shop or prevailing wage, and residential against commercial rate
  classification underneath it. Record it; do not assume its direction.
- Whether spray application will be permitted and whether the sequencing will hold.
- The floor release schedule and the turnover sequence, which drive punch and remobilization.
- How many go-back hours per unit the general contractor expects, and on what basis. See below.
- The warranty duration required. The market default is one year, stated unprompted by a minority.
- Insurance basis, whether an owner-controlled program applies and the cost of conventional general
  liability if it does not, and bond cost. Both quoted as a percentage of contract value, rarely.
- Project portal and payment-application fees, which an estimator can carry as a scope row with the
  rate on the row: a percentage of contract value to a cap, plus a flat amount per sub-tier
  subcontractor.
- Whether a mock-up unit is expected.
- Whether trim and doors arrive primed, and whether the shop primer is compatible with the specified
  finish system.
- Whether millwork and casework arrive prefinished.
- Whether wallcovering is owner-furnished, and whether the schedule's marks are selected at all.
- Whether exterior cladding, siding, trim and railings arrive prefinished.
- Site access, staging, parking availability and free parking.
- Ceiling height and lift access. Where a bidder bands its rates by height, height is a rate variable
  and not just an access nuisance; the banding is a feature of a house estimating system rather than a
  market convention, and most proposals do not band at all.
- Occupied or phased work, and any restriction to normal business hours.
- Temporary heat and enclosure, and who provides the temperature and humidity the coatings require.
- Historic requirements and who is responsible for compliance.
- Whether a bidder's certifications are current, since they are usually asserted undated.
- Building certification regimes. A solicitation can name an energy-performance certification, a
  green-building certification level, a passive-house standard and a local hiring policy and ask every
  trade for a deduct alternate to a code-minimum alternative. Painting bidders answer none of them.
- On a conversion, the condition of the building itself. A bidder can exclude scope and project
  conditions not reflected in the drawings, including anything only a site walkthrough would reveal,
  which is an honest statement that the documents cannot describe the building.

### The go-back problem

Go-backs and punch remain the trade's most consistent gap: no bidder carries an hours-per-unit
allowance and, where a bidder states anything, it is a visit count rather than an hour count.

What has changed is the estimator's side, and the practical instruction with it. The go-backs row
appears on about a third of leveling sheets and touches about half of projects. A plug actually
reaches a column on about a quarter of projects, and the basis is not stable: four labor hours per
unit at a stated hourly rate is the commonest, two hours per unit occurs from the same office on a
different project, a flat sum per occupancy unit with no hours and no rate occurs on a hotel, and a
rate per square foot occurs on a renovation phase. The hourly rate applied differs between projects
as well. And the plug is not reliably uniform: it can be withheld from exactly the column where an
omitted uniform plug most changes the comparison, and a whole plug set can vanish between the
preconstruction round and buyout with nothing recording the decision.

So a painting scope line set must carry a go-backs and punch line with an explicit basis stated on the
line, and it must state which basis. A reader that assumes four hours per unit is assuming a practice
that is not consistent even inside one estimating office.

### Set-stage behavior

Set stage changes what a wallcovering line means, and painting is solicited as early as schematic
design.

At schematic design the set has no wallcovering marks at all, and the pool answers incompatibly: a
blank department on a bidder's own printed form, an invented coverage fraction of the amenity wall area
priced as labor and material, and silence. The estimator opens no wallcovering tab and writes no
wallcovering row. Treat a wallcovering line at schematic design as absent, not as an allowance carry.

At design development bidders openly price around missing information and say so. Door and frame types
are not shown, so an assumption is named. Wall heights, doors per unit and painted extent above the
ceiling line at rated partitions are stated as assumptions. Wallcovering is priced with the allowance
provided per finish schedule, or at a carried rate per linear yard rather than a quantity, sometimes
capped as a not-to-exceed. Rates carried that way span about twice across a market. Treat a
wallcovering line at design development as an allowance carry.

By construction documents and conformed sets the same bidders switch to citing mark ranges and counted
quantities.

Where the schedule leaves specific marks undesigned, the market will tell you which ones. Several
independent bidders flagging the same two marks in three different instruments, a rate per square foot
with a written explanation, a labor-only carry with no material picked, and a to-be-determined note on
a vendor's own shop drawing, is stronger evidence about the documents than any single disclosure.

A proposal's stated basis and its folder's set stage disagree far more than occasionally. A schematic
proposal can sit unchanged in a design-development folder; a bid can price a permit set eight months
older than the round; a pool of eleven can cite four different governing sets, one superseded by five
months and one naming a year that does not occur in the solicitation. Nothing on the leveling sheet
records that the pool is not pricing one set.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet for wall and ceiling area, banded by substrate. The priced unit in a quantified proposal
  and the basis for most estimator plugs.
- Banding by height, where a house estimating system carries it, with rows reading up to twelve feet,
  up to fifteen feet, and over twelve feet for columns. Rare, and a property of the estimating system
  rather than of the trade.
- Linear feet for base and running trim. Rare.
- Each for doors with frames and both faces, and for columns. Rare, and it survives into unit-type
  pricing where a bidder prices per bedroom count.
- Linear yards for wallcovering material, the supply chain's unit; square feet for installed
  wallcovering area, which is how the general contractor asks for it and is usually supplied by email
  on request; and the square yard, which appears as a finish-schedule allowance unit. Three
  incompatible units, per section 5.
- Locations or each for miscellaneous metal items such as guard rails, elevator door frames and access
  panels.
- Mobilizations, as a first-class production basis: a whole price stated on one mobilization, a
  mobilization per released area, a mobilization per unit, and stated rates for each additional one.
- Hours, for go-backs, punch and time-and-material repair, and occasionally against a single priced
  line. Bidders publish an hourly rate on the face of a proposal often enough to recover a market
  labor rate: a bidder publishing regular and off-hours rates per man-hour and a second bidder in the
  same pool publishing hour counts that divide to the first bidder's rate within cents is a
  cross-checkable figure from two independent documents.
- Crew size and duration in place of hours, which is recoverable to a crew-hour figure.
- Gallons of paint and of primer. Usually an internal rollup, but the whole material derivation where a
  bidder shows a cost model, with a coverage rate per gallon for primer and a different one per coat
  for finish.
- Dwelling units, keys or another occupancy unit, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package.
  Universal on the estimator side and used by almost no bidder.
- Gross square feet, where an office normalizes a painting package that way instead.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, for general liability insurance and for a project portal fee.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they actually cause:

1. What the number covers. Scope definition dominates everything else in this trade: the widest spreads
   sit where the documents cannot describe the building and the sheet asks the fewest questions, and
   the top of a range is usually a bidder carrying scope the rest excluded rather than a bidder who is
   expensive.
2. Total finished surface area, driven by unit count and by how much amenity, common and back-of-house
   area the project carries.
3. Whether spray application and clean sequencing are available. Named as the explicit basis of the
   competitive price by a minority, with additional charges stated otherwise.
4. Whether the exterior cladding is field painted, and whether interior wood base is field painted.
5. Coat count and product grade. Contractor-grade product and a three-coat system are the defaults, and
   the difference between one and two finish coats at ceilings lives only in the coat column.
6. Substrate mix. Masonry needs block filler, exposed concrete needs a masonry primer, metal needs a
   direct-to-metal system, existing plaster needs prep the painter will disclaim.
7. Whether wallcovering is in, out, owner-furnished or absent from the set. Its weight is the
   unpredictable part: the priced wallcovering block runs from under a fiftieth of a package to more
   than a third, driven by where the schedule puts the marks. A deduct alternate removing wallcovering
   and substituting painted accent walls can be worth almost the whole wallcovering segment, so it
   behaves as a nearly pure add-or-delete block whose size you cannot guess.
8. Prep level actually required, against the drywall finish level specified and delivered.
9. Ceiling height and lift access.
10. Number of colors and the presence of accent walls or stripes. An accent-color adder priced as a
    rate per square foot plus material recurs, but it recurs on one authored template travelling
    between differently named companies, so read it as a house clause rather than a market rate.
11. Go-back hours per unit, and the basis they are stated on.
12. Off hours, overtime, remobilization count and phasing.
13. Labor regime. Record it, do not rank on it, per section 4.
14. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.
15. The commercial basis of the printed total, below.

### The commercial basis is a leveling axis

Three conventions cut a painting price into labor and material, they wear similar names, and they
produce material shares that differ by a factor of about four. A separately stated paint-material line
runs about a tenth to an eighth of the total and leaves every other line in the bid net of material. A
whole-price labor and material split runs about three fifths labor and counts everything, not paint.
A direct-cost split inside a cost model puts material at about a fifth of direct cost because the
labor rate carries the prep. A per-unit rate taken off the residential line of the first understates by
that share, and any pass treating the three as one axis is wrong by that factor.

Sales tax on materials is a priced line in this market and it is applied inconsistently. Some bidders
break it out, some state it as included, most say nothing. A shared house form can tax the painting
department and not the wallcovering department in the same document. A bidder can tax several rows in
one solicitation and nothing at all in the next. A platform estimate can mark its single line
non-taxable. A house template can apply the identical percentage to the identical material line and
label it sales tax in one solicitation, mark up in the next and tax percentage in a third, with the
rate and the base holding and the meaning moving. And tax lines can leave the paper between rounds
at falling totals with no note. Establish whether tax is inside a printed total before ranking it; a
total-level comparison silently mixes tax-in and tax-out numbers.

Bidder margin is disclosed on the face of a bid more often than expected: a printed net profit
percentage beside a paint and materials total and a subcontracted labor total, an open-book overhead
block with a stated profit margin on direct cost, and a pre-markup rate per square foot published in an
email beside a selling rate about a third higher in the proposal.

### Dispersion, and what it does not prove

Within-project spread between the lowest and highest cost per unit runs from about one and a fifth to
about seven times. The wide end sits on conversions, on healthcare work and on schematic sets, where
the documents cannot describe the building and the sheet asks the fewest questions. The tight end sits
on the thinnest sheets and the smallest pools, where the spread is tight because nothing was asked, not
because the market agrees. Scope definition compresses spread more than competition does.

Pool depth is thinner than the trade's reputation. Distinct painting operations per project run from
two to about seventeen, with a median around nine; fewer than a fifth of projects draw twelve or more.
Ranking rows overstate the pool substantially and must never be used as a bidder count. A ranking row
can be a company, a round, an internal budget or target-value column that carries a labor type and a
full base bid and looks exactly like a bidder, an estimator-assembled combination of two companies
under one label, a reconstructed verbal quote, a duplicate of another column, a partial-scope specialty
vendor, a column carried forward from a superseded set and labelled with the old set, or a late bid.
One operation can occupy two rows under two names. A ranking commonly carries a quarter to three
quarters more rows than there are operations behind it, and has been seen to carry three times as
many.

Width has not been seen to buy a lower price. Where a project was re-bid at nearly three times the
pool on comparable scope, the low cost per unit moved less than one percent while the spread widened
by about half again, and none of the added bidders came in below the floor. That is one sighting and
not a law, but do not expect depth alone to move the bottom of a painting pool. What width does buy,
across every kind of pool seen, is the ability to tell boilerplate from project signal, to see
quantity divergence between bidders who publish quantities, and to set aside documents that are not
usable offers, which a thin pool cannot afford to do.

### The estimator does not reliably level

An estimator does not reliably compare base bid plus adjustments with plugs added to every bidder.
About a third of painting leveling sheets apply no plug to any column. Where plugs exist they are
commonly applied to some columns and withheld from others, and the unadjusted columns are frequently
the cheapest, so the top of the ranking is unleveled numbers compared against leveled ones.
Adjustments are also often not plugs at all but the bidder's own breakout moved out of its base into
a second column, which fires only for bidders who
happened to break the scope out and gives nothing to bidders who answered no to the same scope. A
computed plug can sit on its row and never reach the comparison. A whole plug set can vanish by buyout.

The consequences for a reader are direct. Never take a sheet's own adjusted ranking as comparable
without checking which columns were adjusted. Never assume a scope the sheet names has been plugged.
And expect a priced offer in the folder to be missing from the ranking entirely: unranked offers appear
on more than half of projects, and on several the unranked offer would have ranked first or within a
fraction of a percent of a ranked column. The mirror also occurs, with a ranked column carrying a base
bid and no document behind it anywhere in the folder, sometimes because the document exists filed under
another trade's folder and sometimes because there is no document at all.

Two further leveling hazards worth carrying into a scope line set. A stated alternate can be an add from
a base that excludes it and a deduct from a base that includes it in the same solicitation, so two
printed base bids are not the same scope and the lower printed base can be the higher offer once
normalized; check which side of a stated alternate each base sits on before comparing. And an
alternate's sign cannot always be read from its own words, since an add can be stated for work that
deletes scope.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file rests on 245 documents read across 22 projects in one regional market, all bid by one
general contractor, producing 245 proposal records, 35 leveling records and 3 answered scope sheets,
alongside 1,675 priced lines captured for a later cost pass. Its original calibration was four
multifamily projects and a few dozen proposals; those four projects contribute 85 of the 245
documents, and only 26 of those 85 were opened by the original calibration, so 219 of the 245
documents are evidence the first pass never saw.

The counts are computed from the per-proposal records by a stated method. Projects are counted as the
census slug on each record. Solicitation moments are counted as distinct project-and-round-folder pairs
in the corpus tree, which gives 32 across the 22 projects, because the set-stage field on a record is
free text: readers wrote the bidder's own cited basis into it, so clustering the free text alone
over-counts a project whose bidders each cite a different set. Set stage is used to name each moment,
not to count them. Leveling records are counted one per ranking table read, which is why a project
with two workbook copies or a trade split across tabs contributes more than one.

The evidence base by shape. Building types: fifteen multifamily podium projects, three
office-to-residential conversions, one historic residential conversion, one hotel, one office core and
shell, one phased healthcare project on prevailing wage. Bid years span five, from 2022 to 2026. Set
stages span schematic design, design development, permit, guaranteed maximum price, construction
documents, conformed sets, addendum rounds and buyout. Distinct company names on the proposals number
95, resolving to about 65 operations once name variants and disclosed aliases are collapsed; 35 of
those appear on more than one project and a handful appear on most of them, which is what makes the
boilerplate-against-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable. Bidder kinds run well past
painting contractors, per section 1.

What the corpus now shows that the earlier calibration could not:

- Renovation, conversion and existing-conditions work, on five projects rather than one.
- Building types outside multifamily residential: office core and shell, hotel, healthcare on
  prevailing wage, and office-to-residential conversion.
- Prevailing wage as a first-class labor class rather than a bidders' negative.
- Measured takeoffs, in several independent shapes rather than a single document, so the quantity
  conventions in section 7 no longer rest on one proposal.
- A project carrying no wallcovering at all, and a solicitation at schematic design where no
  wallcovering marks exist yet.
- Buyout-stage leveling as a distinct instrument from preconstruction leveling.
- Where intumescent coating goes when the painter excludes it, which the earlier calibration recorded
  as unknown.

What this file still cannot claim:

- It is one regional market and one general contractor. Nothing here is tested against another
  estimating office, and the sheet-side conventions in particular are one office's evolving habit.
  Where this file describes what an estimator does, read it as what this estimator does.
- No signed subcontract and no executed buyout is in evidence. Every leveling sheet carries a selected
  subcontractor and a carried value, and a buyout-stage sheet shows which exclusions were still open,
  which plugs were carried forward and how each excluded scope was routed. None of that is an award
  document, so which exclusions survived negotiation is still unknown.
- No change order history. The claim that the go-back and sequencing qualifications convert into change
  orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed in the field.
- The corpus has not shown interior wall protection systems as a priced package. Corner guards, crash
  rails and wall panels appear only as routing notes and as a vendor's cure condition.
- The corpus has not shown shop-applied against field-applied finishes on structural steel.
- No drywall, flooring, fireproofing, millwork or specialties document was read from the other side.
  Every counterparty in section 4 and every seam in section 5 is the painting side's own assertion.
  The one exception is part of the drywall seam, where the statements about what drywall bidders
  themselves carry and exclude rest on drywall bidders' own documents read separately rather than on
  any painting document. The routing of intumescent to fireproofing is asserted by an estimator and
  confirmed by no fireproofing document.
- Public and institutional prevailing-wage work rests on a single project, and office work on two. The
  claims in this file that are specific to those building types carry the weight of that.
- Almost no answered scope sheet comes back from a bidder anywhere: bidder answers exist as cells in
  the estimator's own workbook, often for some columns and never for all, and on several sheets in a
  single column. Where this file says the market answered something, it usually means the estimator
  recorded an answer.

A note on reading the documents themselves, because it changes what is knowable. Text extraction is
insufficient as a default in this trade. Proposal pages come back as character-shifted cipher with a
bound email thread extracting cleanly, so a text-only read returns a proposal with no thread, no
breakout and no certification claim. Spreadsheet-derived quotes extract their amounts in a column order
unrelated to their row labels, which on one itemized quote would have put a wallcovering material line
at roughly eight times its true value; only a render gives the correct pairing. A specialty vendor's
whole scope definition can be color-coded markups on sheets that extract to zero characters. A page
that extracts to zero characters must be checked for ink before it is called blank. Render anything
whose extracted text looks structurally thin, and render anything whose grain is a grid.

This file carries a structural gap list, in section 3, in three parts: what no bidder carries, what is
contested between packages, and what the general contractor carries outside every package. The first
part is a specification for what a scope sheet should ask, not a description of what these leveling
sheets actually plug.
