# Final cleaning

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the final cleaning
package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings, though the drawings will help less here than in any other
trade. Final cleaning is priced almost entirely off things the documents do not contain: how many
times the building gets cleaned, how the exterior glass is reached, and whether anyone else is still
working when the cleaner arrives.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Final cleaning is not a trade package in the ordinary sense. It lives in the general requirements
division, and the leveling tabs that carry it say so on their face: one is titled as carried in the
general requirements, another records its section number as not found on the cost summary, and on a
third project the general requirements tab holds a single lump with no cleaning rows and no
subcontractor column. On a fourth project, eight cleaning proposals sat in the bid folders across
three rounds with no leveling tab at all.

The practical consequence is that this scope is bought as a jobsite requirement rather than
solicited as a trade, and it gets a scope sheet only when an estimator decides to make one. That is
why the boundaries in section 4 are so poorly settled.

Four kinds of company bid it and the kind predicts the shape of the offer.

- The dedicated post-construction cleaning company. Sells passes, publishes hourly rates, and
  carries a long boilerplate body about supplies, equipment, supervision and insurance.
- The demolition and site services contractor. Sells final cleaning alongside demolition,
  environmental remediation, labor support and disposal. These firms write the most granular area
  lists in the market and are also the most likely to be union.
- The janitorial company. Prices a recurring service agreement rather than a one-time construction
  clean, sometimes with material outside the number entirely.
- The general trades or specialty subcontractor with a cleaning arm, which prices a paragraph of
  prose and one figure.

Collapse attractors:

- Interior window and glass cleaning. Effectively always inside the base.
- Appliance protective coating and film removal, and in some proposals appliance assembly.
- Sticker, tag, label and adhesive removal throughout.
- Elevator cab cleaning.
- Back of house, mechanical, electrical, telecommunications and storage room cleaning.
- Garage sweeping or washing, though see section 4 for why this is the trade's worst-defined row.

Specialty carve-outs:

- Exterior glass. Detachable almost everywhere, and the single largest optional block. See section 5.
- Roof washing, quoted as an add.
- Carpet cleaning, floor stripping, waxing and refinishing, and pressure washing, which one bidder
  lists as available with price quoted on request, meaning it advertises scope it has not priced.
- Owner furniture cleaning, excluded by name where anyone thinks of it.

## 2. How the market bids

There is no house style. The corpus holds five distinct proposal shapes for one scope, and that is
itself the finding.

- A prose paragraph and one number, with no exclusions at all. Six sentences naming spaces and
  surfaces. Every boundary open.
- A numbered operation list, running to a dozen paragraphs describing what gets wiped, vacuumed,
  mopped and scraped, with one number under it.
- An area list, walking the building floor by floor and room by room by function, with one number.
  The demolition contractors write these and they are the most informative documents in the trade.
- A pass list, pricing an initial rough clean, a main detail clean and a touch-up separately, with an
  hourly rate card for anything beyond.
- A segmented bid, splitting units, common areas and exterior windows into separate figures with a
  crew day rate for extra passes. Only one bidder in the corpus does this and it is the only bid
  whose additional passes are priceable in advance.

The pass count is the largest scope variable in the trade and it is invisible in a lump sum. The
same bidder priced three passes on one project and one pass on another, said so on the price line
both times, and ranked lowest on both leveling sheets. A single-pass bid against three-pass
competitors is not a cheap bid, it is a different product, and only the price line says so.

What a proposal will reliably state:

- The spaces covered, at some level of granularity.
- Whether power and water must be working, and that all loose debris must be picked up first.
- An hourly or crew day rate for additional cleaning, in most proposals.
- Whether the labor is union or open shop, and the estimator records it for every bidder anyway.

What a proposal will reliably not state:

- Any area quantity. One bidder in the whole corpus stated the garage area in square feet and no
  bidder stated any other area.
- The number of passes, unless it happens to itemize them.
- Which basis document it read. Several proposals cite no drawings, no specification and no set. One
  was priced off a site tour and a conversation and referenced the bidder's conduct on a named prior
  project as the standard.
- Crew size or duration.

Revision habits:

- The commonest revision is a silent reprice. A five page proposal reissued nine months later with
  twelve numbered operation paragraphs byte-identical, one apartment fewer in the stated count, and
  an hourly rate about fourteen percent higher.
- The second commonest is a readdressing. One document in the corpus is addressed to a competing
  general contractor entirely, and the later version is the same proposal with the addressee changed
  and its phase breakout block deleted. The earlier, wrongly addressed document is the more
  informative one.
- Alternates arrive and inclusions leave at an unchanged base. One pair of rounds shows a furniture
  dusting line disappearing from the alternates block and a boom lift window alternate appearing,
  with the base price and every qualification identical.

Diff the scope sentences and the pass count. A total diff in this trade is nearly always zero when
something real has moved.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Cleaning scope sheets in the corpus have run three, five and nineteen rows, which is far too few.
Target roughly twenty-five to thirty-five lines. The organizing axis is pass first, then area, then
operation.

### What enumerates

- Each pass as its own line: rough or initial clean, main or detail clean, and touch-up or powder
  puff after the punch list. Give the touch-up line a stated hours-per-unit basis, because one
  bidder states two hours per unit and that is the only production figure the trade publishes.
- Each area type as its own line: residential units, corridors, stairwells, lobbies and mail rooms,
  amenity spaces, back of house and mechanical rooms, basement, garage, retail or commercial space,
  roof and terraces, and any sub-basement. Sub-basements get excluded by name when anyone notices
  them.
- Interior glass as its own line, separate from exterior.
- Interior of exterior windows, mullions and sills as its own line, separate again from exterior
  face. Bidders draw the line exactly here and a single windows row will always be ambiguous.
- Exterior glass as its own line with the access method named. See section 5.
- The access method itself as its own row, because it is the price.
- Garage cleaning as its own line with the method named, sweeping or power washing. See section 4.
- Roof washing as its own line.
- Protection removal as its own line, split by what is protected: floor protection, wall and
  countertop protection, and film on exterior windows. Every bidder answers this differently.
- Appliance work as its own line, covering shipping material removal, protective film, and assembly.
  Assembly of appliances is real scope that one bidder includes.
- Elevator cabs, doors, floors and frames as their own line.
- Owner furniture and equipment in common areas as its own line.
- Phasing and remobilization as its own line with a count, because a corridor cleaned in phase one
  and recleaned in phase two is priced separately by the one bidder who thinks about it.
- A time and materials rate line, hourly or crew day, for everything beyond the signed-off scope.
- Trash rooms and interior signage as their own line before turnover.
- Bike rooms and bike racks as their own line.

### What collapses

- The individual operations inside a room type collapse to the room type. Nobody prices dusting
  apart from wiping.
- Fixtures, cabinets, countertops, drawers, shelving and hardware collapse into the room they sit in.
- Unit by unit enumeration collapses into a unit count.
- Supplies, equipment, supervision and insurance collapse into a general line. Every bidder carries
  all of them.

### The include or exclude click test

The test fires hardest on the pass count, on exterior glass and its access, on protection removal,
on the garage method, and on the roof. It fires hardly at all on the in-unit cleaning operations,
which every bidder describes near identically.

Silence is a finding. A proposal with no exclusion list is not offering broader scope; it is leaving
every boundary open, and there are more open boundaries in this trade than in most. Two of the
proposals in the corpus carry no exclusions at all and both put exterior glass inside the base with
no access method named, which is the one variable that moves this trade most.

### The structural gap list

Scope no cleaning bidder reliably carries:

- Additional passes after the ones bid, including everything caused by other trades returning.
- Exterior glass where it is not explicitly priced, and the lift or staging to reach it.
- Roof and terrace cleaning.
- Removal of excessive paint overspray, silicone, caulk, grout and adhesive, which is universally
  time and materials.
- Removal of floor, wall and countertop protection, contested between bidders.
- Overtime, off hours and premium time.
- Owner furniture cleaning.
- Recleaning caused by continuing construction, universally tracked on time and materials.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

The qualifications matter more than the exclusions in this trade, because the qualifications are all
conditions on sequence and site readiness, and violating them converts the lump sum into hourly work.

The one qualification that appears in essentially identical wording across unrelated competitors is
that all pricing is contingent on the cleaner being the last subcontractor to enter the premises, and
that any subcontractor work not completed beforehand will be cleaned as an extra. That is house
terms, not a bidder position, and it is also the truest statement in the trade. Final cleaning is
priced on an assumption about the schedule that the schedule almost never honours.

The other recurring bases:

- All areas broom cleaned by others first, and all loose debris picked up before the cleaner starts.
- Power and running water available in every unit at the time of cleaning, and functioning electrical
  outlets.
- Units signed off daily.
- Normal weekday construction hours, with overtime and overnight work excluded.
- No removal of light fixtures for interior surface cleaning.
- Minimal or no over-spray removal.
- Product specific cleaning and maintenance sheets to be provided, with the bidder disclaiming any
  responsibility for improper cleaning if they are not.
- Defects to be reported in writing within a short window after completion.

| Excluded by the cleaner | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Excessive paint, grout, silicone, caulk and adhesive removal | Time and materials, or back to the trade that caused it |
| Removal of floor, wall and countertop protection | Contested; some cleaners include it and others exclude it by name |
| Film on exterior windows | Contested between the cleaner and the glazing package |
| Exterior glass and the access to reach it | A separate alternate, or unassigned |
| Roof and terrace work | A separate alternate, or unassigned |
| Owner furniture and equipment | Owner or general contractor |
| Sub-basement and other named areas | Frequently unassigned |
| Overtime, premium time and out of sequence work | General contractor |
| Dumpsters, water, power, toilets and permits | General contractor |

What is genuine project signal:

- An exclusion whose stated reason is a discrepancy in the drawings. One bidder excluded exterior
  handrail glass and wrote that the prints disagree about whether it exists. That is a request for
  information wearing an exclusion's clothes.
- A note that the bid is preliminary and may be revised after a walk through of the property. That
  is a gate on the number, not a courtesy.
- A method named where the documents name none. The garage is the standout case and it deserves its
  own paragraph.

The garage is the trade's worst-defined row. The documents never say how a garage is to be cleaned,
and the answers split between sweeping and power washing. On one project the estimator wrote a
leveling row specifically to capture the method, having learned it was undefined, and the two answers
that came back differed by roughly a factor of five on the same garage, with a third bidder's power
washing figure sitting between them. That row alone can be a tenth of the package. Any cleaning scope
line set must name the method, and if the documents do not fix it, that is an upstream question and
not a leveling problem.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This trade has no furnish and install seam in the usual sense; every bidder furnishes all supplies
and equipment. Its seams are sequence seams and liability seams, and they are the whole story.

Exterior glass and its access is the seam that matters most, because it is the largest optional block
and its price is entirely an access decision the documents do not make. Four access positions appear
in the corpus:

1. Cleaned from the inside, where the window type allows it. The cheapest by a wide margin, and one
   bidder states that assumption explicitly as the basis of its exterior window price.
2. Cleaned from roof davits at all areas, stated as the basis by another bidder.
3. Cleaned from a boom lift of a stated height, quoted as an add alternate. This runs from roughly a
   fifth to roughly a third of a base bid, and it carries its own assumptions: that all sides are
   accessible and that the surrounding paving is rated for the lift's weight.
4. Cleaned with a lift rental billed per mobilization, so that a second visit is a second charge.
   Several bidders state that the lift cost covers one mobilization only and that a second changes
   the price.

So the same exterior window scope has been quoted across comparable buildings at a range of roughly
five or six to one, and the whole range is access method. Decide the access before soliciting, or the
line is unlevelable.

The other seams:

- Glazing. A cleaner will disclaim scratches caused by fabricating debris left by the glass
  manufacturer, refuse to use razor blades, and require that protection stay on the glass for the
  project duration. Meanwhile several cleaners exclude removing the film from exterior windows. Both
  ends of that seam need to be assigned or the protection stays on at turnover.
- Flooring and countertops. Protection removal is contested. One cleaner includes floor protection
  removal by name, another excludes peeling of protection, a third excludes removal of flooring, wall
  and countertop protection. Assign it.
- Appliances. Protective coating removal is universal, but assembly of appliances is carried by one
  bidder and by nobody else, so it is a real gap where the appliance package does not cover it.
- Demolition and site services. The same firms sell both, which means final cleaning and the
  demolition turnover scope frequently sit with one company and their boundary is a commercial
  question rather than a scope one.
- Painting and drywall. Overspray, caulk and grout removal is universally time and materials, which
  in practice means it becomes a back-charge conversation with whoever left it.
- Property management. The touch-up or powder puff pass happens after the property management punch
  list, so the pass count depends on a walk-through that is not on any construction schedule.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

This is the section that carries the trade, because the documents contribute almost nothing.

What the bidder actually prices from:

1. The unit count. This is the trade's denominator and effectively its only quantity.
2. The floor plans, for the common area, back of house and amenity extent.
3. A site tour, in at least one case instead of any documents at all.
4. Nothing else. No specification is cited by any proposal in the corpus. Several cite no drawing
   set at all.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- How many passes are expected. The single largest variable, and worth a factor of three between a
  one-pass and a three-pass bid.
- How the exterior glass will be reached, worth five or six to one on that line.
- How the garage is to be cleaned, worth about five to one on that row.
- Union or open shop labor. This trade is the one place in the cluster where the labor basis reliably
  predicts rank: on both projects with a real bid list, the two most expensive bidders were the two
  union ones, at roughly double the open shop cluster per unit.
- Whether the bidder is carrying multi-year labor escalation inside its base. One union bidder states
  that its price includes anticipated labor rate increases through a winter roughly four years out,
  which is part of why the union numbers sit where they do and which has nothing to do with
  productivity.
- Whether the cleaner will genuinely be last, and how much rework the real sequence will cause.
- Whether the units will be released in phases, and whether corridors will need recleaning after
  each phase.
- Whether an owner controlled insurance program applies, quoted as a flat add.
- Whether the roof needs washing.
- Whether protection will still be in place when the cleaner arrives, and whose job it is to take it
  off.
- Whether minority, women or veteran owned business participation is required. Several bidders in
  this trade lead with those certifications, so the participation requirement shapes the bid list
  itself.
- Whether the building is occupied or phased during turnover.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Residential units. The denominator for the whole package and the basis every leveling sheet in the
  corpus normalizes on.
- Passes, meaning cleaning visits. The trade's real production unit and almost never stated.
- Hours per unit, for the touch-up pass. One bidder states two hours per unit.
- Hourly rates for laborer and foreman, published by most of the dedicated cleaning companies.
- A crew day rate for a stated crew size, published by one bidder, which is the only form in which
  an additional pass can be priced before it is needed.
- Square feet, for the garage only, and stated by exactly one bidder.
- Mobilizations, for lift rental.
- Percent of contract value for bond, rarely.

Cost drivers, roughly in order:

1. Pass count.
2. Union versus open shop labor, worth roughly a factor of two.
3. Unit count and the amount of common and amenity area.
4. Whether exterior glass is in, and by what access method.
5. Whether the garage is swept or washed.
6. How much rework the sequence causes, which nobody prices and everybody bills.
7. Whether protection removal is in.
8. Multi-year labor escalation carried inside the base on long projects.
9. Overtime, phasing and remobilization count.
10. Roof, terrace and other exterior areas.

On spread, and no amounts are given here. The per unit spread on a single project has run about
three to one, and on another project about three and a quarter to one. Neither figure is a
competitiveness signal. On both projects the top of the range is occupied by the union bidders and
the bottom by a single-pass bid or by a round quoted months before every other round on the sheet. In
the middle of one project's list, three open shop bidders from the same six week window landed within
about three percent of each other per unit, which looks like a tight, well-defined market. It is not.
Those same three answered the garage method row three different ways, one of them priced a garage
line at a fifth of another's, and none of them stated a pass count. Their agreement on the total is
agreement about how much a cleaning company charges per unit, not agreement about what work is
included. Convergence on price and convergence on scope are entirely independent here, and this trade
converges on price while agreeing on almost nothing.

One further convention. Because the sheet is usually thin or absent, an estimator does not compare
base bids in this trade so much as pick a number and carry it in the general requirements. That means
the plugs in section 3 rarely get added at all, and the scope that gets missed at bid gets bought
later at hourly rates. A cleaning scope line set that names the pass count, the exterior access
method and the garage method has removed most of that risk before it exists.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on about twenty-two cleaning proposals from thirteen companies across four
multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor, plus two
usable estimator leveling tabs and two projects where the trade has effectively no sheet at all. The
proposals span design development, permit, construction documents and conformed rounds, and several
companies appear on more than one project, which is what makes the boilerplate and project signal
distinction in section 4 testable. One proposal was an image only scan and one was addressed to a
different general contractor.

The corpus is broad on bidders and thin on evidence, and the file is written to that.

- The pass count finding is the best evidenced claim here. It rests on one bidder's own two projects
  where the pass count is stated on the price line and the ranking follows it, corroborated by three
  other bidders whose pass structures differ. Treat it as solid.
- The exterior access range rests on four bidders across two projects quoting four different access
  methods, but no two of them quoted the same building by two methods, so the five or six to one
  range is assembled across projects rather than measured on one. The direction is certain; the
  magnitude is an estimate.
- The garage method finding rests on one project, where the estimator happened to write a row for it.
  The roughly fivefold gap is two priced answers and one partial third.
- The union premium of roughly double rests on two projects with two union bidders each. It is
  consistent across both, which is more than most claims here can say, but four bidders is four
  bidders.
- The hours-per-unit touch-up basis comes from one bidder on one project.
- Only one bidder in the corpus segments its bid or publishes a crew day rate, so everything in
  section 7 about pricing additional passes in advance rests on that single company.
- Everything is multifamily residential turnover work. Nothing is tested against commercial fit-out,
  healthcare, laboratory or food service cleaning, where regulated final cleaning standards,
  certification requirements and specialized surface protocols apply and none of which appear here.
- No project in the corpus had a specification section for cleaning that any bidder cited. Whether a
  properly specified cleaning scope would change bidder behaviour is untested, and it is the most
  interesting open question in this trade.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout appears, so nothing shows which of the many open
  boundaries survived negotiation.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that this trade's qualifications convert into
  hourly work is read from the proposals' own language and from the near-universal last-subcontractor
  clause, not observed in the field. It is the claim most in need of field verification and it is
  also the one an estimator is most likely to believe already.
- The corpus does not show what happens when the general contractor self-performs final cleaning,
  which the general requirements framing suggests is common and which no document here records.
