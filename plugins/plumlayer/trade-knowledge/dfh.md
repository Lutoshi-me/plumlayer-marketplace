# Doors, Frames and Hardware

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the doors, frames and
hardware package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling
sheets. This file speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the
words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real suppliers and real installers can bid it and a
real estimator can level it.

One warning before anything else. This is not one trade. It is a material supply trade and a labor
trade that the documents describe as a single package, and most of the difficulty in scoping it comes
from that. Every rule below is downstream of that fact.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Three specification sections anchor the package: hollow metal doors and frames, flush architectural
wood doors, and door hardware. Proposals cite them by number routinely, and they cite the door
schedule sheet by number even more often. A fourth body of scope, the commodity or residential
prehung assemblies that carry the unit interior doors, frequently has no specification section of its
own, and bidders write their own headings for it. That gap matters, because on a residential project
the prehung assemblies are the largest single count in the package and the least specified.

Suppliers also quote against a coarser pair of billing sections, one for hollow metal and one for
wood, which do not match the specification numbers. Treat the section number as a weak identifier and
the product class as the strong one.

The package as bought in the market is doors, frames and hardware together, as one buy, from one
counterparty. An estimator carries it on a single leveling tab, and windows, storefront and glazing,
coiling and overhead doors, and shower enclosures each get their own tab. That single tab is doing a
great deal of work, because the bidders answering it are not all selling the same thing.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another section:

- Standing and running trim: door casing, unit base, interior window stool and apron, unit entry back
  panels. Genuinely contested. One bidder titles its whole proposal doors, trim and hardware and
  prices the trim in linear feet; another states plainly that casing, trim and veneer wraps belong to
  the carpentry division; most say nothing and quote casing only where it comes attached to a prehung
  assembly. Casing at hollow metal frames is excluded by nearly everyone, and nobody obviously picks
  it up.
- Electrified and access control hardware: power supplies, door contacts, controllers, power
  transfers, electric strikes, electrified locksets and panic hardware, card readers. Almost every
  bidder pushes these to the electrical or security divisions. One carries the whole set inside the
  door package while excluding all of its installation, and one names the smart lock brands
  specifically and assigns them out to access control. The same item lands in three different
  packages depending on who is bidding.
- Smart unit entry locks with their fob and software ecosystem. Usually carried by the door supplier,
  sometimes as its own separate number, occasionally assigned out entirely. Setup, commissioning and
  installation of the locks are excluded by nearly everyone who furnishes them.
- Automatic door operators. Furnished by the door supplier commonly, installed by the door supplier
  rarely, wired by the electrician always, and commissioned by nobody named in any proposal.
- Barn and sliding door hardware. Usually inside the package, occasionally excluded outright with the
  door slabs excluded alongside it.
- Miscellaneous accessory installation, where the same installer who hangs the doors also receives
  and installs bath accessories, extinguisher cabinets and mailboxes.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like doors:

- Aluminum, storefront and curtain wall doors, frames and hardware. Excluded by every bidder, without
  exception, and assigned to glazing. This is the most reliable boundary in the trade.
- Glass and glazing at hollow metal and wood doors. Excluded by nearly everyone, though a few include
  glazing at specific door types and say so explicitly, and a bidder will add it on request.
- Overhead, coiling and garage doors, and access doors and panels. Excluded routinely, the access
  panels usually assigned to mechanical.
- Elevator smoke containment curtains. Bid by a single-product manufacturer direct, with lead times
  running roughly half again longer than the door suppliers quote.
- Integrated door assemblies, where a proprietary door, frame and hardware unit is scheduled as one
  product. The supplier quotes it to the intent of the specification and folds the manufacturer's own
  installer into the price, so a handful of openings flip the package mode all by themselves.
- Vault doors and utility company openings. Excluded, sometimes on the stated ground that no jamb
  detail exists.
- Balcony, patio and French doors. Excluded routinely and usually assumed aluminum.
- Shower and bath enclosures, which sit in the specialties division and bid separately.

One further packaging shape worth knowing. An interior finishes supplier may bid doors as one chapter
of a bundled fit-out estimate that also covers casework, stone, flooring, tile, carpet and base, with
supply and installation priced as separate chapters. Such a bid is not comparable to a door package
bid without work, and it will sit at an unrepresentative place in the ranking.

## 2. How the market bids

The first thing to establish about any proposal in this trade is what it is selling. There are three
answers, and they are not variants of one thing.

- Furnish only. The clear majority. A distributor or supplier quotes material delivered to the
  curb, and installation is not merely excluded but declared impossible under the terms of sale.
- Furnish and install. A minority, and the bidders offering it are typically carpentry contractors
  with a hardware division rather than distributors.
- Install only. A finish carpenter, usually filed by the general contractor under the carpentry
  division rather than under doors, pricing labor and fasteners against material somebody else
  supplies.

Several bidders offer both, with installation as a priced add alternate on a furnish base. A bidder
can also change mode between rounds, and does: a furnish-and-install bidder may strip all labor and
rebid as furnish only, then return to furnish and install a round later. When that happens the
document is edited by prefixing the words all installation to the first line of each existing
exclusion block and leaving everything else untouched, so the mode change is easy to miss.

Unlike the trades that quote a number and a bullet list, this one quotes a bill of materials. Nearly
every proposal states counts by product class: hollow metal frames, hollow metal leaves, wood doors,
prehung assemblies, hardware sets. That is the trade's native habit, and it means quantities are
usually available. What varies is depth.

- Segmented into three to nine numbers, most commonly by specification section or product class, and
  next most commonly by area (base building, amenity, garage, or units against common areas). The
  usual case.
- A single lump sum with the counts stated only as clarifications. Common among the smaller and the
  budget-stage proposals.
- Priced by door mark, with an extended price per mark. Rare, and the bidders who do it are not
  distributors.
- A full opening-by-opening takeoff replicating the door schedule columns, or a unit-type by floor by
  mark quantity matrix. Rare, but it happens on the largest projects, and a bidder who does it will
  say the counts are its own rather than the documents'.
- One line and one number with no scope statement whatsoever. This is the install-only degenerate
  case, and it is the shape a leveling sheet is least able to absorb.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Counts by product class, nearly always.
- The basis documents by name and date, nearly always, and often distinguishing the plans, the
  specification sections, the door schedule sheet, addenda and RFIs as separate dated rows. Some
  proposals record explicitly that there were no addenda, no RFIs, no scope sheet and no project
  schedule, which is itself useful information.
- Manufacturers for hollow metal, wood doors and hardware, commonly, and usually either as the
  specified maker or as a named equal.
- Lead times by product class, commonly, and stated as ranges in weeks. Shop drawings run longest to
  approve, hollow metal and wood doors run comparably, finish hardware and keyed cylinders run
  longest to deliver, and specialty products longer still. One supplier will quote factory lead times
  roughly half those of its competitors on the same project, so this is a real differentiator and not
  boilerplate.
- Delivery counts and sometimes a delivery plan by truck size, load count and floors per load. Some
  bidders make the number of deliveries an explicit priced attribute of each scope block.
- Sales tax, as its own line, very commonly, because material supply is taxable and installation
  labor generally is not. A supply-only number without a tax line is not a cheaper number.
- Commercial terms that are really scope: no retainage, material purchase order only with subcontract
  agreements refused, a material deposit before release, a submittals and engineering charge, credit
  approval, a cap on the number of submittals included, and a price validity window.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- A hardware schedule reconciled to the door schedule. Bidders count sets and report the total; they
  do not show the mapping. Set totals from different bidders on the same schedule land within a few
  percent of each other while the frame and door counts diverge much more, which tells you the sets
  are being counted off the same list and the openings are not.
- Who installs the hollow metal frames. Excluded by essentially everyone, including bidders who
  install everything else.
- Keying and master key system responsibility beyond a note that cores must be returned.
- Commissioning of anything electrified.
- Field measurement, excluded by nearly all, with dimensions released on submittal approval instead.
- Storage, hoisting and offload. Tailgate delivery is the near-universal assumption.
- Warranty duration, unless the specification is being excluded paragraph by paragraph, in which case
  the warranty paragraph is usually one of the excluded ones.
- Bond, which is quoted as a percentage adder if requested, rarely.

Exclusion behavior is uniformly heavy in this trade, which is the opposite of the trades where a
short list signals a thin bid. Lists of thirty to fifty exclusions are ordinary. A distinctive habit
is excluding specification requirements by citation: section, page, part and subparagraph, without
describing what is being excluded. Read those. They are where inspection requirements, field quality
control, protection obligations, warranty terms, ceiling struts and drill-and-tap obligations get
removed, and none of them are visible from a scope description.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs and triples:

- Price moves with scope text byte-identical. The cleanest example in the corpus moves eleven numbers
  by roughly a tenth downward, changes nothing else, and documents no reason.
- Breakout restructuring, where the total barely moves but individual items are pulled out as their
  own numbers. Automatic operators, aluminum door hardware and the smart lock package are the items
  most often extracted this way. The information is in what became visible, not in the total.
- A value engineering round issued as its own document alongside the base, so both are live at once
  rather than one superseding the other. Value engineering items can then migrate into the base bid
  on a later round without announcement.
- A mode change, as described above.
- A scope sheet answer, where the general contractor issues an enumerated scope sheet and the bidder
  returns it answered row by row, sometimes bound into the back of its own proposal.
- Constructability blocks, which are neither inclusion nor exclusion. A bidder reports how it
  resolved ambiguity: pricing a complete opening wherever only one component was marked existing to
  remain, declining to price hardware alternates because the sets were too thin to substitute
  against, or inferring a frame type from the assigned hardware set and the fire rating. These blocks
  are the highest-value paragraphs in the trade and no scope sheet asks for them.

Two consequences for a reader. First, a revision here is more often a package restructure than a
reprice, so compare the shape before comparing the number. Second, revisions frequently arrive by
email with attachments rather than as a replacement document, and a bid may be a supplier's quote
addressed to an entirely different general contractor, priced off a different plan date, that reached
this bid folder by some other route.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets in this trade have run 9, 60, 76 and 78 rows. Target roughly sixty to eighty scope lines
for a full multifamily doors, frames and hardware package. The nine-row sheet is a design development
artifact with two bidders on it, and it could not distinguish anything.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Package mode, as the first rows of the sheet: supply, installation, sales tax. Every estimator
  sheet in the corpus opens with exactly these, and installation is always its own row, never an
  attribute of the supply row. This is the single most important structural rule in the trade.
- Product class: hollow metal frames, hollow metal door leaves, wood doors, prehung assemblies,
  hardware sets. The primary organizing axis in every proposal and every estimator sheet.
- Location, wherever the product or the access changes: unit entry, unit interior, common area,
  corridor, amenity, stairwell, garage, back of house, exterior. The secondary axis, and the one that
  drives the area breakouts a bid form asks for.
- Frame construction and type: welded against knockdown, the nailer or slip-on profile used at unit
  entry, thermal break at exterior, masonry profile, concealed or trimless. These change price
  materially and they change who installs the frame.
- Gauge and coating, split interior against exterior. Bidders enumerate these as separate rows and
  the substitution between them is a live value engineering item.
- Door core, solid against hollow, at the unit interior. A recurring alternate in its own right, and
  the deduct for going all hollow core is small enough that it is usually not worth the argument.
- Door height by floor. First-floor doors are commonly taller than upper-floor doors, and the
  alternate to make all floors tall is one of the largest single alternates in the trade. Price it as
  its own line, and expect its labor component to exceed its material component by a wide margin.
- Every hardware product category as its own row: butt hinges, locksets, smart locks, exit devices,
  closers, track closers, pulls, stops, overhead stops, thresholds and gasketing and seals, and power
  operators. An estimator levels the specified manufacturer against the acceptable equals category by
  category, because brand substitution is the primary value engineering lever in this package. This
  is the trade's distinctive grain and it has no analogue in the other finish trades.
- Cylinders, keying, master key system and construction cores as their own line.
- Electrified hardware and the access control interface as their own line, with the boundary stated:
  who furnishes, who installs, who wires, who commissions.
- Automatic door operators, split four ways: furnish, install, electrical connection, commissioning.
  No proposal in the corpus carries all four, and most carry only the first.
- Hollow metal frame installation as its own line, separate from door installation. It is excluded by
  every supplier and by every furnish-and-install bidder, and the finish carpenter offers it as a
  priced add alternate rather than carrying it in a base bid. If your scope list does not have this
  row, nobody is setting the frames.
- Glazing, split by where it occurs: at hollow metal openings, at wood doors, at barn or sliding
  doors. Bidders answer these differently within one proposal.
- Trim and casing by condition: casing attached to prehung assemblies, unit entry casing, common area
  door casing, casing at hollow metal frames, jamb and casing at barn and bifold openings, unit base,
  window stool and apron. Each is answered differently by different bidders, and several are excluded
  by everyone.
- Ratings as their own rows where they exist: fire rating, temperature rise, sound transmission,
  smoke. Temperature rise in particular is excluded by most bidders on the argued ground that a
  sprinklered building does not require it, which is a code position the design team may not share.
- Existing conditions on renovation work, as a block: new hardware sets on existing doors,
  modifications to existing doors and frames, reuse of existing openings, repaint or restain or
  retain of existing doors. On a renovation project this block alone runs several rows, it is
  excluded by nearly every bidder, and the estimator ends up carrying the whole of it as a plug.
- Green and certification requirements: forest certification, low emission material, recycled
  content. Bidders decline these individually and by product, and a manufacturer may simply not
  certify, which converts a specification requirement into an exclusion nobody can close.
- Submittals: shop drawings, hardware submittals, the number of resubmittals included, and the
  hardware schedule itself.
- Deliveries, phasing, offload, hoisting and storage as their own block. Tailgate delivery is assumed
  by essentially everyone, and hoisting is excluded by essentially everyone.
- Lead time by product class, as a line with weeks on it, because a supplier's lead time can
  supersede the construction schedule and one bidder says so outright.
- Sales tax as its own line.
- Field measurement and dimension verification as its own line.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual openings collapse into door type or mark. No document prices a single opening except the
  one full takeoff, and even that one groups by tag.
- Gauge, coating, core, finish and rating are attributes of a product-class line, not separate lines,
  except where the gauge or coating split is itself the interior against exterior split.
- The contents of a hardware set collapse into the set. Sets are counted and priced as sets
  everywhere; only the product category behind them gets its own row, and only for brand leveling.
- Floors collapse into a range unless the door height or the finish actually changes at a floor. First
  floor against upper floors is a real break; floor by floor is not.
- Hinge, ball catch and other prehanger hardware supplied as part of a prehung assembly collapses
  into the assembly. Bidders state this explicitly and it is not contested.
- Colour and finish selection collapses into an attribute, though a finish change across all hardware
  is worth an alternate line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied here,
the test fires hardest on the seams in section 5, on the hardware product categories in this section,
and on anything electrified.

The test also has a second, sharper form in this trade, because the package mode question sits
underneath every line. For any scope item, ask separately whether it is furnished and whether it is
installed, and expect different answers. A row that reads simply hollow metal frames will be answered
yes by a supplier who will never set one.

Silence is a finding here more often than in most trades, because the exclusion lists are so long
that the absence of an item from a fifty-line exclusion list is meaningful. If a bidder has excluded
forty-five things and not the item you are asking about, that is closer to an inclusion than silence
usually is. The reverse also holds: an item nobody mentions at all, on either list, is almost
certainly unpriced by everybody.

### The structural gap list

These are the items the market systematically will not carry, that an estimator adds to every number
before comparing. A scope list naming all of them is doing the plugging in advance.

1. Installation wherever the bidder is supply only.
2. Sales tax wherever it was excluded.
3. All work at existing to remain openings, which on a renovation is the largest single addition and
   is carried both as a hardware value and as an hourly allowance for field work.
4. Sliding and barn door leaves.
5. Hardware at aluminum openings.
6. Automatic operator installation.
7. Hollow metal frame setting.
8. A per-opening allowance for reusing existing doors and frames.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of that table is boilerplate. Repeat bidders reproduce identical exclusion lists across projects
and across rounds, and the same lists appear from the same companies on all four projects in this
corpus. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal:

- A named door tag or mark that is excluded. When a bidder writes out specific tags and excludes them,
  it read the door schedule and found something it will not do or cannot get.
- An exclusion justified by the absence of a detail. One install-side proposal excludes most of its
  trim scope on the stated ground that no detail exists, and asks for head and jamb detail
  clarification before it can price common area door casing. That is a drawing gap report in the shape
  of an exclusion list, and it is worth an RFI rather than a plug.
- A reported manufacturing constraint. A door mark exceeding the widest leaf a manufacturer makes, a
  gauge unavailable in a double egress configuration, a hollow core unavailable at a bifold size, a
  fire label unobtainable because the door elevation falls outside the rating limits. Each of these is
  a document error the bidder found for you, and each one will otherwise surface at submittal.
- A frame type or a scope boundary inferred from indirect evidence, such as reading the assigned
  hardware set, the proximity to unit entries and the fire rating to conclude that certain openings
  must be hollow metal at a taller height. That is the bidder telling you the schedule is ambiguous.
- A stated disagreement with the specification on code grounds, most often on temperature rise rated
  construction in a sprinklered building.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the price into a change
order or a delivery problem. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Materials only, as a standing term of sale rather than a scope line. Some suppliers place it in the
  terms block, not the scope, so a proposal can read as a complete door package and still be a
  delivery ticket.
- Retainage is not allowed, and payment terms run net from requisition with credit approval. Suppliers
  state this flatly, and one refuses subcontract agreements outright and will accept only a material
  purchase order. Another accepts a reduction from the usual retention to half of it within a stated
  period after final delivery. This is not a payment detail, it is a determinant of what contract form
  the package can even be bought under.
- A material deposit before release, plus a separate charge for submittals and engineering, with a
  deeper deposit reserved for custom or rated product. Some suppliers.
- A cap on submittals included, commonly one original plus one or two revisions, with further
  resubmittals charged. Common.
- Tailgate delivery only, with truck-load quantities assumed and a stated number of deliveries
  included. Additional deliveries or phasing must be agreed before buyout. Carpenters must offload by
  forklift or standing charges apply, and must note damage or shortage on the bill of lading at
  delivery or claims are void. Common among suppliers.
- Lead times supersede the construction schedule, with the general contractor asked to verify and
  advise. Stated outright by some, implied by all.
- Price validity of thirty to ninety days, and a stated month beyond which material is requoted for
  escalation. Universal, and the escalation date is frequently well before the real delivery date.
- Tariffs, duties and surcharges excluded, with any imposed after the proposal date either voiding
  the proposal or arriving as a change order. Present in essentially every proposal in this corpus,
  which reflects the market at the time rather than a permanent feature.
- Warranty in effect only once all payments are received. Some suppliers.
- Approved shop drawings govern over the plans and specifications where they conflict. Stated by a
  specialty manufacturer, and implicit in every supplier's release-on-approval position.
- Manufacturer substitution reserved where the specified door, frame and hardware makers are not
  compatible with one another. Some bidders, and it is a real problem rather than a hedge.
- Door and frame sizes and elevations may vary from those shown in order to hold the specified fire
  and warranty requirements. Some bidders.
- Prehung doors carry the manufacturer standard undercut; anything else is a charge. Casing heights
  will not align between prehung swing units and non-swinging doors without extra engineering and
  custom material. Field trimming of precut casing packs will be required. These three appear
  together and are the recurring quality complaints of the residential door package.
- Field painted prehung doors must be finished on all six sides to hold the manufacturer warranty, and
  paint grade material requires field sanding and cleaning by the finish paint contractor before
  finish paint. Stated by the same bidders in nearly identical words on every project, which makes it
  the most reliable cross-trade dependency in the package.
- An exterior hoist must be provided by the general contractor on a tall building, with no money
  carried for it. Some bidders.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The furnish and install split: the seam that defines the trade

This is not one seam among several. It is the organizing fact of the package, and a scope list that
does not carry it explicitly on every line will produce bids that cannot be compared.

The default market shape is that a supplier furnishes and a carpenter installs, under two separate
contracts, and neither one is responsible for the other. Around that default sit four variants:

1. Supplier furnishes, finish carpenter installs, under separate contracts. The most common shape by
   a wide margin. The carpenter's proposal is filed under the carpentry division, so the door
   installation price is only visible if that bidder chose to break it out, and often it is buried
   inside a much larger trim and millwork number.
2. One bidder furnishes and installs, with material and labor stated as separate numbers. A minority,
   and the bidders offering it are carpentry contractors with a hardware division. Even here, hollow
   metal frame installation is carved out.
3. A supplier furnishes and offers installation as a priced add alternate. Several bidders. The
   alternate carries its own carve-outs, typically excluding automatic operators, hollow metal
   frames, all work at existing to remain openings, field glazing, untaken trim and off-hours
   loading, so accepting the alternate does not close the gap.
4. A specialty manufacturer supplies and installs one product as a single number, which is the
   opposite of the package norm and appears only for smoke curtains and proprietary integrated
   assemblies.

Installation labor as a fraction of material value runs wide and predictably. Common area
installation runs roughly a third of common area material. Unit interior installation runs roughly
three quarters of unit interior material, because unit doors are numerous and cheap and the labor per
opening does not fall with the price of the door. Amenity installation runs a very small fraction of
amenity material, because those doors are few and expensive. Whole-package installation therefore
lands somewhere between a third and two thirds of material, and the position within that band is
driven by the mix of unit doors against common area doors, not by the value of the package. Where a
bidder prices labor against an explicit opening count, treat the resulting rate per opening as the
most transferable cost fact in the trade.

Three additional scope lines exist only in the split configuration and in no other, so put them on
the sheet: receive and offload the delivered material, distribute it to the floors, and store and
protect it until installation. Suppliers exclude all three, installers claim receive and distribute
but not storage, and hoisting is excluded by everyone.

### Hollow metal frame installation: the seam nobody owns

Every supplier excludes it. Every furnish-and-install bidder excludes it. The finish carpenter
excludes it from the base bid and offers it as a priced add alternate. It is excluded more
consistently than any other item in the trade, and no proposal in the corpus carries it.

The reason is sequence rather than reluctance: hollow metal frames get set by the framer or the
drywall contractor before the board goes on, months before doors arrive on site. That is why the door
installer will not touch it and why an add alternate for setting knockdown frames at interior stud
walls is priced separately when it is offered at all. If your framing or drywall scope does not carry
frame setting, it is unbought, and the drawings will never tell you.

Grouting, frame insulation and bituminous coating attach to the same seam and are excluded by the
same parties, several citing an industry technical bulletin advising against grouting in drywall
partitions and another arguing bituminous coating should only ever be field applied.

### The painting seam

All material arrives primed only. Field applied paint is by others, on every proposal, without
exception. Three consequences the drawings do not carry:

- Paint grade material requires field sanding and cleaning by the finish paint contractor before
  finish paint. The door supplier says so and the painting package does not always carry it.
- Field painted prehung doors must be finished on all six sides to hold the manufacturer warranty.
  Whether all six sides is in the painting scope is contested often enough to be worth its own words
  on both trades' scope sheets.
- Bituminous coating at frames is by others and is not paint, which means it can fall between the
  painter and the waterproofer.

Beware also of the install-only bidder that claims sanding, finishing or painting doors per
specification inside its own installation scope, which puts the same work in two packages.

### The access control seam

The most contested boundary in the package and the one that moves the most money. The pieces are the
lock, the electrified hardware around it, the power supply and controller, the wiring, the card
reader, the software, the training and the commissioning. Every bidder draws the line in a different
place. One carries locks, electrified hardware, controllers and card readers while excluding all of
their installation. One carries the lock and the software and training while excluding install and
commissioning. One assigns the named smart lock brands out to the access control divisions entirely.
Several carry a placeholder budget for common area hardware and state that it accounts for no access
control or electronic components at all.

Enumerate this as at least four rows, and expect that even then commissioning will be unassigned.

### The automatic operator seam

Four parties, and no proposal in the corpus names all four. The operator is commonly furnished by the
door supplier, sometimes furnished only and explicitly not installed, occasionally furnished and
installed by the supplier's own manufacturer partner, and occasionally excluded altogether. Its
electrical connection is by the electrician on every proposal that mentions it. Its installation is
sometimes assigned to the finish carpenter on the general contractor's own scope sheet even when no
bidder claimed it. Its commissioning appears nowhere.

### Other seams

- Glazing. Excluded at hollow metal and wood doors by nearly everyone and assigned to the glazier, but
  several bidders include glazing at specific door types, most often frosted glass at bedroom barn
  doors and clear tempered at amenity doors, and one added glazing at all hollow metal openings when
  the general contractor asked. Never write a single glazing line.
- Trim and casing. Casing attached to a prehung assembly is in the package. Casing at hollow metal
  frames is excluded by nearly everyone. Unit entry casing is contested. Common area door casing is
  excluded by the carpenter for want of a detail. Trim at barn, bifold and balcony doors is excluded
  on the ground that none is specified and the openings are gypsum board returns. Each is a separate
  row and several of them are real gaps rather than assignments.
- Framing and drywall. Beyond frame setting, the drywall contractor owns opening preparation, wall
  backing, blocking and any patching around openings, and the door package excludes masonry work,
  drywall installation or repair, and cutouts for recessed accessories.
- Millwork and casework. On projects where the door installer is also the trim carpenter, the two
  scopes arrive in one proposal and the door portion must be extracted before it can be levelled.
  Where they arrive separately, watch for corridor portals, cased openings and stair millwork being
  excluded by both.
- Elevator work. Smoke containment curtains sit at the elevator opening and depend on elevator
  submittals for their fabrication dimensions, on the fire alarm for their trigger signal, and on a
  factory-trained installer for their final terminal connections. Their exclusion list disclaims
  opening preparation, backing, drywall, ceiling and soffit work and access panels, so the opening
  they hang in is somebody else's problem entirely.
- Windows and balcony doors. Excluded by every door bidder and commonly assumed aluminum, which is an
  assumption rather than a reading. On a design development set a bidder may assume whole categories
  of openings are aluminum on the basis of a mark prefix.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What door bidders actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The door schedule. This is the primary document and nearly every proposal names it by sheet
   number. Several suppliers state as a standing term that only openings listed on the door schedule
   are included and that unlisted doors, frames and borrowed lites are not, which makes the schedule
   the contractual boundary of the package. Ratings are priced as listed on the schedule and nowhere
   else.
2. The hardware sets in the specification. Where a hardware schedule exists, bidders count sets from
   it. Where one does not, they budget against the outline of sets in the specification and say so,
   and every hardware alternate becomes unpriceable because there is nothing to substitute against.
3. Door and frame type and elevation sheets, and jamb and head details. Frame type substitutions and
   trim exclusions are argued from these, and the absence of a jamb detail is a stated reason for
   excluding whole groups of openings.
4. The floor plans, for the unit count and the unit mix. A bidder that does a real takeoff builds a
   unit type by floor by mark matrix off the plans and states that the counts are its own.
5. The specification, for manufacturers, gauges, coatings, ratings and warranty, and as the target of
   paragraph-by-paragraph exclusions.

The count divergence problem is the single most consistent hazard in this trade, and it deserves its
own statement. Different bidders reading the same door schedule return materially different counts.
Hardware set totals cluster tightly, usually within a few percent, because everyone counts the same
list of sets. Hollow metal frame counts diverge much more, and on one project the highest frame count
ran roughly half again above the lowest. Prehung counts diverge by a hundred openings or more on a
large project.

Most of that divergence is categorical rather than arithmetic. The unit entry frame is the culprit:
some bidders count it as hollow metal, some as a prefinished steel nailer frame in the residential
section, some in neither. Whether a pair opening is counted as one opening or two leaves, whether a
barn slab counts as a door, and whether openings marked existing to remain are counted at all all
compound it. So do not treat a count as a fact. Treat it as each bidder's reading, put the unit entry
frame on its own row so the categorization becomes visible, and expect the estimator sheet to be the
place where the divergence first shows up.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Whether the package is being bought as furnish only, furnish and install, or split. The largest
  single structural variable, and it is a procurement decision rather than a design one.
- Who is setting the hollow metal frames, and whether that scope is in anybody's contract.
- Whether sales tax is inside or outside the number, which alone is worth several percent on a
  supply-heavy package.
- What contract form the counterparty will accept. A supplier that refuses subcontract agreements and
  refuses retainage is a different commercial animal from a subcontractor, and the general contractor
  carries risk it would otherwise pass down.
- Deposit and submittal charge requirements, and how many resubmittals are included before charges
  begin.
- Lead times against the construction schedule, and whether shop drawing approval will happen in time.
  Hardware and keyed cylinders are the long poles, and lead time runs from approval of submittals, not
  from award.
- The number of deliveries, whether phased delivery is required, who offloads, whether a forklift is
  available, and whether an exterior hoist exists.
- Where material is stored between delivery and installation, which nobody carries.
- Whether a hardware schedule exists at all at bid time.
- Who owns keying, the master key system, and the construction core return.
- Which trade is integrating access control, and who commissions it.
- Whether the specified green certifications are actually obtainable from the specified
  manufacturers.
- Manufacturer availability constraints on specified gauges, sizes and configurations, which a bidder
  will report as a substitution rather than as a question.
- Tariff and escalation exposure, and the date beyond which material is requoted.
- Whether the openings marked existing to remain get new hardware, modified doors, or nothing, and who
  verifies the existing conditions in the field. Bidders exclude all of it and several state that
  field verification of existing hardware is not their responsibility.
- Whether the project is union or open shop, which the estimator records for every bidder even though
  most suppliers have no labor at all.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders price around missing information openly: assuming
whole categories of openings are aluminum on the basis of a mark prefix, reading unit entry doors off
an architectural rendering because no door schedule exists yet, carrying a placeholder for common
area hardware that explicitly excludes access control, and labelling the whole thing a budget. At
permit and construction document stage the same bidders switch to citing schedule sheets, counted
quantities and specification paragraph numbers. Treat a design development hardware number as a
placeholder and expect it to move most of all, because the hardware package is the last thing to get
specified and the largest single line in the finished bid.

Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its folder's set stage frequently disagree. A bid may
price a construction document round off an earlier permit set and say so, cite an RFI as its
governing addendum, or arrive addressed to a different general contractor entirely.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, for frames, door leaves, prehung assemblies, wood doors and most hardware items. The dominant
  unit everywhere.
- Openings, distinguished from leaves. A pair opening is one opening and two leaves, and bidders
  report both. The distinction matters for hardware, which is counted by set against openings.
- Sets, for hardware. The normalizing count for the hardware portion of the package, and the count
  that agrees best between bidders.
- Pair, for pair openings and bifold pairs, quoted separately from singles.
- Linear feet, for base, casing, window stool and apron where the trim rides with the door package.
- Pieces, for back panels and similar fabricated trim.
- Units, meaning dwelling units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator
  sheet in the corpus ranks every bidder on cost per unit.
- Deliveries and truck loads, stated per scope block by some suppliers, with an approximate quantity
  per full load and floors served per load.
- Weeks, for lead time by product class.
- Percent of contract value, for bond.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Package mode. Whether installation is in or out is worth between a third and two thirds of the
   material value, and it is the difference that dwarfs everything below it.
2. The hardware package, which is routinely the largest of the three product classes, exceeding
   hollow metal and wood doors combined on most projects. Within it, brand and grade substitution is
   the primary lever: an alternate hardware brand at the same grade is regularly the single largest
   value engineering deduct available, and it is the reason an estimator levels hardware manufacturer
   by product category.
3. Unit count and unit mix, which drives the prehung assembly count, which drives both the material
   and the labor.
4. Door height. Making upper-floor unit interior doors match the taller first-floor height is one of
   the largest alternates in the trade, and its labor component runs about three times its material
   component, so it is a labor event dressed as a material change.
5. Smart lock and access control content. A named smart unit entry lock package is a large enough
   line to be broken out as its own number, and substituting the lock family is a meaningful deduct.
6. Proprietary and integrated products. A scheduled integrated door assembly, a proprietary acrylic
   or specialty door system, or a named track closer family will each carry a large deduct to go
   conventional. The integrated assembly deduct is the largest single alternate seen.
7. Frame construction: welded against knockdown, and seamless against seamed doors. Real money, and
   the substitution keeps the specified manufacturer, which makes it an easy approval.
8. Door core, solid against hollow, at unit interiors. Frequently proposed and worth surprisingly
   little.
9. Sales tax treatment, which is a straight percentage on the material portion.
10. Automatic operators, thresholds, gasketing and the other hardware accessories, individually small
    and collectively meaningful.
11. Lead time and delivery count, which do not show in the number but show in the schedule.
12. Bond, minority participation requirements, overtime and multiple mobilizations, all excluded by
    default and added back only if asked.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per unit among bidders offering the whole package runs from about 1.6 to
about 2.2 times, which is tighter than the wide-open finish trades. Bidder counts per project run
from three to ten ranked rows, and those rows include the same company at multiple revisions and at
multiple set stages, so the number of distinct companies is smaller than the ranking suggests.

The ranking has a trap that is specific to this trade and it is worth stating plainly. A partial
scope bidder, one quoting prehung assemblies only or one quoting only the doors it also supplies
hardware for, will land at the bottom of an ascending cost-per-unit ranking and will look like the
low bidder. It is not cheap, it is incomplete. On one project the two lowest ranked rows were both
partial scope, and reading the ranking without that knowledge would have been badly misleading. Any
scope list for this trade should make partial-scope bids structurally visible by carrying the product
classes as separate rows, so a bidder silent on three of five rows cannot be mistaken for a
whole-package number.

One more pricing convention worth knowing, and it differs from the trades where leveling means adding
a standard set of plugs to everybody. Here an estimator compares base bid plus adjustments, but the
adjustments are bidder-specific rather than uniform. Each bidder is credited with the value of what
it individually failed to carry, most often installation, sales tax, a missing product class, or a
scope block priced by another bidder and carried across at that other value. That last mechanism,
carrying one bidder's price for an item into every other bidder's total, is the workhorse of leveling
in this trade and it appears on every sheet.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on about thirty doors, frames and hardware proposals, one specialty smoke
curtain quote, three install-only proposals, and four estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four
multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The
proposals span design development, permit, construction document and conformed sets, and the bidder
pool includes several companies that appear on three or four of the projects, which is what makes the
boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable. Two of the projects carry
same-bidder revision pairs or triples, and one carries a bidder that changed package mode twice
during a single negotiation.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back of house space, in
  one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors,
  institutional, healthcare, hospitality or public prevailing-wage work, where the hardware package
  is heavier, the openings are fewer and larger, and the prehung assembly volume that dominates the
  count here largely disappears.
- Only one project involved renovation of an existing building, so the entire existing-to-remain
  block in section 3 and the plug behavior around it rest on that project alone.
- The unit entry frame categorization problem is well evidenced, but the corpus does not show how it
  was finally resolved on any project, so the guidance to give it its own row is a diagnosis rather
  than a proven remedy.
- No signed subcontract, purchase order or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows
  which exclusions survived negotiation, whether the no-retainage and material-purchase-order terms
  held, or where the hollow metal frame setting scope actually landed.
- No change order history and no submittal history are in the corpus. The claims that lead times
  supersede the schedule, that submittal caps generate charges, and that manufacturer substitutions
  surface at submittal are read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed, so treat
  them as untested here.
- One proposal on one project could not be read at all: a long scanned document with no text layer.
  Its total is on the estimator sheet, so its scope shape is missing from this file rather than its
  price.
- The corpus has not shown a project where a hardware schedule existed in full at bid time, so the
  behavior described for hardware leveling is drawn from projects where the sets were thin or absent.
- The corpus has not shown how the package is bought when a single contractor furnishes and installs
  everything including the hollow metal frames, which is the one configuration that would close the
  frame-setting gap.
- The corpus has not shown keying, master key system handover, or access control commissioning
  actually being assigned to anyone. Those are identified here as gaps precisely because no document
  closes them.
- The tariff and escalation language is pervasive across every proposal, which reflects the market at
  the time these were bid rather than a durable feature of the trade.
