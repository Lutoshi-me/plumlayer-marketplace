# Casework

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the casework package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real cabinet suppliers can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

One warning up front, because it governs everything else in this trade. The drawings show a designed
kitchen. Most of the market bids a catalogue. The gap between the two is where the money and the
argument live, and it is invisible on any sheet.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 123530 or 123500, Residential Casework, is the anchor. Both numbers appear for the same
scope, and a scope sheet may reference a third variant in its own row labels, so the section number
is a weak identifier and the trade token is the strong one. A proposal names a section only
occasionally; most name a drawing set and a date instead.

The package as bid in the market is factory-manufactured unit casework: kitchen cabinets and bathroom
vanities in the residential units, plus the panels, fillers, toe kicks and hardware that make them
land. It is a product purchase with a shop drawing cycle, not a field trade. That single fact drives
the rest of the file.

Three packages sit adjacent and are constantly confused with this one. Keeping them separate is the
first job of a casework scope list.

- Unit casework. Modular, factory built, priced per unit, usually supply only. This package.
- Amenity and common area millwork. Custom, shop drawn, usually furnish and install, often at union
  field rates, and frequently routed to Division 06 by name. A separately read trade.
- Countertops. Stone or quartz, a different supply chain with a different fabrication and templating
  cycle, and its own installer.

The same company will bid all three, and will price them as separate proposals with separate numbers
even when it bids them together. One bidder split unit casework and amenity millwork into two
numbered proposals and had to carry a written cross-reference warning that one room's cabinets sat in
whichever proposal was bought, and would be missed if only one were awarded. Read that as the norm to
expect rather than as an oddity.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another spec section:

- Countertops. The strongest attractor by a wide margin. Many cabinet bidders price kitchen and
  vanity tops in the same document, and several are cabinet-and-stone companies by name. Whether the
  tops are in or out is the single most common reason two casework numbers are not comparable.
- Cabinet pulls and knobs. Usually carried, but as often as not as a separate priced line or an add,
  and occasionally as a stated dollar allowance per piece rather than as the specified product.
- Kitchen and vanity sinks, sink clips, sink cutouts and countertop support brackets. These travel
  with the countertop rather than with the cabinet, so they land in this package whenever the
  countertop does and fall in the gap whenever it does not.
- Medicine cabinets, mirrors and bathroom accessories. Some bidders carry them, commonly as an add,
  and more exclude them outright.
- Closet, linen and laundry shelving. Occasionally carried, more often excluded as by others, and
  the exclusion is usually phrased as an assumption rather than as a line.
- Undercabinet lighting and light valance. Almost always excluded from base and offered as an add.
  It carries an electrical dependency that no cabinet proposal mentions.
- Appliances, shower doors, surrounds and pans, and even flooring. Multi-division suppliers bundle
  these under one summary with a separate purchase order required per product division.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like casework:

- Installation. The dominant case. Most of the market is supply only. See section five, because this
  is not a boundary detail, it is the structural fact of the trade.
- Amenity and common area custom millwork, as above.
- Appliance panels and panel-ready appliance fronts. Excluded by nearly everyone who mentions them,
  and rarely picked up anywhere else.
- Crown, light rail and shoe moulding. Where a supplier offers these at all it supplies them loose
  with installation by others, which creates finish carpentry labor with no home.
- Blocking, backing and die walls at islands and peninsulas. Universally by others, and universally
  assumed to already be there.

## 2. How the market bids

Casework is not a degenerate proposal trade the way painting is. Proposals carry real product
description, because the bidder is selling a catalogue product and needs to tell you which one. What
they do not carry is scope reconciliation against the drawings.

The market splits into a small number of recognisable proposal shapes.

- A short list of scope-family numbers. The most common shape by some margin: cabinets, hardware,
  delivery, installation, tax and tariff as four to seven lines. Segmentation is by commercial
  component, not by building or area.
- An element count with a single price. The bidder lists every kitchen, vanity, island, linen,
  pantry, filler and panel with a count, then gives one number for all of it. Maximum quantity
  transparency with zero price transparency. Common enough to expect on any bid list.
- A per-unit rate card. Rare but very informative when it appears: a price per unit for each of
  several door styles, a price per unit for union installation and another for non-union, a price per
  vanity, a price per pull, and amenity rooms priced individually by room number.
- A schedule of values. The whole package presented as payment tasks, with material, hardware, tax,
  assembly, installation and freight as separate rows per product family. Occasional, and the
  clearest furnish and install decomposition available.
- A catalogue extract. A price list with the quantity column left blank. Not a bid, but it will be
  filed as one.

Line-item pricing at the cabinet-box level does exist, but only from installers. No cabinet supplier
in the corpus prices box by box; a dedicated installation subcontractor does.

Segmentation by building or by area is rare. It happens where the project is physically two
buildings, and essentially nowhere else. Segmentation by element is common. Segmentation by level
does not occur.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The cabinet product by manufacturer and line name, with box construction, door style, drawer box,
  slides, hinges, edgebanding and interior finish. Nearly universal, and often as an appended
  manufacturer specification sheet.
- A unit count, usually kitchens and bathrooms separately, and often a unit-type table listing every
  unit type and its quantity. Very common, and it is the leveling denominator.
- The drawing set it priced from, by date. Nearly universal. The specification is named far less
  often, and one bidder stated outright that it had no spec book.
- Delivery terms. Tailgate delivery is the default and is stated as such.
- Lead time, in weeks from approved shop drawings. Commonly stated, which is unusual across trades
  and reflects that this is a manufactured product.
- Price validity, usually thirty days, occasionally ninety with a capped escalation percentage after.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Installation, unless asked. Most bidders are supply only.
- Any reconciliation against the drawn elevations. A proposal will tell you what it is selling. It
  will rarely tell you where what it is selling differs from what is drawn, and when it does, that
  disclosure is the most valuable paragraph in the document.
- Labor type. Where installation is offered the labor basis is sometimes given, and open shop
  non-prevailing wage is the default. An estimator records a labor type for every bidder regardless,
  with supply-only bidders simply marked as suppliers.
- Who offloads and distributes. Where it is stated it is stated as an exclusion.
- Hoisting and vertical lifting. Almost always the general contractor's, usually by an explicit
  clause requiring it free of charge.
- Blocking. Assumed to exist, occasionally with a warning that it must be behind a particular
  cabinet.
- Protection after installation. Rarely carried, and one bidder prices it per unit conditional on the
  owner signing off that the cabinetry was undamaged first.
- Mock-up. Almost never in base. Where priced it is either a unit that must remain part of the
  finished project, or an accelerated offsite unit that is explicitly refused.
- Bond, insurance program participation, payment platform fees and certification fees. A minority
  state these, and those that do state them as percentages of contract value rather than amounts.

Exclusion behavior does not split this market the way it splits painting. Nearly every casework
proposal carries exclusions. What varies is whether the exclusions are boilerplate or project signal,
and that distinction does most of the work at leveling. See section four.

Revision habits are distinctive and worth knowing precisely, because two of the three common
mechanisms move no money at all.

- Price moves with unchanged scope. The scope text is byte-identical and the number drops. Drops of
  around a seventh have been seen, with any value-engineering deduct scaled proportionally alongside.
  A reason is not documented.
- The unbundle revision. The revision splits one combined number into a cabinets column and a
  countertops column, at an identical grand total, splitting delivery, tax, tariff and installation
  across the two columns as well. Seen from more than one bidder on more than one project. This is
  the trade's signature revision: the general contractor needs the countertop separable so it can be
  leveled against a stone bidder, and asks the supplier to restructure rather than to re-price.
- Options accrete. The base holds and successive rounds add door style choices, labor options and a
  bundled-award discount, so the later revision is a menu rather than a new price.

Two consequences for a reader. First, do not read a revision in this trade as a response to an
addendum. It is a negotiation or a formatting request. Second, a proposal is not the same thing as a
file. The identical document appears filed under two different trade tokens in the same folder more
than once. A whole-package bid spanning four divisions gets filed once under each division. An
installation quote exists only as an email thread with the general contractor's own scope request
quoted below it. Treat the proposal as the unit and list the files it draws from.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Casework scope sheets have run twenty seven, forty one, forty two and sixty six rows. Target roughly
forty to seventy scope lines for a full multifamily casework package. The twenty seven row sheet is
where the install plug had to do the most work, because the sheet had no rows for the supply and
install seam beyond a single installation line.

This trade differs from most in that the commercial rows are scope rows. Supply, installation,
delivery, transport, offloading, tax and tariff each change hands between bidders, and each has to be
its own line or the numbers cannot be added up.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Supply, separately from installation, separately from delivery to the site, separately from
  offloading and distribution within the site, separately from hoisting. These are five different
  answers from five potentially different parties, and every one of them is a real gap on some
  bidder. Every estimator sheet separates at least supply from installation, and the better ones
  separate all five.
- Sales tax and import tariff, each as its own row. Both are excluded by a meaningful share of
  bidders, both are large, and tariff in particular is carried as an open question on most bidders on
  most sheets. A bidder may state the tariff dollar amount to account for at the current rate without
  including it.
- Unit kitchens and unit bathroom vanities as separate lines, with their own counts. They are counted
  differently, priced differently, and bidders disagree about the counts.
- Islands, split into fixed and movable. Almost always excluded from base and offered as an add, and
  they are large. One bidder priced islands as their own line specifically because an island type did
  not consistently match the same kitchen type in every unit it appeared in, which is a drawing
  consistency finding surfaced as a pricing decision.
- Tall linens, tall pantries, wall cabinets above toilets, and medicine cabinets, each on its own
  line. Each of these is carried by some bidders and excluded by others, and several are offered as
  adds in two finish grades.
- Panels, broken out by condition rather than lumped: refrigerator tall end panels, kitchen surround
  tall panels above and beside the uppers, finished back and side panels at peninsulas and islands,
  dishwasher end panels, finished cabinet sides at exposed ends, fillers, and finished backs at sink
  bases against windows. This block is where the deducts and adds cluster, and where the panel
  thickness argument in section six lives. Enumerated on every detailed proposal and it is the
  richest source of add and deduct pricing in the trade.
- Toe kicks, with the material called out. Pressure treated toe kick is specified on some projects,
  excluded by some bidders with a note that it would be a significant increase, and priced by one
  bidder as a separate material and a separate installation line where the installation cost several
  times the material.
- Cabinet pulls and hardware, its own line, with the answer allowed to be an allowance rather than a
  product. Also carry pre-drilling for hardware as its own line, because a supplier that did not
  supply the hardware will not pre-drill for it.
- Countertop support brackets, subtops and sinks, each its own line, even when the countertop itself
  is a different package. These are the items that fall between the cabinet and the stone.
- Removability at accessible units, split from ordinary vanity and sink base cabinets, and split
  again between where it is required and where the drawings show it without requiring it. Bidders
  offer deducts on this, and one read a supplementary narrative document to establish that it was not
  required at standard units.
- Amenity and common area casework, room by room, using the architect's room numbers. Where a bidder
  prices amenity casework it either lists the rooms it carried or excludes every room not on its
  list. A room-by-room row set is the only way to test that list.
- Appliance panels and panel-ready fronts, its own line, because the answer is almost always no.
- Closet, linen and laundry shelving, its own line, for the same reason.
- A specification conformance block. The richest estimator sheet devotes sixteen consecutive rows to
  basis of design, construction style, front and face construction, box construction, exterior
  finish, interior finish, shelves, toe kicks, door style, door material, drawer fronts, drawer
  construction, drawer slides, soft close hardware, hinges and pulls, with the specified attribute
  stated and each bidder's actual product language transcribed into its cell. Leveling in this trade
  is done on construction attributes, not on yes and no.
- A specification versus drawing conflict block. See section six. If the spec and the elevations
  disagree, those rows are the deliverable.
- Project fees that are stated as percentages: payment platform fees, certification and insurance
  program fees, contract-instead-of-purchase-order processing fees, bond, fuel surcharge. Small
  individually, real in aggregate, and stated by only a minority.
- Mock-up, protection and storage, each its own line, with the basis stated. Storage in particular is
  quoted per truck per week by more than one bidder and is a live cost the moment the schedule slips.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual cabinet boxes collapse into their kitchen or vanity. Only an installer prices box by
  box, and it does so off the supplier's own schedule after award, not off the drawings.
- Cabinet construction attributes are attributes of a line, not lines. Box thickness, edgebanding,
  hinge type, slide type and interior colour belong in the conformance block, not in the quantity
  block.
- Unit-by-unit enumeration collapses to unit type. Every bidder that counts, counts by unit type or
  by kitchen layout type, and cites the plan and elevation sheet for each type.
- Levels collapse entirely. No document in this trade organizes by level.
- Colour and finish options collapse into one row with the options named, unless a finish grade
  changes the price, in which case it becomes an alternate rather than a new scope line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
casework, that test fires hardest on the five-way supply and install split, on the panel conditions,
and on everything that sits between the cabinet and the countertop.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding, and in this trade silence is usually a finding.
A bidder that says nothing about installation is supply only. A bidder that says nothing about tax
has excluded it. A bidder that says nothing about the panel above the refrigerator has not priced
it. Where painting bidders leave boundaries open, casework bidders leave them unmentioned, and the
default answer to an unmentioned item is no.

### The structural gap list

An estimator does not compare base bids in this trade either. It compares base bid plus adjustments,
and the adjustments here are larger relative to the base than in most trades, because the single
biggest one is the labor to install the product.

The plugs to expect, in rough order of size:

1. Installation. On one project the estimator applied one identical install value to seven different
   supply-only bidders, because there is nothing to level: the install is bought separately and
   everyone gets the same number added. Installation runs from roughly forty percent to roughly
   ninety percent of the supply price, and exceeds it where the cabinets ship knockdown.
2. Delivery, transport and freight. Quoted separately by most, excluded by some, and on an imported
   product it can approach half the material cost.
3. Offloading, distribution to the units, and hoisting or vertical lifting. Almost universally the
   general contractor's, almost never priced by anyone.
4. Sales tax and import tariff.
5. Countertop support brackets, subtops and sinks, when the countertop is a separate package.
6. Amenity and common area casework, when the unit bidders exclude it and the millwork bidder has not
   been asked for it.
7. Blocking and backing behind wall cabinets and at islands and peninsulas.
8. Appliance panels, closet and laundry shelving, medicine cabinets and mouldings.
9. Protection, mock-up and storage.
10. Payment platform, certification, bond and processing fees.

A scope line set that names all ten explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging for it.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Repeat bidders reproduce their assumption, clarification and legal blocks verbatim across projects.
Two bidders' documents six months and two projects apart are identical down to the stated fee
percentages, the shop drawing revision count and the lien threshold. Repeat bidders make up a good
share of any casework bid list. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- The deviation list. This is the highest-value paragraph in the trade and it deserves its own
  reading. A stock manufacturer will list, item by item, where its standard product does not match
  the drawn design: a wall cabinet height that is not available in the drawn dimension, a tall
  cabinet width that is not made, a corner base that does not come as wide as drawn, a panel
  thickness quoted thinner than shown, a cabinet depth capped below what the plans show. One bidder
  closes such a list by stating that the whole quote is per the manufacturer's standards rather than
  per plans and specs, and is not limited to the listed items. That sentence is not boilerplate; it
  is the bid.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. A bidder writing that wall cabinets are not
  shown and therefore not included, on a project where other bidders priced wall cabinets, is a
  documents problem worth an RFI, not a bidder problem.
- A named contradiction in the contract documents. One bidder flagged that the specification and
  other documents give contradicting determinations for formaldehyde and certification requirements
  and asked for direction rather than resolving it unilaterally. Another priced a deduct off a
  narrative summary of changes document that clarified a requirement the drawings over-stated.
- An amenity room list. Where a bidder enumerates the amenity rooms it carried and excludes every
  room not listed, that list is checkable against the plans and frequently comes up short.
- A live unresolved qualification. One bidder stated openly that it was still working on
  understanding the project's certification requirements and could potentially meet them only if the
  finish came from one of two named colour programs. That is a real constraint on the finish
  selection, disclosed before award.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the purchase order into a
change order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Production begins only after written shop drawing approval, and lead time is measured from the last
  of approved shops, confirmed field dimensions and approved finish samples. Nearly universal. Lead
  times of twelve to twenty weeks are normal, and one bidder will not even submit shop drawings until
  a signed contract exists and the final construction set is released.
- A minimum release batch. One bidder requires no fewer than twenty units released at a time for
  fabrication and twenty at a time for installation. This is a phasing constraint hidden in a payment
  clause.
- Full truckload delivery only, tailgate, during normal business hours. Extremely common. Several
  bidders add that the site must accommodate a fifty three foot trailer, that street permits are the
  contractor's, and that delivery outside normal hours is chargeable.
- A hard unloading window. One bidder allows ninety minutes from arrival and charges hourly beyond
  it. The general contractor offloads.
- Continuous uninterrupted installation. Where the installer is a travelling crew, the price assumes
  a single continuous term with one mobilization included, lodging and per diem for the whole job,
  and a stated remobilization fee per occurrence if crews stand down for more than two days.
- Environmental control before delivery. Temperature and humidity must be controlled to the
  woodworking standard before material arrives, or the contractor waives its warranty and accepts
  responsibility for warping, cracking and peeling.
- Storage if the site is not ready, charged per truck per week. Priced by several bidders and offered
  free of charge for an undefined period by one, which is a genuine commercial difference.
- Material shipped fully assembled and ready for installation, and shipped with the delivery for the
  floor scheduled for installation. Backordered material or returns cause extra cost. This is the
  installer's condition on the supplier, and it makes the supply and install seam a schedule seam as
  well as a scope seam.
- Nothing is included that is not on the approved shop drawings. Stated flatly by more than one
  bidder: items not on the shops will be charged. The shop drawing set, not the bid documents,
  becomes the governing scope after award.
- Staged deposits tied to procurement milestones, commonly half or a quarter of the contract before
  any delivery, sometimes with named percentages funded a fixed number of weeks before first
  delivery to buy long lead materials. Deposits are non-refundable once production starts.
- Price validity and escalation. Thirty days is the default. Ninety days appears with a capped
  percentage increase after. Several bidders reserve the right to reprice for market volatility at
  any time, and one states the quoted number excludes a fuel surcharge that must nevertheless be
  added to the contract value.
- Tariff regime. Stated by date of an executive order in one case, quantified but excluded in
  another, and carried as an add in a third. On imported cabinetry this is a percentage-scale item.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### Supply versus install: the seam that defines the trade

Most casework bidders are suppliers, not subcontractors. Several will only sign a purchase order and
will refuse a construction subcontract outright, and one adds a processing fee of about one percent
of contract value if a contract is issued instead. So the base question on every casework line is not
what is included, it is who is standing in the unit with a screw gun.

Installation appears in five distinct commercial shapes, and the drawings will not tell you which one
applies:

1. Supply only, installation bought separately. The most common shape by a wide margin.
2. Supply with installation offered as a priced add. Common, and the cleanest to level, because the
   add is a real number from a party who has to honor it.
3. Supply and install as one contract. A minority, and usually from a regional company rather than a
   manufacturer.
4. Installation by the finish carpenter, with the cabinets furnished by others. The general
   contractor's own scope request may route kitchen and bath cabinets, movable islands and appliances
   to the finish carpenter as installs of others' material, while routing common area millwork to the
   same trade as furnish and install.
5. Installation by a specialty casework installer, sometimes a travelling non-union crew, sometimes
   the manufacturer's own certified third party quoted alongside the material.

The specialty installer case is the one that exposes the real dependencies, because that party prices
them as conditions rather than mentioning them as courtesies:

- The material must arrive fully assembled. Where cabinets are quoted knockdown, meaning flat packed,
  assembly moves into the install number and the install can exceed the material cost.
- Material must arrive with the delivery for the floor being installed. Split deliveries and
  backorders are chargeable.
- The contractor provides the lull lift for unloading and an elevator or buckhoist above four
  stories. Without one, a lift is added to the bid or claimed at the end.
- The installer is not responsible for material once staged, including damage by other trades and
  weather.
- Sink setting may or may not be included, and shower pan setting is excluded on the grounds that the
  installer is not a licensed plumbing company. A plumbing licence sits inside this seam.
- Caulking, blocking, protection, subtops, island supports, light rail, crown moulding and appliance
  panels are excluded from the install scope as well as from the supply scope, so they are excluded
  twice and provided never.

Install cost sits at roughly forty to ninety percent of the supply cost when the product ships
assembled, and above it when it ships knockdown. Where a bidder offers both union and non-union
installation, the union rate runs about a quarter higher. One bidder offered to discount installation
substantially in exchange for being awarded material and labor together, which is worth knowing as a
negotiation shape rather than as a price.

### Countertops

The second great seam. Even where one company sells both, the numbers are separable and the general
contractor will ask for them separated. What falls in the gap between cabinet and stone:

- Countertop support brackets. The cabinet supplier commonly excludes them. The stone fabricator
  states plainly that supports must be installed by others before stone installation and that the
  fabricator will not install them. In-wall steel brackets have to go in before the drywall, which
  means the decision has to be made months before either trade arrives.
- Subtops, plywood or honeycomb, and the adhesives. Priced by a bundled supplier as separate items,
  excluded by most cabinet suppliers.
- Sinks, faucets, sink clips and sink cutouts. Cutouts are commonly assigned to the installer on
  site, which only works if there is an installer.
- Splash. A proposal may include splash at bathrooms and exclude it at kitchens in the same sentence,
  on the stated assumption of free-standing ranges.
- Colour uniformity and seam placement. A stone supplier may condition its price on all tops being
  the same colour, and reserve the right to place seams to maximize its own yield.

Beware of a countertop-only bidder appearing in the casework ranking. On one project the lowest entry
on the casework sheet was a stone company, at a cost per unit that is not a casework number at all.

### Framing, drywall and painting

- Die walls, knee walls and framed walls at islands and peninsulas are by others, and cabinet bidders
  assume they are already gypsum boarded and painted. One bidder excluded matching panels to cover
  those walls specifically because the drawings show them painted, while another priced finished back
  and side panels at peninsulas as an add because no section was drawn through the condition. The
  same condition, answered two opposite ways.
- Blocking and backing is by others and assumed present. One bidder warns specifically that blocking
  must be located behind the wall cabinets above the refrigerator or there is a cost.
- Painted casework, where the finish schedule calls a painted cabinet finish keyed to a paint colour,
  is bid by the cabinet supplier as a factory painted product, not by the painter. The painter will
  have excluded casework. Confirm that the factory finish and the scheduled paint colour actually
  correspond.

### Appliances

- Appliance panels are excluded by nearly everyone who mentions them and are not picked up elsewhere.
- Final appliance specifications are required before shop drawings can be finished, because the oven
  cabinet and the refrigerator opening are built to the appliance. This is a procurement dependency
  that sits on the casework critical path and appears on no drawing.

### Electrical and plumbing

- Undercabinet lighting is excluded from base by nearly everyone and offered as an add by several.
  The add is for the fixture and the aluminium moulding, not for the circuit.
- Sink base cabinets ship with no back to allow plumbing configuration, and vanity top drawers are
  shaped to clear the trap. These are cabinet decisions driven by plumbing that the plumbing drawings
  do not show.

### Millwork

The amenity boundary is the cleanest available written statement in the corpus and it is worth
adopting: unit casework is Division 12 furnish, and amenity and common area custom millwork is
Division 06 furnish and install. Where a room appears in both packages it will be bought twice or not
at all. Where a company bids both, ask which of its two proposals a given room sits in.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

### The stock versus drawn problem

This is the defining non-deducible in the trade and it explains most of the price spread.

An architect draws a kitchen: cabinet heights, widths, depths, panel thicknesses, corner
configurations, reveals. A stock modular manufacturer builds a catalogue with fixed increments and
fixed sizes. Most of the market bids the catalogue. The difference is absorbed silently by most
bidders, disclosed item by item by a few, and priced by almost none.

The disclosures that do appear are consistent about where the mismatch happens:

- Wall cabinet height. Where the drawings call one height and the manufacturer makes another, the
  bidder quotes its own and says so, or meets the drawn dimension with an overhanging door on a
  smaller box.
- Cabinet depth. A cap at twenty four inches is common, with a stated significant upcharge above it
  because of material sheet yield, so a deeper pantry or wall oven cabinet shown on the plans is
  quietly excluded.
- Panel thickness. Elevations showing one inch or thicker panels are routinely quoted at three
  quarters of an inch, with the difference offered as an alternate. That alternate is a large number.
- Tall cabinet widths and corner base widths. The nearest available size is substituted.
- Wall cabinet heights in accessible units. Where the drawn height is not made, a taller one is
  quoted.
- Removability. Base cabinets specified removable away from the sink or narrower than a stated width
  may be excluded outright with a note that the substitution would not meet code.

A bidder that prices the drawn design rather than the catalogue will sit at the top of the ranking.
On one project the highest bidder was several times the lowest and offered deducts worth about thirty
percent of its own base for stepping down to a standard finish line and deleting the drawn kitchen
surround panels. That is not an expensive bidder. That is the only bidder who read the elevations.

### Specification versus drawing conflict

Casework is unusually prone to the specification and the drawings disagreeing, and the estimator's
sheet is where it gets caught. Conflicts observed include a casework specification that contradicts
the finish schedule, tempered glass required in the spec but not shown on any elevation or detail,
open shelf cabinets in the spec but absent from the kitchen elevations, frosted glass at uppers in
the spec but not drawn, front panels to conceal appliances required in the spec but not shown, a
shaker profile in a finish mark that conflicts with the drawn elevations, and severe use construction
standards in the spec that several bidders explicitly exclude.

When a specification requires something no elevation shows, treat it as a scope line with an open
question attached, not as an inclusion to assume. Every bidder will resolve it differently and most
will resolve it silently.

### What casework suppliers actually price from, in descending order of what they cite

1. The unit type matrix and the unit schedules. This is the primary document. Kitchens and bathrooms
   are counted by type off the unit plans, and the counts become the scope denominator.
2. The enlarged kitchen and bathroom elevations. Cited constantly, and by sheet and detail number in
   the more careful proposals. The most drawing-anchored proposal in the corpus counts each cabinet
   family against the specific plan or elevation sheet it was counted from.
3. The casework details and the interior details. These carry the panel conditions, the island
   configurations and the removability requirements.
4. The finish and hardware marks. Cabinet marks, hardware marks and paint marks tie the cabinet to
   the scheduled product.
5. The specification, for construction standards, certification requirements and drawer
   construction. Bidders cite the section and page and then exclude what they will not do.
6. The appliance schedule, without which the shop drawings cannot be completed.
7. Any narrative or summary of changes document. Rarely present and rarely read, but one bidder
   priced a deduct off one, which means the governing answer sometimes sits outside both the drawings
   and the specification.

### Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number

- Whether the package is bought supply only or furnish and install, and who installs.
- Whether cabinets arrive assembled or knockdown.
- Union versus open shop for the installation, where installation is in scope at all.
- The tariff and duty regime at the time of order, on an increasingly imported product.
- Freight basis, which for an imported product can approach half the material cost.
- Lead time, and whether the project can wait twelve to twenty weeks after approved shop drawings.
- The shop drawing cycle itself: how many review rounds are included, who pays for further revisions,
  and when the supplier will agree to start.
- The deposit and release structure, and whether the project can fund a large pre-delivery deposit.
- Minimum release batch size, which constrains how the floors can be turned over.
- Whether the site can take a fifty three foot trailer, whether street permits are needed, and
  whether there is a hoist or elevator available free of charge.
- Whether the site will be ready when the product is, and who pays weekly storage if it is not.
- Whether protection after installation is expected, and who owns damage once material is staged.
- Whether a mock-up unit is required, and whether it may remain part of the finished work.
- Certification and material requirements: quality certification seals, woodworking standard grade,
  formaldehyde-free or FSC material, domestic content, severe use construction. Each is a material
  substitution with a real cost, not a paperwork item.
- Payment platform, certification program and insurance program participation, each quoted as a
  percentage of contract value by the minority who quote it at all.
- Warranty duration. One year is the market default. Two years is priced as an add by at least one
  bidder, which is the only place the delta becomes visible.
- Who counts the units, and whether everyone counted the same thing.

### The count problem is worth stating on its own

Unit count is the leveling currency of this trade. Every estimator sheet normalizes every bid to a
cost per residential unit and ranks on it, and several sheets carry a second denominator for
bathrooms because the bathroom count does not track the unit count.

But bidders disagree about the counts. On the same set, bathroom counts differ by one or two between
bidders, and one estimator recorded a check against a bidder's own count asking whether one was
missing. Island counts differ far more, because whether a movable island belongs to its kitchen type
is a drawing consistency question. If the set does not make the kitchen count, the bathroom count,
the island count and the accessible unit count unambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable and no
amount of leveling fixes it.

### Set-stage behavior

At design development, bidders price around missing information and say so. One called its number a
budget price outright because details and elevations were missing. Another priced from a plan set
with the spec book field marked no. Design development casework numbers should be read as unit rates
applied to a unit count, not as scope.

By construction documents and conformed sets, the more careful bidders switch to citing sheet and
detail numbers and appending long interpretation blocks. The most thorough of these ran roughly
eighteen numbered notes, each resolving one ambiguity by naming the sheet and elevation it assumed:
which kitchen type follows which elevation, which bathroom type carries a linen closet, which sink
bases are accessible and removable, which unit shows a kitchen type that has no elevation drawn at
all. That block is an unrequested RFI log embedded in a bid, and it is the single most useful
document a casework reader can find. Read it as a list of drawing defects, not as a list of
assumptions.

Note also that a proposal's stated basis and its folder's set stage disagree often enough to check. A
bid filed in a construction document round may cite the earlier permit set by date and say so.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- The residential unit, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator sheet,
  without exception.
- Each, for kitchens, vanities, islands, tall linens, tall pantries, wall cabinets above toilets and
  medicine cabinets. The counting unit in every quantified proposal.
- Each, for pulls and knobs, counted at the piece level by bidders who read the hardware marks. Pull
  counts run into the thousands on a large project and are priced per piece or against a stated per
  piece allowance.
- The room, for amenity and common area casework. The only sensible unit there, and the one bidder
  who priced amenity rooms individually priced them by the architect's room number.
- The building, where the project is physically more than one.
- The truckload or container load, for delivery. Truck counts in the low tens are normal on a
  multifamily project, and one bidder priced twenty two trailers.
- The delivery, where deliveries are counted rather than trucks.
- The truck week, for storage.
- Working days, for installation duration, and weeks for lead time from approved shop drawings.
- The hour, for shop drawing revisions beyond the included rounds and for open shop labor.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, payment platform fees, certification and insurance program
  fees, contract processing fees and escalation caps. The only way any of these is ever expressed.
- The mile, in one case, as the basis for a delivery charge.

Square feet and linear feet do not appear. This trade does not think in area at all.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Whether installation is in the number. It is the largest single component after the cabinets
   themselves and it is absent from most bids.
2. Door style and construction grade. Slim shaker versus slab is the recurring choice, and the slab
   deduct runs from about a fifth to about a quarter of the cabinet value. Painted shaker and
   lacquered shaker bidders sit at the top of every ranking.
3. Whether the bidder priced the catalogue or the drawn design, as set out in section six.
4. Box material and construction. Particleboard versus plywood, and thermally fused laminate versus
   thermofoil versus painted. A plywood upgrade is a large add and a standard-construction deduct is
   a large save.
5. Countertops in or out, and if in, thickness and colour group.
6. Freight and tariff. On an imported product these together can rival the door style decision.
7. Panels. The kitchen surround panels, refrigerator tall panels and peninsula panels together carry
   the largest deducts offered in the trade, and a deduct removing the kitchen surround can be worth
   a meaningful share of the whole cabinet number.
8. Hardware grade, and whether pulls are specified product or allowance.
9. Drawer construction. Dovetail versus metal box is a distinct add or deduct on several proposals,
   and one bidder offered dovetail as an add while citing the specification section that required it.
10. Accessible unit requirements: removable sink and cooktop bases, angled aprons, finished
    interiors behind removable fronts, retractable breadboards, oven cabinet configurations.
11. Amenity and common area casework, which is a whole second package.
12. Union versus open shop, where installation is in scope. Roughly a quarter higher.
13. Certification and material requirements, which are substitutions rather than paperwork.
14. Storage, remobilization and phasing, which only bite when the schedule slips but bite hard when
    it does.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest comparable cost per unit runs from about 1.9 times on a small project to about 3.5
and 3.7 times on the large ones. Bidder counts per project run from twelve to twenty two. Two
cautions on reading that range. The bottom of the ranking is usually populated by bids that are not
comparable at all: a countertop-only bidder filed in the casework tab, a partial-scope number, an
internal budget. And the top is usually a bidder who priced the specified product against a market
pricing a substitute. In this trade neither end of the ranking means what it appears to mean, and
the useful spread is the middle.

One more convention worth knowing. Where a bidder includes something without pricing it, an estimator
records its own valuation in the cell in plain words rather than leaving it blank, so an unpriced
inclusion still enters the comparison at an assigned worth. Fixed and movable islands are the items
this happens to most often.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly thirty casework and cabinet proposals, one dedicated casework
installation proposal, one finish carpentry install-only quote, one amenity millwork proposal and
four estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional
market, all bid by one general contractor. Unit counts across those projects run from forty to three
hundred and twenty five. The proposals span design development, permit, construction documents and
conformed sets, and the bidder pool includes many companies that appear on three or four of the
projects, which is what makes the boilerplate versus project signal distinction in section four
testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity space, in one market, through
  one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors, institutional, healthcare,
  laboratory or educational casework, which are different products with different standards and a
  different furnish and install culture.
- Amenity and common area millwork is a separately read trade. What is here about the boundary comes
  from the unit-side documents plus one amenity proposal, so the millwork side of the seam is
  described from outside it.
- Countertops are likewise a separate package, described here only where they collapse into a
  casework proposal. The stone supply chain's own conventions are not covered.
- No signed purchase order or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which
  deviations from the drawn design survived negotiation and which were bought out.
- No shop drawing set and no shop drawing review record is in the corpus. Since more than one bidder
  states that the approved shops, not the bid documents, govern the delivered scope, the most
  consequential document in this trade is entirely absent from the evidence base.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the shop drawing gate, the release batch
  requirement and the continuous installation condition convert into change orders is read out of the
  proposals' own language rather than observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the general contractor self-performed casework
  installation, so the install pricing here is all subcontracted or supplier-quoted.
- The corpus has not shown owner-furnished casework, so the owner-furnished contractor-installed case
  is unexamined even though it is common in this trade elsewhere.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the casework and the countertops were bought from the same
  party under one contract, only where one party quoted both separably.
- Prevailing wage and union casework installation appear only as priced options and exclusions, never
  as an awarded basis, so the union premium here rests on one bidder's own rate card.
