# Appliances

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the appliance package.
Everything here is learned from real supplier proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real appliance suppliers can bid it and a real
estimator can level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 113100 (Residential Appliances) is the anchor, and it is the section an estimator's own
scope sheet names. Proposals themselves rarely cite a section number at all, which is the first
signal that this is not a subcontract trade. Where the package is carried as an owner allowance
rather than bid, the sheet drops to the division-level 110000 instead, so the section number tracks
the commercial shape more than the content.

The load-bearing fact about this trade, and the one that governs everything downstream: the bidder is
a supplier, not a subcontractor. Every bidder on every leveling sheet is classified as a supplier.
The proposal that arrives is a sales quote off a dealer's order system, not a construction proposal
written against a specification. It has a customer number, a salesperson, payment terms, a price
validity window and a restocking policy. It does not have a scope narrative, and usually it does not
have an exclusion list, because a purchase order does not need one. Treat this package as a purchase
with a service tail attached, and the rest of its behavior follows.

Four kinds of bidder show up, and they are not interchangeable:

- Regional appliance dealers with a commercial or builder division. The most common by a wide margin.
  They price the goods, sell delivery as a tiered service, and sell hookups as itemized options.
- Plumbing and appliance wholesalers. Less common but the most granular: they carry a catalogue of
  install and handling service codes and price each one against each appliance mark.
- Cabinet, countertop and finishes suppliers who also broker appliances. They fold the appliances
  into a multi-package quote, sometimes as a schedule and sometimes as a single line.
- The manufacturer direct, through an owner's national account. Seen once, arriving through the owner
  rather than through the bid list, and it is the cleanest pricing document of the four because the
  goods and the services are separately rated.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another spec section:

- Range hoods and over-the-range microwave hoods. Nearly always carried here, and on one project the
  owner's own bid form settles it by writing the appliance allowance as including kitchen hoods. That
  matters, because a hood is a ventilation device and a reader coming from the mechanical drawings
  will assume it belongs to the mechanical package. It does not.
- Garbage disposals. Nearly always supplied here, and nearly never installed here.
- In-unit laundry, washers, dryers, stacked laundry centers and all-in-one combination units. Always
  carried here rather than in plumbing.
- The whole accessory tail: power cords and pigtails, braided water and fill hoses, ice maker
  connectors, dryer vent kits and clamps, stacking and stack bracket kits, dishwasher install kits
  and elbows, anti-tip brackets, charcoal recirculation filters, trim kits, hood remote controls,
  washing machine pans, and sink top air switches for the disposal. These are always priced as
  separate lines and they are individually trivial and collectively substantial.
- Stovetop firestop devices above the range. Suppliers almost never mention these; one carried it as
  a priced accessory with its own install line, which is worth knowing because it is a life-safety
  item nobody else surfaces.
- Amenity and common-area appliances: private dining kitchens, coffee bars, club lounges, work rooms,
  package rooms, fitness towel areas. Carried here nearly always, and priced per room.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like appliances:

- Commercial and foodservice-grade refrigeration, such as a reach-in solid door unit in a package
  room. Some dealers carry it and others go silent, and an estimator plugs it when they do.
- Automatic coffee machines at an amenity coffee bar. No bidder seen carrying one, and an estimator
  plugs it to every bidder without exception.
- Fireplaces and pet grooming dryers. These land on the appliance scope sheet because they are
  equipment with a schedule mark, and no appliance bidder carries either, so both are plugged
  identically across the whole bid list.
- Panels and handles for panel-ready appliances. Always the millwork or casework package. Bidders say
  so explicitly and repeatedly.
- Fitness equipment, television and audiovisual equipment, and window treatments. Not seen in this
  package.

The packaging risk worth naming on its own: a casework and finishes supplier may quote appliances as
a single lump line inside a much larger proposal, with no schedule, no models and no quantities. On
one project that lump was the largest appliance number on the bid list by a wide margin and the least
substantiated, and it sat in the appliance folder looking like a bid. A number without a schedule
behind it is not comparable to a number with one, and in this trade the schedule is the whole
comparison.

## 2. How the market bids

Appliances are the inverse of a degenerate proposal trade. The default output is a full model
schedule with quantities and unit prices, and the reason is mundane: the dealer is quoting off an
order system, so itemization is free.

- Model-level line pricing with quantities and unit prices: nearly every proposal.
- A single lump for the package: rare, and when it happens it is a bundler folding appliances into a
  multi-trade quote, or an owner allowance standing in for a bid.
- The proposal will therefore tell you quantities, and this is the opposite of the situation in most
  finish trades. It will not tell you whether those quantities are right.

Three grains of enumeration appear, and which one you get changes how levelable the bid is:

1. Mark grain. The proposal reproduces the schedule marks from the drawings and prices each one, with
   the unit group it serves. The most levelable shape, and common among the dealers and wholesalers
   who actually opened the schedule.
2. Unit-type grain. The proposal segments by typical units, ADA units, studios, penthouse units,
   amenity rooms, and prices a model set per segment. Also common, and readily levelable if the unit
   type names match the drawings.
3. Flat model grain. Brand, model number, quantity, description, in one undifferentiated list with no
   marks, no rooms and no unit types. Some proposals do this, and it is the hardest to level, because
   you cannot reconstruct which unit each line serves and the quantities do not resolve to the unit
   mix.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Brand, model number, quantity and a manufacturer description for every appliance. Nearly always.
- Unit count, printed at the head of the quote as the scope denominator. Common, and it is the number
  the estimator will normalize on.
- Sales tax as its own separate line at a stated rate, applied to the goods subtotal. Nearly always,
  and this is a trade-defining habit. A base number in this trade is commonly pre-tax, and at a
  little over six percent on a package of this size the tax is a material sum. Never compare a
  tax-inclusive number to a tax-exclusive one.
- Delivery as its own priced line or block. Nearly always.
- A price validity window, commonly thirty days, sometimes tied to a purchase order by a stated date.
- Payment terms, deposit percentage, and whether retainage and pay-when-paid apply. Common.
- A tariff clause reserving the right to adjust. Nearly every proposal in the current market carries
  one, and they are not identical: some state the quote includes current tariffs, some state it
  excludes any contingency for future tariffs, and one states pricing cannot be guaranteed at all
  above a stated tariff threshold.
- Lead time. Stated by about half, and the stated ranges run from two or three months to secure
  product up to eight months, plus a notice period to schedule delivery.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Which unit each appliance goes in. Almost never. You get a quantity, not a distribution.
- Any reconciliation of its quantities against the drawings. Never. One dealer's form preprints an
  instruction that the general contractor is to confirm all models and counts, which is the trade
  saying out loud that it does not warrant the takeoff.
- Installation, unless asked. Commonly absent from the base number entirely.
- Warranty basis beyond the manufacturer default. Stated rarely, and usually only after an estimator
  asks by email.
- Bond cost and insurance basis. Almost never volunteered, always available on request, and quoted as
  a percentage of contract value.
- A conventional exclusion list. Most proposals carry none. The boundary of the work is carried
  instead inside the description of the delivery and install services, which is a very different
  place to have to look.

That last point is the one to internalize. In most trades you find the boundary by reading the
exclusions. In this trade you find it by reading the install line's description, and the descriptions
are written in verbs: plugged in, placed on countertop only, screw-in installation only, prong cord
attached, waterline and power cord fixed at the appliance end. A proposal with a rich install
description has a narrow scope and is telling you so precisely. A proposal with no install
description at all is supply only and is telling you nothing.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs and successive set rounds:

- Most change price only, with the model schedule and the quantities byte-identical. The movement is
  the information, and it is almost never explained. Movements seen include a small single-line
  adjustment worth well under half a percent of the quote, a broad set of unit-price reductions
  running from a few percent to about seventeen percent line by line, and a large drop on premium
  amenity models of more than forty percent.
- The reductions are usually uneven across the schedule rather than a flat percentage. That unevenness
  is the signature of a manufacturer program re-quote rather than a margin cut, and it means you
  cannot level a revision by applying a single factor.
- A revision sometimes changes nothing but the manufacturer, holding every mark, quantity and
  qualification constant. This is the trade's dominant value-engineering move and it deserves its own
  treatment, below.
- A revision sometimes changes no price materially and instead adds a page explaining what the
  install actually covers. One bidder answered a leveling question by attaching a one-page matrix
  stating, per appliance family, the physical act performed and who performs the connection beyond
  it. That revision moved the understanding far more than it moved the number.
- A revision may exist only as an email sentence. Warranty deducts, bond percentages and insurance
  positions routinely arrive that way and never appear on any proposal document.

The whole-package brand swap is worth stating separately, because it is how this trade does value
engineering and there is nothing else like it in the finish trades. The bidder reissues the same
schedule, the same marks, the same quantities and the same service scope, changing only the
manufacturer. On one project a bidder issued a specified package and a value-engineered package on
the same day; on another, twelve days apart. The saving is real and large, but it is not uniform:
across one matched pair the refrigerator fell about thirty-five percent and the dishwasher about
thirty-four percent, while the ventless dryer rose about twelve percent. So a brand package cannot be
levelled as one number against another. It has to be compared mark by mark, because the substitution
wins on some families and loses on others, and because the substituted models differ in real
attributes: ice maker and water dispenser presence, ventless versus vented drying, capacity, and ADA
compliance.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Appliance scope sheets have run 50, 54 and 74 rows on projects that were actually bid, and 6 rows on
the project carried as an owner allowance. Target roughly fifty to eighty scope lines for a full
multifamily appliance package. The 74-row sheet is not the most detailed on appliances; it is the one
where the estimator had to enumerate the unit mix on the sheet itself because the documents did not
settle it.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every schedule mark. This is the primary organizing axis. Marks come through as U-AP-1 through
  U-AP-12, REF-1 through REF-3, OVEN-1 through OVEN-3, MW, DW, RH, GD, WD, AP and EQ series. Both the
  proposals and the estimator sheets key to marks, and a scope sheet that does not carry the mark
  cannot be reconciled against a proposal that does.
- Unit type, wherever the appliance set changes: typical or standard units, ADA or accessible units,
  studios and micro units, one bedroom, two bedroom, penthouse units, and any lettered unit-type code
  the drawings define. ADA always earns its own rows, because the ADA set is a genuinely different
  appliance list, commonly substituting a cooktop and a separate wall oven for a slide-in range and
  requiring ADA-compliant dishwashers and hoods.
- Amenity and common-area appliances, one line per room and per mark: private dining, coffee bar,
  club lounge, work room, package room, entertainment kitchen, mail and trash area, fitness. These
  rooms carry premium and specialty equipment and they are where bidders go silent.
- Every accessory as its own line: power cords by appliance and voltage, water lines, ice maker
  connectors, braided fill hoses, dryer vent kits and clamps, stacking and stack bracket kits,
  dishwasher connectors and elbows, anti-tip brackets, charcoal filters, hood remote controls, trim
  kits, washing machine pans, sink top air switches. Every proposal that itemizes at all prices these
  separately, and a bidder can be complete on appliances and short on accessories.
- Install, broken by appliance family rather than as one line: refrigerator, range, cooktop, wall
  oven, hood, over-the-range microwave, countertop microwave, dishwasher, disposal, washer, dryer,
  stack kit, laundry center, panel-ready units, microwave drawer. The install scope differs per
  family and is priced per family by the bidders who price it at all, so a single install row cannot
  distinguish them.
- Delivery, as its own line, and specify the tier. This is not a formality; see section 5.
- Vertical transport, as its own line. Elevator or lift provision is a stated condition on nearly
  every proposal and a priced escalating charge on some.
- Sales tax, as its own line. It appears as a line on nearly every proposal and on every estimator
  sheet that levels this trade, precisely because some bidders include it and some do not.
- Warranty term, as its own line, with the term stated. It is a priced variable in this trade.
- Bond and insurance basis, as their own lines, each expressed as a percentage of contract value.
- Every panel-ready appliance, individually, because each one creates a millwork dependency and a
  hinge-handedness question that a plain appliance line will hide.
- Hinge handedness and door swing wherever a unit is not reversible. One proposal priced a left-hand
  and a right-hand beverage center as two separate lines and asked the general contractor to confirm
  which was needed; another priced a door hinge reversal across forty-four refrigerators as its own
  charge.
- The non-appliance equipment that lands on this sheet anyway: fireplaces, pet grooming dryers,
  coffee machines. Give them rows so the plug is visible rather than buried.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual dwelling units. Collapse to unit type. No document in this trade prices a single named
  apartment.
- Finish and color. Stainless, white, black stainless and panel-ready are attributes of a line, not
  lines, except where panel-ready changes the trade seam, in which case the seam is the reason for
  the split, not the finish.
- Model number. An attribute of a mark, not a line of its own. The mark is stable across brand
  packages and the model number is not, so keying to the model number destroys your ability to
  compare a value-engineered package to a specified one.
- Floors and levels. Collapse, unless vertical transport is priced by floor, in which case the floor
  bands are a delivery line, not an appliance line.
- Individual accessory quantities per unit type. One accessory line per accessory type is enough; the
  quantity does the rest.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
appliances, that test fires hardest on the install seams in section 5, on delivery tier, on the
accessory tail, and on the amenity rooms, which is why those get lines even when the drawings show
them as one mark on a schedule.

There is a second test peculiar to this trade, and it is about quantity rather than inclusion. If a
line's quantity could differ between two bidders reading the same drawings, it earns its own row with
the quantity stated. This is not hypothetical. On one project the estimator recorded each bidder's own
count against each appliance line, writing has 66, has 64, has 54, has 50 across the same row.
Bidders disagreed with each other and with the schedule about how many of each unit type exist. A
scope sheet that states the quantity converts that disagreement from an invisible pricing difference
into a visible answerable question, and a scope sheet that omits the quantity lets it through.

Silence is a finding here too, and it has a specific shape. Because most proposals carry no exclusion
list, silence is the normal way scope is omitted. A bidder that does not mention the amenity rooms
has not excluded them; it has simply not priced them, and the number will look competitive because
of it. Any mark absent from a proposal is a gap to surface, never an inclusion to assume.

### The structural gap list

These are the items that recur as plugs on the estimator's sheets, meaning scope no bidder reliably
carries that has to be added to every number before comparing. A scope line set that names all of
them is doing the plugging in advance:

1. Installation, in whatever form the project needs it. The single largest and most consistent plug.
2. Vertical transport, hoisting or lift provision.
3. Amenity specialty equipment: coffee machines, fireplaces, pet grooming dryers, commercial
   refrigeration in package rooms.
4. Individual amenity appliances where a bidder priced units only.
5. Sales tax, against bidders quoting pre-tax.
6. Delivery, against bidders quoting tailgate or ex-works.
7. Warranty extension beyond the manufacturer default.
8. Bond, at a percentage of contract value.
9. General liability insurance where no owner-controlled program applies, or where a bidder declines
   to participate in one.
10. Panel-ready panel and handle supply, if the millwork package has not picked it up.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

Most of these are stated inside an install description rather than in an exclusion list, which is why
they are easy to miss. Their value is that they name the trade that must pick the work up, and if no
other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| Excluded by the appliance supplier | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Final water connection at the dishwasher; full dishwasher installation (nearly all) | Plumbing, and the appliance vendor's priced install often buys only the cord |
| Garbage disposal installation (nearly all, even inside install options) | Plumbing |
| Refrigerator water box and the supply behind it (nearly all) | Plumbing, before the appliance arrives |
| Laundry pans and shutoff boxes where required (some) | Plumbing, before appliance installation |
| Final hardwire connection at cooktops and wall ovens (nearly all) | Electrical |
| Final connection of hoods, and the remote switch in accessible units (nearly all) | Electrical |
| Hardwire at over-the-range microwaves and hoods (common) | Electrical |
| Final gas connections (common) | Plumbing or gas fitting, and only relevant where a unit type carries a gas range |
| Final HVAC connection and any duct cutting or transitions at hoods and vented microwaves (common) | Mechanical, and the appliance install attaches to existing ducting only |
| Panels and handles for every panel-ready appliance (nearly all who supply one) | Millwork or casework, delivered with hardware pre-installed |
| Anti-tip bracket installation (some, though others sell it as an option) | General contractor, or unassigned |
| Vertical transport above the ground or second floor (nearly all) | General contractor, and priced back as a carry charge if not provided |
| Offload equipment, forklift and lifting equipment (some) | General contractor |
| Receiving, signing for and distributing the goods on site (some, on tailgate terms) | General contractor |
| On-site storage of delivered goods (some) | General contractor, in areas it designates |
| Packing and carton removal (a minority; most carry it to a contractor dumpster) | General contractor, and the dumpster is the contractor's in every case |
| Bonding of any kind (commonly stated) | Priced back as a percentage of contract value on request |
| Owner-controlled insurance program participation or credit (one bidder, flatly) | Unassigned, and a real conflict where the project runs such a program |
| Extended warranty beyond the manufacturer term (common) | Unassigned, or bought as a priced adder |
| Any scope not named in the install list, stated as to be delegated by the general contractor (common) | Whatever package the general contractor decides, which is the point of the clause |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate. The dealers repeat their house terms verbatim across every
project, and repeat bidders make up a good share of any appliance bid list. One bidder's laundry
clearance note, breaker warning and elevator condition appear word for word on four different
projects. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- A named mark that is quoted with a substituted model, with a note saying the specified model is
  discontinued. This happens often enough to expect it, and it means the schedule and the quote no
  longer agree on model number by the time of bid. The mark still matches; the model does not.
- A note that a specified appliance is not compliant for the unit it is scheduled into. One bidder
  flagged that the dishwasher in one accessible unit type is not ADA compliant. That is a
  specification conflict found by a bidder reading the schedule more carefully than the schedule was
  written, and it is worth an RFI.
- A quantity that differs from the schedule's, especially where the bidder states the unit breakdown
  it used and cites the sheet it came from. When two bidders cite the same sheet and report different
  counts, the documents are ambiguous, not the bidders.
- A hinge or handedness question against a specific amenity appliance, with a note that the unit is
  not reversible.
- An appliance carved out of the quote entirely, such as accessible units priced separately or not at
  all. One proposal states plainly that it does not include the accessible units, which leaves a hole
  that the number alone will not reveal.
- A physical clearance note that constrains the architecture rather than the appliance. See section 6.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the quote into a change
order or a back charge. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- A working elevator or an outside lift must be available for deliveries above the ground or second
  floor, provided and paid for by the contractor. Stated by nearly every bidder. Where it is not
  provided, a carry-up charge is assessed, and at least one bidder publishes that charge as a rate
  per floor that escalates with height.
- Tenant-ready conditions. The most demanding version states that floors, paint, trim, cabinets,
  countertops, blocking and rough and finished mechanicals must be in place and prepared to accept the
  specified appliances before the quoted install prices hold, and that any modification needed to
  reach that condition is by others. Stated by the wholesalers; implied by the dealers in the softer
  form that if units are not ready there is an extra charge to remove packing and set in place.
- Unobstructed access on every delivery date, from the offloading and staging area through the
  building entrance, hallways and stairwells to the final install location. Stated commonly.
- Delivery based on full truck quantities. Stated by several, and it constrains phasing: the package
  cannot be released floor by floor without repricing.
- A price validity window, commonly thirty days, sometimes sixty, sometimes tied to a purchase order
  by a stated date. Nearly universal, and shorter than most trades.
- Escalation for delivery slipping into a later year. Stated as a percentage per year by more than
  one bidder, and at ten percent a year on a package this size it is a large number attached to a
  schedule risk the appliance supplier does not control.
- Tariff adjustment. Nearly universal now, in mutually inconsistent forms, so the tariff position is
  itself a comparison field.
- A deposit, commonly a quarter to a half of material and freight, and commonly nonrefundable on
  special-order product. Restocking fees on returns run high.
- Manufacturer model numbers subject to change and to substitution as needed. Printed on at least one
  dealer's standard form, which means the schedule you bid is not necessarily the schedule you get.
- Storage. Where goods arrive before the building can take them, storage is the contractor's problem
  or an added cost, and one bidder ties this to a delivery acceptance window measured in weeks after
  the goods reach the seller's warehouse.
- Warranty running from date of occupancy per unit rather than from delivery. Offered by one bidder as
  a better answer than a flat two-year term, on the reasoning that unoccupied apartments otherwise
  burn warranty time. That is a genuinely multifamily-specific term and it is worth asking every
  bidder for.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section that matters most in this trade, because the package is furnish-dominant and
almost every dollar of dispute lives at the seam.

### The appliance end and the wall end

The governing boundary, stated almost identically by bidders who otherwise share no language, is that
the supplier's work stops at the appliance end of every connection. The recurring phrase is that the
waterline and power cord are fixed at the appliance end. The wall end, the valve, the receptacle, the
water box, the duct and the gas line belong to plumbing, electrical or mechanical. Everything else in
this section is a consequence of that line.

Read the install description as a verb list, because that is how it is written, and the verbs are
precise:

- Plugged in. The appliance is energized from an existing receptacle. Nothing is wired.
- Placed on countertop only. Applied to countertop microwaves and, revealingly, to cooktops. A cooktop
  that has been placed on the countertop is not installed.
- Screw-in installation only. Applied to hoods and microwave hoods. The unit is hung and no
  connection is made.
- Prong cord attached. Applied to ranges, wall ovens and dryers.
- Anti-tip bracket installed. Applied to ranges, and some bidders sell it while others disclaim it.
- Level and set in place. Applied to refrigerators and laundry.
- Stack kit installed and dryer door reversed. Applied to stacked laundry.
- Hardwire by electrician, will plug in the unit if corded. The clearest single sentence in the trade.

### The per-appliance seam map

- Refrigerator. Supplier delivers, levels, sets, and connects the water line hose to the refrigerator
  and to the water box installed by the plumber. The water box is the plumber's and must exist first.
  One bidder adds the condition that if water is not turned on in the building and the unit at
  installation, the valve at the box is not opened and the general contractor or plumber must return
  once water is on. That is a second trip, and nobody carries it.
- Range and slide-in range. The most fully installed appliance in the package: cord attached, anti-tip
  bracket installed, levelled and set. Usually complete.
- Cooktop. Set in place only. The hardwire connection is the electrician's, always.
- Wall oven. Cord attached or set only. The hardwire connection is the electrician's, always. Multiple
  bidders write that cooktop and wall oven installation is something your electrician is to do.
- Hood and over-the-range microwave. Hung, and attached to existing ducting where vented, with
  charcoal filters supplied where recirculating. Cutting and transitions are excluded, the final HVAC
  connection is excluded, and the hardwire is the electrician's. The remote switch some accessible
  units require is the electrician's to provide and install.
- Dishwasher. The most misleading line in the package. A dishwasher install line commonly exists and
  commonly buys only the cord and the water line at the appliance end. The full installation is the
  plumber's. Reading the line without the note beneath it will overstate coverage by a wide margin.
- Garbage disposal. Supply only, essentially always, and one proposal spells out that the general
  contractor is to delegate the proper scope. The sink top air switch, where used, is supplied here
  and wired elsewhere.
- Laundry. Cords and hoses attached, stacked where applicable, vent attached to existing duct with a
  stated hose length limit and clamp count. Pans and shutoff boxes, where the design uses them, must
  be installed by the plumber before the appliances arrive.
- Panel-ready anything. The panel and the handle are the millwork package's. At least one bidder
  requires the panel to be on site with the cabinet hardware already installed by the carpenter before
  the appliance can be set, and then charges to hang it. So a single panel-ready appliance generates
  work in three packages and a sequencing dependency between two of them.

### The delivery tier is a scope decision, not a logistics detail

Delivery is sold as a ladder, and the rungs carry materially different scope and materially different
prices. Named tiers seen, from narrowest to broadest:

1. Tailgate delivery only. Goods arrive at a designated area at the site. The buyer receives, signs
   for and distributes. The narrowest, and the one most likely to be mistaken for a normal delivery.
2. Drop off. Box and exterior packaging removed, units left in a secure area inside the building,
   nothing above the first floor, no detailing, no install.
3. Put in place. Supervisor on delivery, box removed, appliances left in the specific units, no
   detailing or install.
4. Delivery into unit, uncrate and set. Appliances brought to the final install location, uncrated,
   debris to the contractor's dumpster, protective plastic left on for final cleaning.
5. White glove. All of the above plus interior packaging removal, set in the designated spots, and a
   defined set of basic hookups, commonly the electric range, washer and dryer cords and levelling.

One bidder priced three of these tiers side by side in a covering email, and the spread between the
narrowest and the broadest was substantial. Two proposals on the same project quoting different tiers
are not comparable, and the tier is often stated only in prose, only on the delivery line, or only in
an email. A scope sheet that names the tier forces the answer.

Vertical transport rides on top of this. Nearly every bidder conditions its price on a
contractor-provided elevator or lift, and one publishes a stair carry-up charge per floor that rises
steadily with height, so the eleventh floor costs several times the second. On a mid-rise with a
freight elevator that is not yet running, that schedule is a real exposure. One bidder draws the line
at the third floor: set in place up to and including the third floor, and above that exclusively by
freight elevator.

### Other seams

- Casework and millwork. Beyond panel-ready panels, the cabinet above the refrigerator, the tall oven
  cabinet, the drop-in stove cabinet and the scribe moulding all belong to casework but are sized by
  the appliance. A cabinet supplier's own note stating that the cabinet above the refrigerator is
  built to a stated depth including the fridge panels is an appliance dimension governing a casework
  line. If the appliance is substituted, that dimension moves.
- Finish carpentry. The carpenter pre-installs hardware on the panel and, on at least one bidder's
  terms, must do so before the appliance can be set. One estimator note records that a particular
  installer can absorb adjacent trades' work such as carpentry, so the seam is negotiable with some
  bidders and rigid with others.
- Electrical. The largest counterparty. Every hardwired appliance, every hood switch, every accessible
  unit's remote switch, and the receptacles behind every corded appliance. There is also a real
  compatibility issue: multiple proposals from more than one bidder carry an explicit warning that
  the quoted ranges may trip arc-fault circuit interrupter breakers and that the electrician should be
  consulted. That is an appliance selection driving an electrical design decision, and it appears
  nowhere on any drawing.
- Plumbing. The water box, the dishwasher connection, the disposal, the laundry pan and shutoff box,
  and the sequencing requirement that all of them precede the appliance delivery.
- Mechanical. Duct provision, cutting and transitions at every vented hood and microwave. Where the
  design uses recirculating hoods instead, the appliance package supplies the charcoal filter and the
  mechanical seam largely disappears, which makes vented versus recirculating a package-boundary
  decision and not just a performance one.
- General conditions. The dumpster is the contractor's in every proposal that removes packaging.
  Storage, offload equipment, site access and the elevator are the contractor's in most.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What appliance suppliers actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The appliance schedule. This is the primary document, and the marks come from here. Proposals cite
   it by sheet number and by mark range, and the more capable bidders reproduce the mark structure
   line for line. Where a bidder cites a sheet, it cites the appliance schedule sheet and sometimes
   the amenity schedule sheet alongside it.
2. The unit matrix or unit mix. Bidders cite it explicitly, sometimes naming the general information
   sheet it came from and reproducing the breakdown: studio count, typical count, accessible count,
   total. This is the second document and it is the one that goes wrong.
3. The unit count, as the scope denominator. Nearly every proposal prints it, and every estimator
   sheet normalizes every bid to a cost per unit and ranks on it. Unit count is the leveling currency
   of this trade. If the set does not make the unit count and the unit-type distribution unambiguous,
   every bid becomes incomparable, and this trade will not tell you that has happened because each
   bidder will simply price its own reading.
4. Unit plans, for appliance placement, door swings and hinge handedness. Rarely cited, but a priced
   hinge reversal across dozens of refrigerators can only have come from reading them.
5. The specification, for the basis-of-design manufacturer and model. Cited less often than the
   schedule, and commonly overtaken by a substitution before bid.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff of anything. The method is: read the schedule, read the
unit matrix, multiply, add accessories, price delivery.

The unit mix problem deserves its own paragraph, because it is this trade's characteristic failure and
it has no equivalent in the surface trades. Appliance quantities are pure counts, so they look
objective, and they are not. On one project four bidders reading the same set reported four different
counts for the same appliance line, differing by more than twenty percent between the highest and
lowest on some lines. Quantities also fail to resolve against the unit count in ways that are entirely
legitimate: a refrigerator quantity one less than the unit count because one unit takes a different
model, a dishwasher count that splits across three sizes, a laundry count that differs from the unit
count because some units take a combination unit. The consequence is that you cannot audit an
appliance bid by checking whether the quantities equal the unit count. You have to check them against
the unit-type distribution, mark by mark, which is only possible if the scope sheet carries the
quantity per mark.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Which manufacturer package is actually going to be bought. The largest single driver, and it is an
  owner decision that arrives late.
- Whether installation is in this package at all, and if so which of the delivery and hookup tiers.
- Whether the specified models are still in production. Discontinuation and substitution are routine.
- Whether an elevator or lift will be available at delivery, and from what date.
- The delivery date and the release schedule, which drive escalation, storage and full-truck
  constraints.
- Whether the building and the units will be tenant-ready when the appliances land.
- Whether water is on at the time of appliance installation.
- Lead time and the notice period the supplier needs, which on the long end reaches most of a year.
- Sales tax treatment, and whether the compared numbers include it.
- Warranty term, and whether it runs from delivery or from occupancy per unit.
- Bond requirement and rate, quoted as a percentage of contract value in the low single digits.
- Insurance basis, and specifically whether an owner-controlled program applies and whether the
  supplier will participate in one. At least one will not, and offers no credit.
- Tariff exposure and how the contract allocates it.
- Whether panel-ready appliances have a millwork package that knows about them.
- Whether hoods are vented or recirculating.
- Whether dryers are vented, ventless or heat pump, and whether laundry is stacked, side by side or a
  combination unit. This changes the appliance, the electrical load, the mechanical duct and the
  laundry closet dimension all at once.
- Whether any unit type carries gas, which introduces a fuel and a trade that no other unit type
  needs.
- Site access, staging area, offload equipment and designated storage.

There is one more category the drawings will not give you, and it runs the other way: appliance
suppliers state physical constraints that constrain the architecture. More than one proposal carries
laundry closet minimum clearances expressed as a required width and depth beyond the machine
dimensions, a required door opening width where a laundry pan is used, and a required counter height
from finished floor for best fit. These are printed as routine notes on a sales quote and they are in
fact dimensional requirements on the unit plan. If the closet as drawn does not meet them, the
appliance does not fit, and the note is the only place anyone says so.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, proposals price against a partial schedule and the
quantities are soft; the estimator's own sheet may carry open questions in place of scope rows,
recording that panel-ready extent, penthouse appliance level, laundry configuration and vented versus
ventless could not be resolved from the documents. By permit and construction documents the same
bidders cite specific sheets and unit breakdowns and the mark structure firms up. What does not firm
up with the set is the manufacturer package, which continues to move through conformed sets and into
buyout, so a late appliance number moving is normal and does not imply a document change.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, per appliance, at a unit price. The dominant priced unit, and it is the same unit in the
  schedule, in the proposal and on the leveling sheet, which is unusual and helpful.
- Each, per accessory. Cords, hoses, kits, filters and connectors are all priced per each and they are
  individually small.
- Each, per install act, per appliance family. The wholesalers and the manufacturer publish these as
  rated service codes.
- Per unit, for the flat installation option. Some dealers collapse the entire install into one
  per-unit adder covering connection to existing plumbing and electric. That adder is a small fraction
  of the per-unit appliance cost, on the order of a twentieth, which is a useful sanity check on
  whether a bidder has really priced installation.
- Per unit, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator sheet, without
  exception.
- Per shipment and per truck, for delivery. One dealer prices delivery as a count of shipments with a
  stated maximum number of units per truck, which makes the delivery cost a function of the release
  schedule rather than of the unit count.
- Per floor, for stair carry-up, escalating with height.
- Lump sum, for delivery, for fuel and distribute, and for the whole package where a bundler quotes
  it.
- Hours at an hourly rate, for the estimator's hoisting, install and cleanup plug on one project.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, quoted in the low single digits, and for general liability
  insurance.
- Percent of the goods subtotal, for sales tax, at a little over six percent, applied to goods and not
  to services.
- Percent per year, for escalation on deferred delivery.

That goods and services tax split is worth stating plainly, because it changes arithmetic. On the
manufacturer's quote the tax is computed line by line, and the install and handling service lines
carry no tax while the goods lines do. So an appliance package total is not the goods total times one
plus the tax rate, and a bidder who quotes a heavier service component carries proportionally less
tax. Comparing two bidders on a tax-inclusive total silently rewards the one selling more service,
which is backwards.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. The manufacturer package. Dominant, and the reason the whole-package brand swap is the trade's
   standard value-engineering move.
2. Model level within a package. Counter-depth versus standard depth, French door versus top freezer,
   ice and water versus neither, capacity, and smart features each move a single mark substantially.
3. The unit mix and unit count, which set every quantity.
4. Whether installation is in the number, and at which tier. This is the difference between a supply
   quote and a supply-and-install quote and it is the single largest plug the estimator adds.
5. Laundry configuration. Stacked pairs, unitized laundry centers and all-in-one combination units are
   very different prices, and ventless and heat pump dryers cost materially more than vented ones.
6. The accessory tail, which is individually trivial and collectively a real percentage of the
   package.
7. Amenity and common-area appliances, which are few in count and premium in price. A handful of
   amenity units can carry a per-appliance cost several times any unit appliance, so silence on the
   amenity rooms flatters a number more than its line count suggests.
8. Delivery tier and vertical transport.
9. Sales tax treatment.
10. Escalation, lead time and the delivery year.
11. Tariff exposure.
12. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.
13. Panel-ready count, which moves this package modestly and the millwork package more.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per unit is much tighter than in a labour trade, running at about 1.09, 1.31
and 1.40 times across the projects that were competitively bid. That tightness is real and it is
because everyone is buying similar goods from similar channels. It is also deceptive: the spread
narrows further once the plugs are applied, and the apparent low bidder is frequently the one carrying
the least service. Measured against the general contractor's own internal budget the gap is much
wider, around 1.6 times on more than one project, which says the trade is routinely underbudgeted
early. Bidder counts per project run from four to ten, far fewer than a labour trade.

One outlier pattern worth recognizing: a bidder total roughly three times the allowance, annotated on
the sheet as three quotes, is a stacked sum of separate packages rather than a comparable bid. In a
trade where several priced options from one bidder commonly arrive in one file, totals get added
together by accident. A number that is a multiple of everything around it is almost always a summing
artifact, not a price.

Finally, an estimator does not compare base bids in this trade any more than in any other. It compares
base bid plus adjustments, and the adjustments here are unusually large relative to the base, because
the base is usually goods only. The plugs to expect are the ten in section 3. A scope line set that
names all ten explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging for it, and in a trade where the base bids
sit within a third of each other, the plugs are what actually decide the order.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly forty appliance proposals and four estimator leveling sheets,
drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general
contractor. The proposals span design development, permit, construction documents and conformed sets.
The bidder pool includes several suppliers appearing on more than one project, which is what makes the
boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable, and it includes all four bidder
types described in section 1, including one manufacturer-direct quote that arrived through the owner.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential rental work with amenity space, in one market,
  through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against for-sale condominium work, where appliance
  specification and buyer upgrade options behave differently, and nothing is tested against
  hospitality, senior living, student housing, or single-family work.
- Only one project carried appliances as an owner-provided allowance, so everything said about that
  commercial shape rests on that project alone. It is common enough in the market to expect more of
  it, and the corpus cannot yet say how a general contractor scopes around an allowance it does not
  control.
- Only one manufacturer-direct quote is present, and it is the source of nearly everything said about
  unbundled install and handling rates. Those rates are a single observation.
- No signed purchase order, subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows
  which delivery tier and which install seam were actually bought, or which qualifications survived
  negotiation.
- No change order history and no field record is in the corpus. The claims that the tenant-ready
  qualification, the second-trip water box condition and the elevator condition convert into extra
  cost are read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed, so treat them as untested
  here.
- No submittal or shop drawing round is in the corpus, so the file cannot say how often the
  substituted models proposed at bid are actually accepted.
- The corpus has not shown commercial or foodservice kitchen equipment as a real package, only as
  single amenity items pulled into a residential appliance quote. Projects with a real amenity
  commercial kitchen will behave differently.
- The corpus has not shown gas-dominant unit appliances. One unit type on one project carried a gas
  range and everything else is electric, so the gas seam is barely tested.
- The corpus has not shown a project with in-unit appliance packages varying by rent tier or by buyer
  selection, which would break the assumption that a unit type maps to one appliance set.
- The corpus has not shown appliance replacement or renovation work, where removal and disposal of
  existing units, and matching into existing casework and utilities, become their own scope.
- The corpus has not shown a prevailing-wage or publicly funded project, where the supplier-versus-
  subcontractor classification and the install labour basis may not hold.
