# Siding

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the siding package,
meaning exterior cladding and the rainscreen assembly behind it. Everything here is learned from real
subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file speaks in calibrated plain language:
how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves. Where real proposals have not shown
something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real cladding contractors can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

One framing note before anything else. Painting is a trade where the proposal is nearly just a number.
Siding is the opposite. A siding proposal is a materials specification with a price attached, and most
of the money in it is not in the visible cladding at all. It is in the layers behind the cladding, in
the flashing and trim at every edge, and in a handful of product decisions the architect has usually
not closed by the time the package goes out to bid.

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Two section numbers anchor the package and they are used interchangeably by the same estimator across
projects: the metal wall panel section and the fiber cement siding section. Which one heads the
leveling tab tracks which cladding dominates the project, not any difference in what the package
contains. Air barriers, thermal insulation and joint sealants each carry their own section numbers and
each of them lands inside somebody's siding number on some projects and outside it on others. Treat
the section number as a weak identifier and the assembly as the strong one.

The package as bid is not "siding". It is the whole exterior wall build-up outboard of the sheathing:
cladding, its trims and reveals, its flashings, the continuous insulation, the girt or clip system
that holds the insulation and carries the cladding, the furring or hat channel over the girts, the
insect screen at the cavity, and the sealant at the cladding's own openings. Bidders price this as a
stack and describe it as a stack, layer by layer, keyed to wall assembly tags. A scope line that names
only the cladding has named perhaps two thirds of the number.

The bidder pool is the least stable of any trade seen. The same package on four projects drew bids
from panel fabricators, cladding specialists, framing contractors, a carpentry company, a drywall
company, a roofing company, a company whose letterhead lists painting and roofing alongside siding, an
interiors contractor, and a glazing contractor. There is no such thing as a siding contractor in the
way there is a painter. What each of these bidders considers naturally inside its package differs
accordingly, and that variation is the single biggest driver of apparent price spread.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another
division or another section:

- The continuous insulation and the girt or clip system. Carried by essentially every bidder and often
  priced as a named work group of its own. This is the largest collapse and it is nearly universal.
- Hat channel, furring and strapping over the girts. Same, though bidders differ on whether they carry
  it everywhere or only where it is drawn, and some say so explicitly.
- All flashing that is part of the cladding system: base, head, jamb, sill, top, corner, end wall and
  thru-wall. Carried nearly always, and nearly always bounded by the phrase "within our system".
- Backer rod and sealant at the cladding's own window and door openings. Commonly carried and always
  bounded, with sealant to dissimilar materials pushed back out.
- Access equipment. Booms, scissor lifts, telehandlers and staging are carried by nearly every bidder
  and are sometimes a priced line of their own. A few bidders name the equipment they refuse, such as
  swing staging or hydro mobiles.
- The mockup. Carried by about half, priced as an add by others, and plugged by the estimator on every
  project where a bidder is silent.
- The air and vapor barrier. Genuinely unsettled; see section 5, where it gets its own treatment.
- Exterior wood screens, privacy fences and slatted rooftop screens. Frequently pulled toward siding
  because nobody else wants them, and frequently excluded on the ground that the material is not
  defined on the drawings.
- Downspouts and drainage collection boxes, where they are color matched to the cladding. One bidder
  carried them and connected them to ground drains. Most say nothing, and gutters and downspouts
  appear on nearly every exclusion list, so this one is worth asking about directly.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like cladding:

- Copings, parapet caps, gravel stops and break metal at the top of wall. Excluded by nearly every
  bidder and usually assumed to be the roofer's, sometimes explicitly on the bidder's own assumption
  rather than on any document. Where the coping is the same composite material as the cladding, the
  cladding bidder does carry it, which makes coping ownership a material question rather than a trade
  question.
- Thin brick, masonry, precast and granite. Excluded by every bidder seen.
- Synthetic stucco. Excluded by every bidder seen, on every project where it appears, and plugged by
  the estimator on all of them.
- Prefabricated mechanical and rooftop screen systems. Excluded by the cladding bidders and bought as
  a system from a screen manufacturer, priced on a linear foot basis. But whether the screen wall gets
  clad on one face, both faces or neither is a live question; see section 5.
- Expansion joints, both the manufactured kind within the cladding and the structural kind at
  firewalls. Excluded by most and worth a large enough sum that it distorts a ranking on its own.
- Windows, doors and storefronts, and the flashing tie-in at the window flange. Excluded by most, and
  carried by a meaningful minority; see section 5.
- Sheathing, framing, blocking and light gauge framing. Excluded by every bidder seen.
- Roofing, standing seam metal roofing, scuppers and louvers.
- Painting. Excluded by nearly every bidder, including the ones whose own letterhead offers painting.

## 2. How the market bids

A siding proposal is a product specification. The default output is a segmented number with the
materials named down to manufacturer, series, profile, thickness, gauge, exposure, species, grade and
finish.

- Segmented into three to seven numbers: the majority, on every project. The segmentation axis is
  cladding system family first (fiber cement, metal panel, composite panel, wood), then building where
  there is more than one building, then the backup assembly as its own group. Segmenting by building
  is common on multi-building projects and is what the estimator wants.
- Single lump sum with a bullet list: a minority, and concentrated among the bidders who submit the
  thinnest documents.
- Priced line by line with quantities and rates: essentially never in a proposal. One bidder published
  a rate card of furnish-and-install rates by assembly layer and printed on it, in capitals, that it
  was not a proposal. That is the only rate publication in the trade.

So the realistic expectation is that a siding proposal will tell you exactly what products it intends
to install and will not tell you how many square feet of them there are. Stated areas appear in a
minority of proposals and, when two bidders both state areas for the same callout on the same set,
they disagree, sometimes by a tenth and sometimes by much more. Quantity is not a settled fact in this
trade even among bidders who measured.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The exact product for every layer, named. This is the trade's defining habit and it is close to
  universal. Manufacturer, product line, profile, thickness, gauge, exposure, color collection,
  fastening method, and for wood the species and the grade.
- The wall assembly tags each layer applies to. Common, and the strongest hook a scope engine has,
  because it connects the proposal to the partition-type sheet rather than to the elevations.
- The basis document with a date. Nearly always, though the date is unreliable; see below.
- Access equipment assumptions and what the site must provide for them.
- Union or open shop. Not always stated in the proposal, but recorded for every bidder on every
  leveling sheet, so treat it as a first-class comparison field regardless.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Quantities. A minority state areas; nobody states a full takeoff.
- Coverage of the whole facade. Most proposals are silent about at least one elevation, one wall
  assembly tag, or one building face, and the silence is only discoverable by comparing the wall tags
  the bidder lists against the wall tags on the partition sheet.
- Labor hours. Stated by one bidder, only after the general contractor asked by email.
- Engineering and delegated design. Nearly always excluded or priced as an add, on a system that will
  need stamped calculations. A bidder writing "no engineering value being carried" is normal.
- Attic stock and spare material. Named on a few exclusion lists and otherwise absent entirely.
- Warranty duration. Usually only after the general contractor asks; see below.

Exclusion behavior splits the market cleanly and matters more than anything else about proposal shape.
The strongest proposals carry exclusion lists running thirty to forty lines, each keyed to a sheet or
detail number. The weakest carry no exclusion list at all. A bidder with no exclusions is not offering
broader scope; it is leaving every boundary open, and at leveling every one of them becomes a plug or
a question. On one project the bidder with no exclusion list of any kind was also the one pricing a
cladding exposure narrower than the drawings called for, so a wide-open document is not evidence of
wide scope.

Beware the exclusion list that contradicts the priced scope in the same document. One proposal priced
the air barrier as a named work group with two substrate breakouts, worth roughly a ninth of its
total, and simultaneously listed the air barrier on its printed exclusion list. Boilerplate exclusion
lists are pasted in and not reconciled against the priced groups. Read the priced breakout as
authoritative and the printed list as a template.

Revision habits are the richest in the corpus and they follow four distinct patterns:

- The negotiation ladder. Four, five or six rounds over two months, price declining monotonically,
  scope text byte-identical after the first round. No reason documented beyond phrases like "after a
  meticulous review". This is the dominant shape on a large package that is heading toward award.
- The alternate-only revision. A round where the base bid does not move by a dollar and the entire
  content is that one or two priced deduct options have appeared. Ignoring these because "the number
  didn't change" throws away the round's whole message.
- The comeback absorption. The general contractor issues a standing checklist by email, and the next
  round folds a previously outside item into the base. On one project a base bid rose by exactly the
  value of a newly offered warranty deduct, because the second warranty year moved from outside the
  number to inside it. That base bid rise is not a scope increase, and comparing base bids across that
  ladder without reading the alternates shows the movement in the wrong direction.
- The breakout restructure. A bidder republishes the same scope split into the breakout headings the
  general contractor's bid form demanded. Sometimes no money moves. Sometimes a whole building area
  appears for the first time inside the new sub-lines, and the number rises for that reason rather
  than from any change in rate.

Two mechanical warnings about revision identity. First, the revision label on the file and the
revision label inside the document routinely disagree, and the offset can be two or three rounds,
because the bidder counts its own rounds across all its packages while the general contractor counts
only what it filed. Second, order rounds by the basis document each one cites, never by the letterhead
date. Template dates carry over: one proposal's letterhead predates the set it cites by more than five
months, and another types a plan date a full year in the future.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Siding scope sheets have run 19, 44, 60 and 63 rows, and the 19-row sheet covered a single penthouse
elevation. For a full multifamily facade, target roughly fifty to seventy scope lines. The grain has
to scale with the package: a one-condition carve-out does not need sixty rows, and forcing them
produces sixty empty cells that hide the four rows that matter.

The organizing axis is not area or room type, as it is in painting. It is the wall assembly. Every
strong estimator sheet and most strong proposals key their lines to wall assembly tags, and the
assembly is what determines the cladding, the insulation thickness, the girt type, the furring and the
flashings all at once.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every cladding callout on the exterior finish schedule, one line each. Not "fiber cement" but each
  mark separately, because marks differ by profile, exposure, thickness, fastening method and color,
  and bidders answer them one at a time. Every strong proposal in the trade is organized this way.
- Every wall assembly tag that carries cladding, separately from the cladding mark. The same cladding
  mark over metal studs, over wood studs, over concrete and over masonry is four different assemblies
  with four different backup builds, and bidders price them separately.
- The insulation layer, with its thickness stated. Its own line, because thickness is contested and
  because the insulated area is larger than the cladding area.
- The girt or clip system, with its depth stated, and separately, whether it is thermally broken. This
  is the single most consequential line in the trade. Whether the girt is thermally broken is worth
  more than a tenth of an entire package, and bidders split three ways on it: excluded with an add
  offered, included with a deduct offered, and excluded with no price at all.
- Hat channel or furring over the girts, its own line, and separately whether it is metal or pressure
  treated wood. The substitution is a standard value engineering move and it changes the visible
  fastener appearance, so it is not purely commercial.
- Every flashing condition, one line each: base, head, jamb, sill, top of wall, inside and outside
  corner, end wall, thru-wall, transition to masonry, transition at setback roofs, flashing at balcony
  ledgers, flashing above trim boards, and the flashing tape over thru-wall flashing. Strong proposals
  enumerate these by width and gauge; weak ones say "all flashings within our system", which is not
  the same scope.
- Trim, separately by condition: base wall, eave and rake, head and jamb at openings, horizontal
  bands, water table, and corner trims and reveals. Trim size and material substitutions are one of
  the trade's recurring deviations because manufacturers do not make every drawn size.
- Reveals and joint trims as their own line, enumerated by profile where the drawings name them.
  Proposals do enumerate them by part number, which means the reveal system is a purchased kit and its
  parts are a real scope statement.
- Sealant and backer rod, split by condition: within the cladding system, at the cladding's own
  window and door openings, to masonry, to dissimilar materials, and at penetrations. Bidders draw
  their line in different places among these five and each split is a different number.
- Soffits, each condition separately: canopy soffits, terrace soffits, balcony undersides, and
  exterior ceiling soffits. Each is answered differently and several sit right on the boundary with
  roofing or with the balcony supplier.
- Penetration treatment, its own line. One bidder counted more than a thousand mechanical and device
  penetrations needing a cladding trim block, priced it as an add, and noted that the detail depicts
  no block at all. That is the only counted element quantity in the trade and it exists because the
  bidder went looking for it.
- Insect screen and cavity venting at the rainscreen, its own line.
- Expansion joints, split into manufactured joints within the cladding and structural joints at
  firewalls. Both are frequently excluded and the structural one is expensive.
- The mockup, its own line, with the sheet it is drawn on.
- Field measurement, layout from control points, and who supplies the lift for measuring. Named
  separately by strong bidders.
- Shop drawings, delegated design and stamped calculations, as their own line, because they are
  almost always excluded or priced as an add.
- Touch-up of prefinished items and field cuts, and color matching or plugging exposed fasteners.
  Named on the general contractor's scope sheet and answered yes by bidders who otherwise exclude all
  painting.
- Fire performance compliance for the assembly and the submittals that prove it, its own line.
- Attic stock and spare material, its own line, because it is excluded by name where it is mentioned
  at all.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Elevation by elevation. Bidders name which elevations a mark occurs on, but nobody prices per
  elevation. Collapse to the mark and record the elevations as a note.
- Individual panel counts and piece counts. Cladding collapses to area by mark. No proposal prices per
  panel.
- Level by level. Cladding does not change by floor unless the assembly does, and where it does the
  assembly tag already captures it.
- Fasteners, closures, starters, terminations and manufacturer accessories. These collapse into the
  cladding mark line as attributes. Bidders list them for completeness, not as separately priced work.
- Color within a collection. Two colors of the same product on the same elevations are one line. A
  color outside the standard collection is not, because premium finishes are a named exclusion.
- Safety equipment, temporary covers, wall bracing and hoisting. These collapse into one general
  conditions block for the trade. Bidders itemize them at length and none of them is separately
  priced.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
siding, the test fires hardest on the backup assembly and on every edge condition, which is why those
get lines even though the drawings show them as one hatch pattern in a wall section.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. If a bidder's proposal enumerates seven wall
assembly tags and the partition sheet has ten, the three unnamed tags are a scope gap to surface, not
an inclusion to assume. This trade makes that check possible in a way most trades do not, because
bidders name their tags. Use it.

### The structural gap list

These are the items the market systematically will not carry, that an estimator adds to every number
before comparing. A scope list naming all of them is doing the plugging in advance.

1. Synthetic stucco.
2. Structural expansion joints at firewalls.
3. Penetration blocks.
4. The mockup.
5. Backer rod and caulking.
6. Painted break metal frames at openings.
7. Exterior screens and fences.
8. The field finishing of any unfinished cladding material.
9. Hoisting and access.

Add to that list the four items that are not plugs but questions the leveling cannot answer for
itself: girt thermal performance, insulation thickness, cladding finish state, and air barrier
ownership.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The deviation list comes first

In a trade where the proposal is a product specification, the highest-value paragraph is not the
exclusion list. It is the place where the bidder tells you its product does not match the drawings.
This happens constantly and it is where the real scope risk lives.

The recurring deviations:

- The manufacturer does not make the drawn size. Trim sizes are the usual casualty, and the bidder
  substitutes a composite or a different species and prices it, sometimes as an alternate. Drawn trim
  dimensions are frequently not purchasable.
- The manufacturer does not make the drawn species or profile. A hardwood specified at one board width
  is only available at another, so all boards change width. Cedar callouts get priced in pine.
- The furring depth is not a standard size, so the bidder prices the nearest standard and the total
  wall depth changes. A drawn cavity depth is an intention, not a purchasable dimension.
- The insulation thickness in the wall type detail disagrees with the thickness in the callout. Two
  bidders on one project disclosed this in writing and priced the detail; a third priced the thinner
  dimension silently; the rest never mentioned it. The same drawing conflict therefore sits inside the
  bid spread invisibly, and the cheapest number is cheapest partly because of it.
- The bidder adds reveals that are not on the drawings. One bidder added a horizontal panel reveal at
  every floor line, changing the elevation appearance, in order to keep the manufacturer's warranty
  valid. Warranty conditions can override drawn appearance, and the bidder who tells you is doing you
  a favor.
- The detailed material will not perform. A bidder disclosed that drawn break metal frames were too
  large for break metal and would oil-can, and offered extruded aluminum instead, noting the details
  would need modification for fastening.
- The exposure differs. One bidder priced a wood cladding at a narrower exposure than drawn, which
  changes material consumption by roughly a fifth and is stated in one word inside a bullet.
- Where a drawn layout cannot be built, because a piece is larger than the material or violates a
  minimum size, the bidder assumes a new layout optimizing waste. One bidder states this once per work
  group as standing policy.

None of these are exclusions and none of them are boilerplate. Each one is a place where the drawings
cannot be built as drawn and a bidder has already chosen a resolution. Surface all of them.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate: a repeat bidder reproduces the same list on every project,
and identical exclusion sentences appear verbatim in the proposals of unrelated competitors. Read
boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else:

- An exclusion keyed to a specific sheet or detail number. When a bidder excludes a soffit at a named
  detail, or a coping above a named wall assembly, or a screen wall at a named landscape sheet, it
  read the drawings and found something it will not do. The strongest proposals key most of their
  exclusions this way.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. A bidder excluding a rooftop screen because
  "material not defined", or noting that no plans exist for the parking garage elevations it is
  nonetheless pricing, is telling you the set is incomplete at that point. That is a documents problem
  and it is worth an RFI, not a leveling adjustment.
- An exclusion of a specific named product where the general product family is included. This usually
  means the bidder is substituting, and the substitution is in the qualifications rather than the
  exclusions.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a courtyard, a garage roof, a ventilation
  area, an interior elevation, a penthouse, a second building.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts a lump sum into a change
order.

- The sheathing must arrive in plane and in usable condition, installed per the manufacturer's
  recommendations. Some bidders state this outright. Out-of-plane sheathing is where a rainscreen
  installation goes wrong, and shimming is either counted on the bracket or it is not.
- Access to the site with the bidder's own equipment, and a stated clear perimeter around the
  building. One bidder states a specific perimeter dimension for its forklift and boom lift and makes
  the price conditional on it. Loading and lift access onto a garage roof or courtyard is a named
  requirement where the project has one.
- A laydown area for material storage and breakout. Cladding arrives in long lengths and is cut on
  site by most bidders, so laydown is a real constraint rather than a courtesy.
- No tower crane or full-time crane carried, with a stated assumption about which floors a forklift
  can reach. A tower crane requirement is a stated extra charge.
- Price hold periods. These run from seven days to thirty, with fourteen seen once. Seven-day holds
  are real and stated with a right to re-evaluate on award. In a trade this exposed to material
  pricing, a hold period is a scope condition.
- Escalation excluded, tariffs stated as included only when the general contractor asked.
- Sales tax, stated as included by most, and worth asking about because it is a meaningful percentage
  on a material-heavy package.
- Custom color excluded, standard color assumed, and premium finishes such as metallics, mica and wood
  grains excluded by name. The number holds only if the architect selects from the standard range.
- One year workmanship warranty is the market default, stated by several bidders and printed into the
  general conditions of others. Two years is what a general contractor asks for.
- Retainage terms, payment terms and lien rights, printed on the form.

### Contract terms as scope

Two bidders attach full general conditions pages that materially change what their number is worth,
and one of them states that its own conditions govern in the event of conflict with the contract
documents. The clauses that matter: work not unambiguously specified in writing as within scope is
excluded, with the stated intent of resolving all questions in the bidder's favor; pay-if-paid and
pay-when-paid disclaimed; liquidated damages disclaimed; retainage capped and reduced at a stated
completion percentage; punchlist rights limited to one list within a stated window; insurance
obligations frozen at the limits in place on the proposal date, with any increase chargeable; and a
unilateral right to void or reprice the proposal if work is not directed within thirty days.

A bidder that attaches this page and a bidder that does not are not offering the same product at
different prices. Capture it in the qualifications and say so at leveling.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section that carries the trade. Siding is defined more by its boundaries than by its
middle.

### Prefinished versus field painted: the seam that matters most

The painting side of this seam records that whether exterior cladding arrives prefinished or is field
painted is the single largest swing on the painting number. From the siding side, the same decision
looks like this.

It is a materials decision, it is made inside the cladding package, and the drawings do not resolve
it. What is seen:

- On one project the exterior finish schedule names a paint color against every cladding mark and the
  cladding is explicitly field painted. Every bidder priced primed panel and excluded painting. The
  painter takes the whole facade.
- On another project the same cladding family is priced by one bidder as factory prefinished with an
  opaque stain to a named color, by a second as stained with painting excluded, by a third as
  unfinished with staining offered as an add on request, and by a fourth as primed. Four bidders, four
  different finish states, one set of drawings and one set of callouts. Whoever is compared against
  whom, the comparison is invalid until the finish state is normalized.
- One estimator carries the field-finishing cost of an unfinished wood cladding as its own plug line,
  separate from the material line, on every bidder. That is the correct treatment and it is worth
  copying: material and finish are two rows, not one.
- A general contractor's estimator asked a cladding bidder, in plain words, whether there was a more
  economical prefinished plank or panel that would cut cost significantly and not require field
  finishing. The value engineering path runs through the cladding bidder, not the painter.
- Prefinished substitutions are then priced as value engineering by the cladding bidder: a prefinished
  composite in place of an unfinished hardwood was worth a fifth off that scope on one project, and
  the general contractor tracked two competing substitutions for the same item side by side.

Practical consequence: every cladding scope line needs a finish state on it, stated as one of
prefinished at the factory, primed for field finish, or unfinished. A line that says only the product
is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is worth more than most of the other variables in the trade combined.
Where the cladding is primed, the painting scope needs a matching exterior line and the two have to be
written together or the work falls in the gap between them.

### The air and vapor barrier: the second seam, and the least settled

Across four projects the barrier landed in four different places.

- Inside the framing package, applied in the panel shop before the wall panels ship. This is what one
  panelizing framer does, and the shop application is offered as a schedule benefit. Where the walls
  are panelized, this is the strongest arrangement and the cladding bidder never touches it.
- Inside the siding package, priced as a named work group broken out by substrate, with sheet barrier
  at some wall assemblies and fluid applied at others.
- Inside the siding package on a small project, where the cladding bidder also carries a scope line
  committing to confirm the barrier is fully installed, sealed, lapped and inspected before cladding
  begins.
- Outside everyone. On one project the estimator routed it to rough carpentry on the scope sheet and
  every cladding bidder answered no, except one that answered yes.

Two bidders also offered barrier installation as a priced add alternate on a project where they had
excluded it from base, which is how the general contractor found out what it was worth.

Because the barrier is the actual weather line and the cladding is a rainscreen in front of it, a gap
here is not a commercial problem. Give the barrier its own scope line, split by substrate, and give
the penetration flashing and the window flange tie-in their own lines beside it. The flange tie-in in
particular is excluded by name by at least one bidder and named by nobody else.

### Window installation inside the siding package

Window and door installation appears inside cladding-adjacent packages more often than the trade
names suggest. One bidder carries window and door installation, with flashing, as a named package
alongside its framing, barrier and siding packages, and quotes all four to the same general
contractor. On another project, window and door installation appears as a priced add alternate from
two different siding bidders on the leveling sheet. And a carpentry company bidding siding excludes
window installation by name, which is only worth saying because others carry it.

So when the window package looks thin, check the siding and framing proposals. The installation labor
is frequently there, priced as an add or buried in an envelope package.

### Sheathing, blocking and substrate

Excluded by every bidder seen, and worth its own lines anyway because the exclusions are specific:
sheathing, exterior gypsum sheathing, all wood blocking, blocking outboard of the barrier, blocking at
window sills where a bidder notes none is shown, blocking and starter flashing at the bottom of siding
over masonry, blocking at siding-to-masonry transitions, and nailers. Bidders also disclaim drilling
and coring at substrates. The bidder who notes "no blocking shown on window sills" while answering a
scope sheet has found a real drawing gap.

### Copings, parapets and the top of wall

Excluded by nearly every bidder and assumed to be the roofer's, sometimes explicitly as an assumption
rather than a fact. The exception is where the coping is the same composite material as the cladding,
in which case the cladding bidder carries it and excludes only formed metal copings. One bidder
offered a deduct to reduce the coping cap so it stops at the top of blocking rather than wrapping
over, which is a detail change with a real number attached.

### The mechanical and rooftop screen

Contested three ways in the same project file. The screen system itself is bought from a screen
manufacturer on a linear foot basis, quoted in one case as a single email sentence against a quantity
the general contractor took off itself. The estimator's own scope note records that the screen arrives
with its own panel and therefore needs no cladding, and every bidder answered accordingly. One bidder
nonetheless priced cladding to one face of the screen wall in its base and offered the second face as
an add, with the sheathing behind it by others. Three answers, one project. Ask the screen supplier
what its system includes before writing the cladding line.

### Wood screens, privacy fences and rooftop slat walls

These land on the cladding bidder by default because no other trade wants them, and they behave
unlike the rest of the package. The material is frequently not defined on the drawings, which some
bidders cite as their reason for excluding. Where they are priced, they are a large detachable block:
on one project the screen scope alone was quoted by one bidder at a tenth of its whole facade number,
and the general contractor tracked the material, the finishing of the material, and two competing
substitute materials as four separate rows. The steel posts supporting the screen are furnished and
installed by others in every case seen.

### Expansion joints

Both kinds sit on the boundary. Manufactured joints within the cladding are excluded by most.
Structural vertical joints at firewalls were carried in the base by one bidder, offered back as a
value engineering deduct worth roughly a tenth of its whole number, excluded by most others, and
plugged by the estimator on the bidders who excluded them. Give them their own line and expect the
answer to move.

### Downspouts and drainage

Gutters and downspouts appear on nearly every exclusion list. One bidder carried drainage collection
boxes and corrugated downspouts color matched to the cladding, and connected them to ground drains
with the transition fitting supplied by the site contractor. Where the downspout is a visible facade
element matched to the siding, ask the cladding bidder; where it is a plumbing element, it is not
theirs.

### Soffits and balcony undersides

Repeatedly contested. Perforated metal soffits under prefabricated balconies were excluded by one
bidder and quoted as an add worth a substantial fraction of the exterior scope, while another carried
them in base and offered a deduct to remove them. Canopy soffits, terrace soffits and exterior
ceilings each get answered separately. The insulated finish ceiling at a parking garage is excluded by
everyone.

### The girts, and the specification that does not match the drawings

The thermally broken girt deserves its own subsection because it is the trade's largest single
contested item. The pattern seen on one project: the specification requires thermal separation, the
drawings show plain galvanized girts, and the general contractor's own scope sheet carries two
adjacent rows asking whether each is included. Bidders answered in three different ways and the value
of the difference ranged from a large credit to a large add, wider than the gap between several
competing base bids. The general contractor eventually resolved it by printing a mandatory bid form
breakout demanding the girt value and a plain yes or no, rather than by fixing the drawings.

That is the wrong end to solve it from. A documents conflict between spec and drawings on a load-
bearing product decision belongs upstream as an RFI, not downstream as a leveling column.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What cladding contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The exterior partition or wall assembly sheet. This is the primary document, more than the
   elevations. Bidders quote assembly tags directly and organize their whole proposal around them. If
   the assembly tags on the elevations do not match the tags on the partition sheet, the trade cannot
   be bid cleanly. This mismatch is real and was recorded by an estimator as scope-row notes on one
   project: one tag not appearing where it should, and two others noted as substitutions for the tags
   used on the elevations.
2. The exterior finish schedule, for the cladding marks, their profiles, exposures and colors, and for
   whether a paint color is assigned to a mark.
3. The elevations, for which mark goes on which face, and for area measurement.
4. The typical window details and the section details, for flashing, reveals, trims and sills. This is
   where most of the enumeration comes from and where most of the deviations are discovered.
5. The specification, for coating standards, girt thermal performance, fire performance of the
   assembly and warranty duration. Bidders cite the section number and price the drawings, so where
   the two disagree the drawings usually win in the number.
6. Landscape sheets, for screens, fences and rooftop slat walls.

They do not price from a room-by-room or elevation-by-elevation takeoff. The normal method is
assembly-tag areas from the elevations, plus judgment, plus a materials list built from the details.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Union versus open shop labor. The largest single driver, and cleanly visible: on two separate
  projects the union bidders occupy the most expensive positions on the ranking in an unbroken block,
  and on a small package a general contractor carried two separate budgets for the same scope
  differing by roughly a quarter, with the union premium as its own line at its own rate.
- Whether the cladding arrives prefinished. See section 5.
- Whether the girt is thermally broken, where the drawings and the specification disagree.
- Which insulation thickness governs where the detail and the callout disagree.
- Whether the air barrier is in this package, the framing package, or nobody's.
- Whether the walls are panelized off site, which moves the barrier into a shop and changes the
  sequence.
- Whether the specified product and size are actually purchasable. Bidders find this out by calling
  their vendor; the drawings never say.
- The manufacturer's warranty conditions, which can require reveals, joint spacing or primers that the
  drawings do not show.
- Sheathing flatness and whether shimming is counted on the bracket or priced separately.
- Site access, clear perimeter, laydown area, and lift access onto garage roofs and courtyards.
- Whether a tower crane is available or a forklift must reach every floor.
- Escalation exposure and tariff status over the procurement window, and how long the bidder will hold
  its price.
- Bond requirement and its cost, quoted around one percent of contract value when asked.
- The insurance program, whether an owner-controlled program applies and what the credit is worth.
- Warranty term. One year is the market default; two years is what a general contractor asks for.
  One bidder priced the second year at roughly one percent of its base and another gave it away free,
  so the same requirement produces a priced line for one bidder and nothing for another.
- Delegated design and engineering responsibility, which nearly nobody carries.
- Mockup expectations. Bidders who price a mockup price it low; estimators plug it four to ten times
  higher.
- Attic stock and spare material quantities.
- Whether the general contractor will issue a scope sheet and a mandatory bid form breakout structure,
  which reshapes every subsequent round.
- Labor hours and crew size, available only by asking.

Two further set-stage behaviors worth knowing. At design development, bidders openly label their
numbers budgetary and pending final scope review, and they describe products as "assumed as per
elevations" rather than confirmed. Treat a design development cladding line as a product hypothesis,
not a selection. And a proposal's stated basis and its folder's set stage disagree often enough that
it must be checked every time: bids priced off a permit set land in the conformed-set folder, bids
priced off a six-month-old set land in a current-set folder, and letterhead dates carry over from
templates.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet of cladding, by mark. The primary unit, the leveling denominator, and the basis of every
  rate seen.
- Square feet of each backup layer separately: insulation, girts, hat channel, furring, barrier. These
  areas are not the same as the cladding area. On one project the estimator's insulated facade area
  exceeded its cladding area by roughly a fifth.
- Squares, meaning hundreds of square feet, used by a bidder whose primary trade is roofing. The same
  facade appears as squares in one proposal and square feet in another on the same project.
- Linear feet for trim, water table, caulking and backer rod, and for prefabricated screen systems.
- Each, for penetration blocks and for discrete elements. Rare, and the one instance in the corpus
  came from a bidder who counted more than a thousand penetrations nobody else had noticed.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, quoted around one percent.
- Residential units, used as a leveling denominator only where the cladding package is a small
  carve-out on a project whose other trades level on units.

The one published rate structure in the trade, taken from a rate card that disclaims being a bid,
gives the trade's cost shape more clearly than any proposal. Composite and metal panel cladding rates
run roughly ten to fifteen times the rate of a single backup layer such as insulation or furring, and
fiber cement runs perhaps two thirds of the composite rate. But there are four to five backup layers,
and taken together the backup assembly approaches a third of the cladding rate, before flashing, trim
and sealant. That is why a scope line naming only the cladding has named roughly two thirds of the
money, and why the girt line alone can swing a package by more than a tenth.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Whether the bidder is union or open shop. Visible as a clean block at the top of two separate
   rankings, and carried by one estimator as an explicit premium line at its own rate.
2. Whether the girt system is thermally broken. Worth more than a tenth of a package, and unresolved
   in the documents on the project where it mattered most.
3. Which cladding material governs. A wood-to-fiber-cement substitution on one project was priced by
   four bidders at roughly five, twenty-one, and twenty-eight percent of their respective bases, and
   as a flat replacement total by a fourth. The same substitution, four wildly different values,
   because each bidder's base carries a different amount of wood.
4. Whether the cladding is prefinished or field painted.
5. Total cladding area, and how much of the facade is cladding rather than masonry or glazing.
6. Whether the backup assembly exists at all. Deleting girts and exterior insulation was offered as a
   value engineering option worth over a tenth of one base bid.
7. Fastening method. Nails instead of screws on fiber cement was quoted at roughly one dollar per
   square foot of cladding, worth about four percent of that base.
8. Whether structural expansion joints at firewalls are in.
9. Access and equipment: staging, boom lifts, garage roof loading, and whether a tower crane is
   available.
10. Escalation, tariff exposure and price hold duration over the procurement window.
11. Sealant scope boundaries, which look small and add up across five contested conditions.
12. Custom versus standard color, and premium finish selection.
13. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within a single project, the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per square foot among full-scope bidders runs from about 1.95 times to about
2.4 times. Bidder counts per project run from two to thirteen. That is tighter than painting's spread,
which makes sense in a materials-dominated trade, but the tightness is misleading: two bids that agree
within ten percent routinely carry different insulation thicknesses, different girt types, different
cladding species and different finish states.

Two ranking traps specific to this trade. First, partial bids sit at the bottom of the ranking and
look like the low bidder. A metal-only bid, a single-item screen quote, and a rate card were all filed
under the same trade token as full facade bids on their projects, and the single-item quote ranks at a
cost per square foot of under two dollars because one scope item is being divided by the whole facade
area. Always check what the denominator is dividing. Second, the general contractor's own budget is
not reliably above or below the market: on one project it was the highest number on the sheet, above
every real bid, and on two others it was the lowest.

An estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus adjustments, where adjustments are
plugs added to every bidder to cover what the market systematically will not carry.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly two dozen cladding proposals across four multifamily residential
projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor, plus the matching estimator
leveling tabs from all four projects and one mechanical screen budget. The proposals span design
development, permit, construction document and conformed sets, and include two complete same-bidder
revision ladders, one running five filed rounds over eight weeks and one running four rounds over a
month. Several bidders appear on more than one project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-
project-signal distinction in section 4 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work in one market through one general
  contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial, institutional, healthcare, industrial or public
  prevailing-wage work, and nothing is tested against high-rise curtain wall interfaces.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, which plugs were bought out, or how the girt and insulation conflicts were
  finally resolved on any project.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the deviations and access qualifications
  convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed in the
  field, so treat it as untested here.
- Only one document in the entire trade publishes unit rates, and it disclaims being a bid. Every rate
  statement in section 7 rests on that single sheet plus rates the estimator wrote into its own budget
  rows.
- Element counts are almost entirely absent. One bidder counted penetrations; nobody counted anything
  else. So there is no basis here for a count-driven scope grain, only an area-and-assembly one.
- The corpus has not shown a renovation or re-clad project. Every project is new construction, so
  nothing here covers demolition of existing cladding, matching existing profiles, or working over an
  existing substrate.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the cladding contractor also held the window package, so
  the window-install-inside-siding pattern is visible only as add alternates and as one bidder's
  separate package, never as an executed combined scope.
- Fire performance of the assembly appears as a scope-sheet row and as bidder answers, but no
  proposal in the corpus prices or details a compliant assembly, so the cost consequence of that
  requirement is unknown here.
- Attic stock, spare material and long-term maintenance obligations appear only as exclusions. Nobody
  priced them, so their value is unknown.
