# Specialties

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the specialties
package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets.
This file speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real bidders can bid it and a real estimator can level it.

One thing to hold on to before anything else. This is not one trade. It is a shelf of unrelated
catalog products that share a division number and nothing else, and the recurring question on almost
every line is not what the product is but who installs it. Get that question onto the scope sheet and
this package levels. Leave it off and the numbers are not comparable at all.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

There is no anchor section. The package is assembled from a handful of narrow sections, and a
proposal that cites sections at all cites several: toilet accessories, fire protection specialties,
postal specialties, wire mesh storage, tub and shower enclosures, wall and door protection. Bicycle
racks are commonly specified in Division 12 and bid inside this package anyway. So the CSI number is
a weak identifier here in a way it is not in most trades, and the work families are the strong one.

The work families that recur across projects, roughly in the order they show up in proposals:

- Toilet and bath accessories: towel bars, rings, robe hooks, paper holders, grab bars, shower rods,
  shower seats, medicine cabinets. Every project. The count is in the hundreds and the unit price is
  in the tens of dollars, which is the shape that drives most of what follows.
- Mirrors, framed and illuminated. Every project, and the most contested family in the division.
- Common area and amenity restroom accessories: dispensers, disposals, baby changing stations. A
  small mark set, priced separately from the unit accessories because the products are commercial
  grade rather than residential.
- Fire extinguishers and cabinets. Every project.
- Postal specialties: 4C mailbox units, parcel lockers, key cabinets, collection boxes.
- Shower doors and enclosures. Usually the single largest number in the package, sometimes larger
  than everything else combined.
- Shower and tub surrounds, and shower pans. A separate population from the doors, and a separate
  supply chain.
- Wire mesh tenant storage lockers.
- Wire and closet shelving.
- Bicycle racks, plus repair stations, pumps and ramps.
- Corner guards.
- Key cabinets and fire department lock boxes.
- Pet spa and dog wash equipment.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another
division or another section:

- Bicycle racks, specified in Division 12 and carried here by nearly every whole-package bidder.
- Shower doors and enclosures, which are glass and could as easily sit in Division 8, and which one
  project did route to a glazing contractor.
- Wire and closet shelving, which sits here on some projects and in the finish carpentry package on
  others. An estimator's own note may read that it is currently being carried with finish carpentry
  while a Division 10 bidder is quoting it, so the same shelves get bid twice.
- Pet spa and dog wash equipment, carried by most whole-package bidders as an accessory line, with the
  tub itself routed to plumbing and the drying table often owner furnished.
- Medicine cabinets, which are casework in form and accessory in supply chain, and land here.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like specialties:

- Signage. Its own bid package, its own bidder pool of sign shops, its own leveling tab. Bidders are
  numerous and none of them bid anything else in the division. Keep signage out of the specialties
  scope list entirely; it needs its own.
- Window treatments. Division 12, its own bidders, occasionally quoted by a company whose name
  contains the word specialties, which is a filing trap rather than a scope overlap.
- Trash chutes. Division 14, its own bidder pool.
- Fireplaces. Their own tab and their own bidder pool of hearth and chimney dealers, and no
  whole-package specialties bidder in this market has been seen quoting one. Worth knowing because
  the amenity equipment tail otherwise looks like it all belongs here.
- Sheet wall protection and wallcovering. This goes to the specialty wallcovering applicator, not to
  the catalog supplier. See section 5, because the boundary here is genuinely three-sided.
- Expansion joint cover assemblies and fire barrier. Quoted manufacturer-direct into this folder, and
  carried by no whole-package bidder and no leveling row. If the set has them, they need their own
  line or they will be missed by everyone.

The load-bearing packaging fact is that no two bidders draw the same boundary. On one project the
widest bid carries ten families and the narrowest carries three, and both are filed as specialties
proposals for the same scope. This is not a defect in one bidder; it is the normal condition of the
division.

## 2. How the market bids

Proposals here are itemized, not lump. The default output is a summary page listing each work family
with a price and a package mode, backed by pages that enumerate every product by manufacturer, model
number and count.

- Segmented by work family with a per-family price: the great majority, on every project. Anywhere
  from three to eight families on the summary page.
- Enumerated to the individual product with counts: nearly always, behind the summary. Counts are the
  substance of the document even when prices are per family, and they are the leveling denominator.
- Line-by-line unit pricing on every item: a substantial minority, and standard among the pure
  suppliers and manufacturers who sell catalog goods rather than a package.
- A single undifferentiated number for the whole division: essentially never.

So expect a proposal to tell you exactly what it counted and exactly which model it priced, and to be
much vaguer about what happens to the goods after they arrive.

The dominant grain is the schedule mark. Bidders key their lines to the architect's accessory mark
series and price mark by mark. The mark vocabularies are project-specific and share no prefix
convention across projects: one set uses a plain letter-number accessory series, another prefixes
everything with a two-letter bath code, a third splits unit marks from amenity marks with different
prefixes. Do not assume a fixed prefix set. Discover the vocabulary from the schedule and treat the
discovered marks as the row keys.

The other grains seen are the CSI section (one page per section, with a counted install line at the
bottom of each) and the manufacturer package (one priced line per supplier, which splits a single
work family across seven lines because seven manufacturers supply it). The manufacturer grain is a
supply-chain artifact, not a scope structure, and it does not map to a scope sheet without work.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Manufacturer and model number for every item. Nearly always, and this is the trade's strongest
  habit. It is what makes deviations detectable.
- Counts per item. Nearly always.
- Package mode per work family, written as furnished only, furnished and installed, or a
  furnish/supply/install notation. Commonly on the summary page, and this is the single most important
  field in the document.
- Freight and tax treatment, either folded in with a note or broken out as their own rows. Commonly
  broken out by the supplier-shaped bidders and folded in by the package bidders.
- The basis document, a named set with a date. Commonly, though a fair number cite nothing at all
  beyond a note that no addenda were received.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Anything about backing, blocking or fastening. Nearly always silent. The furnish bidders say
  fasteners are by others and stop there; the install bidders say the product, the backing and the
  hardware are the general contractor's. Nobody claims it.
- Who prepares the wall opening for a recessed cabinet.
- Labor type, unless the bidder is carrying install, in which case the install line itself often
  carries a labor market label.
- Attic stock, which is excluded when mentioned and simply absent otherwise.
- Mobilization count, except from the bidders who have thought about phased turnover.
- Warranty terms, unless the general contractor asked in a checklist.

Exclusion behavior is unlike a labor trade. Most proposals carry short exclusion lists or none, and
the meaningful boundary information is in the package mode column and in scattered inline notes, not
in an exclusion block. A proposal that lists no exclusions has usually said what it is doing in the
mode column instead. Read the mode column first and the exclusion list second.

Revision habits:

- The most common revision is a brand package substitution: the count and the family stay identical
  and the manufacturer changes, and the price can halve. A shower enclosure segment falling by about
  half while the door count is unchanged is a product change, not a competitive improvement, and the
  document will usually disclose the substituted product in a line the summary total hides.
- The next most common is a mode change: the same eight families at the same counts, reissued with
  every furnish-only line converted to furnished and installed. This is the most informative revision
  in the trade because it isolates the cost of labor exactly.
- A revision may be surgical: one family added, everything else untouched, with the delta stated only
  in the covering email.
- Filename revision labels are unreliable here as everywhere. A file pair labelled as two rounds may
  be the same document to within a handful of bytes, same quote number, same date, same total. Order
  rounds by the basis document each cites, and check the document's own revision field before
  computing any delta.

Two consequences for a reader. First, the covering email is frequently the most valuable page in the
PDF: the deviation disclosures, the value engineering offers, the routing opinions and the one-line
statement of what changed all tend to live there rather than in the attached estimate. Read the whole
file, not the estimate pages. Second, a quoted price is often not a held price. Validity windows of
thirty to sixty days are the norm, several documents make the number contingent on reconfirmation at
award, and one carries different validity dates on different pages of the same proposal.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Specialties scope sheets have run 23, 52, 61 and 76 rows. Target roughly fifty to eighty scope lines
for a full multifamily specialties package. Below about thirty the sheet cannot separate a whole
package bid from a single family bid, and that is the failure mode this trade actually suffers from.

The organizing principle is different from most trades. Do not organize primarily by area. Organize
by work family, and give each family a mode axis, because the same product furnished and the same
product installed are two different scopes and half the market bids only one of them.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every work family listed in section 1 that exists on the set. This is the primary axis and every
  estimator sheet uses it.
- A furnish row and an install row under each family, as separate lines. The best sheets do this
  explicitly and the best proposals answer both. Where a sheet has one row per family, a furnish-only
  bidder and a full-package bidder land in the same column and the comparison is meaningless.
- Every named accessory mark from the finish or accessory schedule, with its count. Bidders price
  mark by mark, estimators list marks as rows, and a sheet that lists the mark range is directly
  answerable by the market. This is the one place in this trade where the enumeration can safely run
  to twenty or thirty rows.
- Mirrors, split from toilet accessories, and split again between plain and illuminated. Illuminated
  mirrors carry power and change hands entirely; see section 5.
- Medicine cabinets, separate from mirrors, because the recessed ones need a wall opening somebody has
  to frame.
- Shower doors separate from shower and tub surrounds separate from shower pans. Three populations,
  three supply chains, three different counts on the same project, and different install trades.
- Fire extinguishers separate from cabinets, and rated cabinets separate from unrated. The rated
  option is a stated per-cabinet adder in the supplier rate cards and is invisible on an
  architectural schedule because the requirement lives on the life safety sheets.
- Fire extinguisher cabinet installation as its own row, because the trimless recessed types are
  routinely handed to the drywall contractor while the surface types are not.
- Mailbox units by configuration, with tenant door count, parcel locker count and outgoing slot count
  as explicit quantities rather than a unit count. See section 6; this is where the trade's worst
  count errors live.
- Wire mesh storage: locker count, tier configuration, ceilings, and locks, as four separate
  questions. Ceilings alone move the unit price substantially and are included by some bidders and
  excluded by others in the same market.
- Wire and closet shelving, with the finish carpentry boundary named on the row.
- Bicycle racks by rack type, with a bike space count rather than a rack count. Vertical, two-tier and
  horizontal systems give wildly different space counts per rack and the price follows the system.
- Corner guards, with locations. Both projects that carried a corner guard row saw every bidder answer
  that no locations were shown.
- Wall protection, with the receiving package named. See section 5.
- Pet spa and dog wash equipment, itemized, with the plumbing and electrical boundaries on the row.
- Key cabinets and fire department lock boxes.
- Expansion joint cover assemblies and fire barrier, if the set has them.
- Backing and blocking behind accessories and grab bars, as its own row, assigned. Nobody bids it and
  nobody excludes it, which is exactly why it needs a row.
- Fasteners for furnish-only lines, as its own row. Furnish bidders exclude them in a footer note.
- Mobilization count and phasing, as its own row, where the building turns over in phases.
- Attic stock, as its own row.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual rooms or units. Every count in this trade is a building total or a per-fixture-type
  total; no document seen prices a single unit.
- Finish and color. These are attributes of a line. Where a finish carries an upcharge the supplier
  states it as an upcharge on the same item, so it is a rate variable, not a row.
- Freight, crating and tax. Commercial attributes of a line or of the package, not scope, even though
  suppliers break them out. Keep them as columns rather than scope rows, with the exception noted in
  section 7 for offshore sourcing.
- Individual accessory pieces within one mark. A mark is the row; the fact that the mark covers a
  towel bar and a matching ring is the mark's business.
- Model numbers. Attributes.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be silent
about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied here, the test
fires hardest on the mode question, on every seam in section 5, and on the amenity equipment tail,
which is why those get rows even where the drawings show them as a single schedule entry.

The test has a second job in this trade that it does not have in most. Because so much of the package
is supply, silence is common and cheap. A bidder that omits a family has usually just not quoted it,
not decided against it, and its total will look attractive for exactly that reason. Any family present
on the set and absent from a proposal is a gap to price, never an inclusion to assume, and the
proposal's own itemized structure makes that detectable: if the family has no page, it has no price.

### The structural gap list

These are the items the market systematically will not carry, that an estimator adds to every number
before comparing. A scope list naming all of them is doing the plugging in advance.

- Installation, wherever the bidder is furnish-only.
- Backing and blocking behind accessories, grab bars and surrounds.
- Fasteners on furnish-only lines.
- Setting of trimless recessed fire extinguisher cabinets, and the drywall wrap the details omit.
- Illuminated mirrors, wherever they have been routed.
- Wall protection, whichever of the three products is on the set.
- Corner guards, which are specified and routinely have no plotted locations.
- Closet and wire shelving, wherever the finish carpentry boundary falls.
- Free standing package room shelving and delivery vestibule lockers, which appear on amenity
  schedules and are carried by nobody.
- Pet spa equipment connection.
- Shower pan setting.
- Postal parcel ratio correction, and mailbox door numbering.
- Attic stock.
- Expansion joint cover assemblies, where present.
- Extra mobilizations under phased turnover.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

The recurring exclusions are short and the recurring qualifications are long, which is the reverse of
a labor trade. Take the qualifications more seriously.

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

| Excluded | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Installation of everything, on a furnish-only bid (about half the market on any given project) | Whichever package the general contractor assigns; frequently unassigned at bid time |
| Fasteners on furnish-only accessory lines (stated as standing house terms by one repeat bidder, silent elsewhere) | Frequently unassigned, and a real gap |
| Backing and blocking behind accessories, grab bars and surrounds (named by the install-only bidders) | Framing or carpentry, and unclaimed in every sheet seen |
| Installation of trimless recessed fire extinguisher cabinets (commonly, and stated as a positive routing rather than a bare exclusion) | Drywall contractor |
| Shower pan setting (commonly, from the surround suppliers) | Plumber, explicitly a certified one |
| Locks on tenant storage lockers; padlock lugs only (commonly) | Owner or end user |
| Mailbox interior labels and door numbering (commonly) | Owner, coordinated with and approved by the postal authority |
| Pet spa tub, and connection of pet spa equipment (commonly) | Plumbing and electrical |
| Fire blanket cabinets (occasionally, and carried by others on the same project) | Frequently unassigned |
| Attic stock (occasionally) | Frequently unassigned |
| Sales tax and freight (routine among suppliers, folded in by package bidders) | General contractor |
| Bonding (occasionally) | General contractor |
| Takeoff itself, stated outright by at least one supplier | General contractor |
| Prevailing wage, off hours and weekend work (from the applicator-shaped bidders) | General contractor |

Boilerplate versus project signal. The footer conditions that a repeat bidder reproduces on every
project (quotes valid thirty days, fasteners by others, freight allowed, house payment terms) carry no
project information. Read them once for the trade map. What is project signal is a bidder writing out
a specific mark and saying something about it, a bidder naming a sheet or a detail, and above all a
bidder disclosing a product departure. Those are written for this set.

### Deviations, which matter more here than exclusions

This is a supply trade, so the highest-value paragraph in a proposal is usually the one admitting that
what is being priced is not quite what is drawn. These are not exclusions and they are not
boilerplate, and they will not show up in any total. Seen repeatedly:

- Glass thickness on shower enclosures departing from the drawn or specified thickness, sometimes with
  a technical justification (a thicker door is needed for the sweeps to fit), sometimes with none, and
  sometimes with the panels and the door at different thicknesses in the same offer. Competing bids on
  one project quoted three different glass thicknesses for the same enclosure.
- Hardware finish departing from the schedule, a chrome package quoted against a matte black schedule.
- A specified product that is discontinued or whose vendor has gone out of business, with a substitute
  or an allowance carried in its place.
- A mark whose manufacturer and model the schedule never states, priced by borrowing the adjacent
  mark's product, with the substitution written out.
- A mirror size priced at different dimensions than the schedule shows.
- A mailbox configuration that matches the drawn elevation and fails the postal parcel-to-tenant ratio,
  disclosed as a defect in the documents rather than absorbed.

Read the deviation list before the exclusion list. A proposal with a clean exclusion list and three
disclosed deviations is a more honest document than one with twenty exclusions and none.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

- Package-award conditions. One bidder states that its pricing is based on all bid sections being
  awarded, that install prices are contingent on acceptance of the entire package, that all materials
  will be delivered at once, and that all areas will be accessible for install at the same time. Every
  one of those is violated by a phased residential turnover and by any mixed award, which is exactly
  what a ranked leveling sheet invites. Treat an all-or-nothing bid as unrankable line by line.
- Single shipment and single mobilization. Common. The bidder who instead prices seven deliveries and
  seven mobilizations for a phased building is not more expensive; it is the only one who read the
  phasing.
- Elevator access for vertical distribution. Distributing several hundred small boxes up a residential
  tower is priced as a condition, not as a line.
- Long lead times, commonly fourteen to sixteen weeks from purchase order and deposit for catalog
  goods, with a stated in-stock flag per item where the supplier keeps one. An earlier delivery date
  can void the quote outright.
- Deposits. Fifty percent to release production and the balance to release shipping is a common term,
  and one supplier charges a cancellation fee in proportion to work performed including submittals and
  quote revisions.
- Approved shop drawings required before orders release, and for mailboxes, written postal approval
  before order release. Both are schedule constraints hidden in payment terms.
- Tariff exposure. Prices stated as including currently active tariffs but subject to change with
  tariff and import policy movement. A general contractor's own bid checklist may ask every bidder to
  confirm tariff inclusion, which is a good sign that this is live.
- Escalation on deferred shipment.
- Standard colors assumed. Custom finishes are an upcharge and custom anything is non-returnable.
- Reconfirmation at award. Several proposals condition acceptance on reconfirming the price when the
  award happens, which means the number is an indication rather than a bid.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This section is the substance of the trade. Everything in the division is a small object attached to
something built by somebody else, so the boundary questions are the scope.

### The mode question, which comes before every other question

Every work family in this division arrives in one of four commercial shapes, and the drawings will not
tell you which one applies:

1. Furnished and installed by one specialty bidder. Common.
2. Furnished only, install unassigned at bid time. Roughly as common, and the default posture of the
   supplier-shaped half of the market.
3. Furnished by one party and installed by a separate labor-only installer, who bids per piece.
4. Furnished and installed with the install offered as a separate priced conversion, so the same
   bidder gives you both numbers.

A single bidder frequently mixes shapes within one proposal: furnished and installed on mailboxes and
bike racks, furnished only on everything else. The same company bids furnish-only on one project and
furnished-and-installed on the next, so mode is a per-project commercial decision, not a company
trait. Never infer mode from the bidder.

What install is worth, measured on a single bidder holding scope byte-identical across two rounds and
moving only the mode: for the package as a whole, roughly a fifth on top of the furnish-only number.
Family by family it varies enormously. On scattered small toilet accessories, install ran well over
one and a half times the material, because the cost is hundreds of separate trips to hundreds of
separate walls. On fire extinguisher cabinets it ran about a third of the material, and on a single
key cabinet about a half. Another bidder's published install deduct on a different project put
accessory install at about a third of the installed price, consistent in direction.

The practical rule: on accessories, labor is the majority of the delivered cost, not a markup on it. A
furnish-only accessory number compared against a furnished-and-installed accessory number is off by
more than a factor of two, and no arithmetic on the totals will reveal it.

### Mirrors, the most contested item in the division

Illuminated vanity mirrors on a residential project can be several hundred units at a few hundred
dollars each, which makes them one of the largest single line items in the package. They have been
seen going four different ways on four projects:

- Carried by the specialties bidder inside its toilet accessory line.
- Carried by a catalog supplier as its own largest line.
- Routed by the estimator to the glazing package, with every specialties bidder answering that it is
  in Division 8.
- Routed to the electrician, on the reasoning that an illuminated mirror is a powered device, stated
  by a bidder in its own covering email while that same bidder was still pricing the mirrors.

All four have occurred, and on one project three of them occurred simultaneously with three bidders
pricing the same six hundred and ninety mirrors that the estimator's sheet had already assigned
elsewhere. Beware of the same item being claimed by two trades. Make illuminated mirrors their own
scope row, name the receiving package on the row, and expect to have to ask.

### Wall protection, which is genuinely three-sided

Three different products travel under the phrase wall protection and each goes to a different package:

- Rigid corner guards. The Division 10 catalog supplier carries them, furnished and installed, and
  the numbers are small. This is the one that stays in this package.
- Sheet wall protection and wall covering. The specialty wall covering applicator carries these, on
  its own shop drawing, with the same conditions it attaches to wallcovering. Not the catalog
  supplier.
- Impact-rated panel systems such as fibre-reinforced panels at back of house rooms. An estimator has
  been seen carrying these in the paint package and every specialties bidder answering that they are
  carried in paint, while on another project the same product sat on the specialties sheet as an
  unbid plug. This one genuinely moves.

Name the specific product on the scope row rather than writing wall protection, and name the receiving
package. Three rows, not one.

### The wall covering applicator's conditions, which reach into this package

Where a specialty applicator carries the sheet goods, it imposes conditions that bind the specialties
supplier directly: one wall covering mark must be installed before millwork, mailboxes and similar,
with a stated substantial cost increase otherwise. That is a sequencing dependency running from a
different package into this one, and it belongs on the mailbox scope row.

### Other seams

- Fire extinguisher cabinets and the drywall contractor. Trimless recessed cabinets are handed to
  drywall by name, repeatedly, on more than one project. Surface mounted and standard semi-recessed
  cabinets are not. So the cabinet type determines the install trade, and a schedule that does not
  distinguish them leaves the install unassigned. A related documents gap recurs: the details show a
  rated cabinet with no drywall wrap, which is a question for the drywall package that only surfaces
  if the row exists.
- Backing and blocking. The labor-only installers state plainly that the general contractor provides
  the product, the backing and all the hardware. No furnish bidder claims backing, no carpenter claims
  it, and no leveling sheet has a row for it. Grab bars and shower surrounds cannot be
  set without it. This is an unclaimed gap and it should be a row with an owner.
- Fasteners. The furnish-only bidders exclude them in a standing footer. Nobody picks them up.
- Wire and closet shelving against finish carpentry. An estimator's note may read that shelving is
  currently carried with finish carpentry while two Division 10 bidders are quoting it. Duplicated
  scope inflates both packages until somebody rules.
- Shower pans and the plumber. The surround suppliers exclude pan setting and route it to a certified
  plumber by name.
- Pet spa equipment across three trades. The specialties bidder supplies the tub, dryer and steps; the
  tub is commonly listed with the plumbing items to be carried by others; the drying table is often
  owner furnished; and setting the equipment requires help from both a plumber and an electrician. All
  three parties have been seen excluding the other two's part while the equipment sits on the
  specialties sheet.
- Recessed medicine cabinets and the framer. A recessed cabinet needs an opening. No document seen
  says who makes it.
- Mailboxes and the postal authority. Written postal approval is required before orders release,
  interior labels are the owner's responsibility, and door numbering waits on a signed and approved
  numbering drawing. Three external dependencies on one family, none of them visible on the drawings.
- Shower enclosures and the tile or surround package. Doors and surrounds are separate populations with
  separate counts on the same project, and a single shower line will always be ambiguous about which
  one it means.

### Owner-furnished programs

Owner-furnished items appear as isolated notes rather than as a program: a pet drying table noted as
owner furnished, locks on tenant lockers left to the end user. Where an item is owner furnished, the
receive-store-and-install rows that exist in no other configuration have to be added, and a
specialties bidder pricing them has not been seen.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What specialties bidders actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The accessory or bath accessory schedule, keyed by mark. This is the primary document and it is
   what makes this trade quantitative in a way that most finishing trades are not. Marks come from
   here and the bidders reproduce them.
2. Unit and fixture counts, which are the leveling currency. Every estimator sheet normalizes every
   bid to a cost per unit and ranks on it.
3. Enlarged plans and elevations for the mail room, package room, bike room and storage rooms, which
   is where the configuration quantities live.
4. Interior design sheets for the amenity and common area accessory marks, which are commonly on a
   different sheet series from the unit accessories.
5. Life safety sheets for fire extinguisher and cabinet locations, which is a different discipline
   from the one the rest of the package reads. One bidder qualified its extinguisher count as being
   taken off the life safety drawings; another priced only where shown and disclosed that fire
   protection was not shown at all in the basement or on the first floor.
6. The specification, mostly for glass thickness, low iron requirements, and rated cabinet
   requirements. Cited less often than the schedules.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff of the whole building. They count marks off schedules
and count units off enlarged plans, and the manufacturers who quote direct sometimes perform and
document their own takeoff.

Counting is where this trade goes wrong, and it goes wrong constantly. On a single project, five
bidders quoted five different mailbox configurations for the same mail room, with tenant door totals
ranging from under two hundred to well over three hundred against a residence count of roughly three
hundred and fifty. One of them provided barely half the doors the building needs and its total gives no
hint of it. On the same project, carbon dioxide extinguisher counts ranged from eight to fifty-four
across bidders. Shower door counts ranged from three hundred and thirty to three hundred and
fifty-five. Wire mesh locker counts differed by a factor of two. None of these divergences is visible
in a total, and all of them are visible in the itemized pages that every one of these proposals
contains. Compare the counts, always, before comparing anything else.

The unit denominator is contested too. A leveling sheet stating a unit count has been seen disagreeing
with a bidder's own stated residence count on the same project, and with every accessory count on the
same page. Pin the denominator explicitly or the cost-per-unit ranking measures nothing.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Package mode per family. The largest single driver in this trade, by a wide margin.
- Whether backing and blocking are being provided, and by whom.
- Which cabinet types are being installed by drywall.
- Whether the project is phased, and how many mobilizations and deliveries that implies.
- Whether elevator access is available for vertical distribution.
- Lead times and whether the specified items are stocked or made to order.
- Deposit and payment terms, and whether the schedule can absorb a production release that waits on
  approved shop drawings.
- Tariff exposure on imported goods, which several bidders now name explicitly.
- Offshore versus domestic sourcing, which moves cost between material and freight rather than
  removing it.
- Whether items are owner furnished.
- Whether the mailbox configuration satisfies the postal parcel ratio, which is a code question the
  drawn elevations can fail.
- Which spec sections the general contractor intends to keep in this package versus finish carpentry,
  glazing, painting and plumbing.
- Warranty term, insurance basis and bond, which appear only when a general contractor's checklist
  asks for them.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, colors are TBD, sizes are approximate, and bidders quote
against an incomplete mark set without much comment. By construction documents and conformed sets, the
mark ranges are quoted in full and the deviations start being disclosed, because the bidders are now
comparing a specific product against a specific requirement. Quantities move materially between sets:
a bike rack count changing, a wire mesh partition scope disappearing entirely between the permit and
conformed rounds, a mirror frame dimension changing to match the casework. An estimator's scope sheet
records these movements in its notes, which makes the sheet a set-delta log as well as a leveling tool.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, for essentially everything. Accessories, mirrors, cabinets, extinguishers, mailbox units,
  lockers, racks, doors, pans, corner guards. This is an each trade end to end.
- Linear feet, for wire mesh partition runs and for sheet wall protection.
- Shelves, for closet and wire shelving, counted individually.
- Bike spaces rather than racks, because a two-tier or vertical system delivers several spaces per
  rack and the space count is the requirement.
- Tenant doors, parcel doors and outgoing slots, for mailboxes. Counting mailbox units instead of
  doors is the error that produces the largest silent shortfalls in the trade.
- Ten-foot lengths, for expansion joint cover assemblies.
- Rooms, for storage areas and for back of house wall protection plugs.
- Linear yards, where wall covering enters through the applicator.
- Units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator sheet.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause. Note that the top three are commercial questions
rather than product questions, which is characteristic of this division:

1. Package mode. Whether install is in or out is worth roughly a fifth of the package overall and far
   more than that on the accessory families.
2. Scope breadth. On one project the spread between the narrowest and widest bid in the same ranked
   list was more than twenty to one, and it measured how many families each carried, not price.
3. Product package selection, above all on shower enclosures. A brand substitution at an identical
   count halved a segment on one project, and a switch from a custom to a stocked door was offered as
   the largest single value engineering lever on two projects by the same bidder years apart. The
   condition on that lever is always the same: the opening has to become consistent and fixed, because
   stocked glass does not come in custom sizes. That is an architectural decision with a large price
   attached.
4. Shower doors and enclosures as a share of the package. On the larger projects this one family can
   exceed everything else combined, so an error here dominates.
5. Bike rack system type. Vertical, two-tier and horizontal systems priced against the same space
   requirement have differed by well over a third between bidders quoting different manufacturers.
6. Sourcing origin. Offshore shower door packages have shown freight running around three quarters of
   the material cost, so the landed cost gap is much smaller than the material gap, and lead time and
   replacement speed go the wrong way. A supplier arguing against imports on lead time, breakage and
   replacement turnaround is making a real argument, not a sales one.
7. Accessory count and the number of distinct marks, which drives both material and the labor that
   dominates it.
8. Ceilings, tiers and locks on wire mesh storage, which have moved the per-locker rate by more than
   half between projects.
9. Finish selection, where matte and specialty finishes carry a stated per-item upcharge of around a
   tenth of the item.
10. Rated versus unrated fire extinguisher cabinets, priced as an explicit per-cabinet adder of about
    half the base cabinet price.
11. Mobilization count under phased turnover.
12. Tariffs, deposits, escalation and validity windows.

On dispersion. Within a single project the ranked cost per unit can span more than twenty to one, but
almost none of that is price competition; it is the ranking placing single-family suppliers, rate
cards with no quantities, labor-only installers and whole-package bids in one column. Once each bid is
adjusted to a common scope the comparable bidders on one project landed within about twenty percent of
each other while their raw base bids spanned more than three to one. Bidder counts per project run from
about two on a small project to over twenty rows on a large one, but the row count overstates the
bidder count substantially: one company has appeared as three separate ranking rows, and a single
supply house as two rows for two product configurations. A row in this trade is a product offer, not a
company.

The plug economics of this trade are extreme and worth stating plainly. An estimator does not compare
base bids; it compares base bid plus adjustments. On one project the adjustments applied to every
bidder ran larger than every base bid on the sheet, so the leveling was plug-dominated and the base
bids were nearly decorative. Adjustments here are per-bidder rather than uniform, because what each
bidder is missing differs, and at least one adjustment has been negative, applying a bidder's own
published install deduct in reverse. On the smallest project seen the plugs were most of the in-house
budget for the division, because with almost no bidders the budget is a plug list by construction. On
the largest they exceeded every base bid.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly two dozen Division 10 proposals plus their bundled email threads,
four estimator leveling sheets and their sibling signage and fireplace tabs, drawn from four
multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The
proposals span design development, permit, construction documents and conformed sets. Several bidders
appear on three or four of the projects, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal
distinction in section 4 testable, and what allows the same company's furnish-only and
furnished-and-installed rounds to be compared directly.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back of house space, in one
  market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors,
  institutional, healthcare or public prevailing-wage work, where the accessory mix, the wall
  protection systems and the toilet partition scope are all substantially different.
- Toilet partitions do not appear at all. Every project in the corpus is residential with no
  multi-stall public restrooms to speak of, so one of the division's signature products is entirely
  untested here, along with its own furnish-and-install seam.
- Lockers other than wire mesh tenant storage are absent. No athletic, employee or staff lockers.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which package mode
  was actually bought, how the unassigned installs were finally resolved, or which of the four mirror
  routings won.
- No change order history. The claim that the package-award conditions and the single-mobilization
  assumptions convert into extras is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed.
- The install premium figures rest on a small number of clean comparisons: one bidder's two rounds on
  one project, and two published install deducts on another. The direction is corroborated across
  projects; the magnitudes are not.
- Signage was read only at the boundary, enough to confirm it is a separate package with a separate
  bidder pool. It needs its own file and this one should not be used to scope it.
- Wall protection is characterized from a small number of documents and one estimator ruling per
  product type. The three-way split in section 5 is consistent across what exists but rests on
  single sightings for the impact-panel case.
- Expansion joint cover assemblies appear once, quoted manufacturer-direct, with no leveling row and no
  competing bid, so nothing here describes how that scope is normally bought.
- The corpus does not show how this package is bid where the general contractor self-performs the
  installation of owner-supplied specialties, which the recurring furnish-only posture suggests must
  happen somewhere.
