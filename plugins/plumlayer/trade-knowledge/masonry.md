# Masonry

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the masonry package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real masons can bid it and a real estimator can level
it.

One thing governs everything below, so it goes first. Division 4 is not one trade. It is two trades
that share a division number and share almost nothing else: new masonry, which is a lump-sum trade
organized around wall assemblies, and restoration masonry, which is a unit-price trade organized
around actions taken on an existing wall. The bidder pools barely overlap, the proposal shapes are
opposites, the units are different, and access sits on the other side of the fence in each. Decide
which one you are scoping before you write a single line.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The anchor sections are 043000 for reinforced unit masonry, 040120 or 040210 for masonry restoration
and cleaning, and 042010 for thin brick veneer where it is separately specified. Restoration bidders
name their sections readily and often price section by section. New-work bidders usually name
Division 4 as a whole and stop there, and one of the more careful ones states that specification
section 04 is the only section it reviewed.

### The new masonry package

The package as bid is concrete masonry backup plus exterior veneer plus everything embedded in them.
Nearly every bidder carries all of the following inside one number without being asked: reinforced
block at shafts, stairs, firewalls and back of house; rebar, grout, joint reinforcement and shop
drawings for the rebar; brick veneer of every mark; cast stone or precast bands, sills, caps and
copings; cavity insulation; brick ties, weeps and cavity drainage; wash down at completion; and all
hoisting and access for its own work.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another section:

- Cast stone and architectural precast. Carried by every mason seen on new work, including the
  anchors and dowel pins. A specialty cast stone supplier may bid the same marks directly, but it
  bids material only, so it is a purchase the mason makes rather than a competitor to the mason.
- Stone and granite veneer. Rides masonry by default. It carries the highest rate per square foot of
  anything in the package and the smallest area, which makes it the favorite target for value
  engineering.
- Thin brick on a proprietary clip-in rail. Usually carried, occasionally excluded outright, and it
  is a different operation from laying brick even though the drawings show it as a veneer.
- Cavity insulation. Carried by most, in whatever the assembly schedule specifies, and excluded by a
  minority who treat all insulation as someone else's.
- Through-wall and base-of-wall flashing, transition membrane, drip edge, termination bar and cavity
  drainage. Usually carried, but the flashing family splits in the middle, which section 5 covers.
- Mock-up panels. Sometimes in the base, more often an add alternate priced per panel, and
  occasionally a priced general-requirement line.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like masonry:

- Joint sealants and caulking. Excluded by essentially every mason on new work. This is the single
  most reliable boundary in the trade.
- Air and vapour barrier, weather-resistive barrier, dampproofing and waterproofing. Excluded on new
  work by nearly everyone, sometimes along with the sheathing behind it.
- Interior masonry finishes: fireplace and hearth stone, feature brick in amenity spaces, interior
  brick marks on the interior design drawings. Excluded by name, and an estimator may route those
  marks to the tile package instead.
- Site masonry: granite curb, hardscape, pavers, retaining walls, site signage masonry. Excluded by
  most masons and usually bought in landscaping or sitework, though the owner's bid form may still
  demand a site masonry breakout from the mason.
- Structural clips, angles, beams and top-of-wall lateral restraint. Excluded on both the furnish
  and the install side by some bidders and install-only by others, which is section 5's first
  subject.
- Delegated design and engineering for masonry assemblies, precast anchorage and lifting. Excluded by
  the mason and excluded by the cast stone supplier, so it lands nowhere unless someone assigns it.
- Firestopping, fire safing, smoke seals. Excluded by nearly all.

### The restoration package

Restoration masonry is bid as a set of operations on an existing wall, and the package reaches much
further sideways than new masonry does. Bidders in this space describe themselves as doing masonry,
waterproofing, restoration, preservation and concrete repair together, and they price all of it.

Riding with restoration, commonly: crystalline waterproofing at vaults, pits and areaways; air
vapour barrier at new construction on the same building; window perimeter sealants and backer rod;
concrete spall repair at columns, beams and slabs; facade cleaning and washing; protection of things
that must survive the work. One bidder reached as far as plumbing fixture caulking and
countertop-to-wall joints, which is a different package wearing this one's clothes.

Leaving restoration, or contested: metal flashings and copper coping, excluded by most while the
brick around them is carried; steel remediation behind the brick, where the mason opens the wall and
closes it again but will not touch the steel; chimney demolition, carried by some and excluded by
others; interior historic stone, marble and tile restoration, which one specialist bidder prices and
everyone else declines. Interior stone restoration is the clearest unbid gap in the trade.

### Access

On new work, access belongs to the mason. Every new-work bidder seen includes its own scaffolding,
lifts, hoisting and rigging, usually in a single line and always without a price.

On restoration, access is contested, and it is the largest single variable in the package. Bidders
place it three ways: inside the base bid, excluded to others, or excluded from the base and offered
as a priced add. When it is priced, it is very large, in one case roughly two thirds of the base bid
and in another roughly a quarter. When the mason excludes it, the general contractor buys it as its
own package from a scaffolding or swing-staging supplier, at which point it stops being a lump sum
and becomes a monthly rental with fixed erect, dismantle and trucking charges. That change of shape
matters more than the price: from that moment on, every week of schedule slip has a masonry cost.

## 2. How the market bids

### New masonry

New masonry is a lump-sum trade. One number is the norm and the base bid is almost never broken out
voluntarily. What varies enormously is how the bidder enumerates the scope behind that one number,
and the styles seen are genuinely different from each other:

- A flat list of materials and operations, one bullet each, no locations and no quantities.
- Room by room and elevation by elevation, naming every shaft, every stair, every named room and
  every elevation segment, with the identical sentence repeated for each. Highly checkable against a
  floor plan, and completely silent on magnitude.
- By exterior wall assembly code, quoting the architect's own assembly designations back, with the
  manufacturer, colour, texture and format named for each brick and cast stone mark, and the actual
  reinforcing spacing named for each block wall. This is the most useful shape and the least common.

Whichever style a bidder uses, it will not give you a quantity unless the general contractor demands
one on a scope sheet. Even then the answers are unstable: a bidder asked the same quantity question
in two rounds may answer with a total area in one and a set of mark areas plus piece counts in the
other, and the two answers may differ by a factor of three.

Commonly stated on new work: the brick material allowance, expressed per individual brick, because
the brick is usually unselected at bid time and a bidder may state two or three tiers plus a higher
tier for shaped sill brick; the mortar allowance per bag and per bulk bag; labour type; bond as a
separate add; a mock-up panel as a separate add; reinforcing spacing; and the wash-down method.

Rarely stated on new work: any quantity at all; which drawing disciplines and specification sections
were actually reviewed; shop drawing and delivery lead times; crew size or labour hours; time and
material rates. Every one of those becomes available if the general contractor asks for it on a
scope sheet, and essentially none of it appears if nobody asks.

### Restoration

Restoration is the mirror image. Unit-price schedules dominate: a row per action, with a quantity, a
unit and a rate. Bidders arrive with this shape already formed, months before any general contractor
issues a quantity file, and the row set they arrive with is remarkably consistent across companies.

Two other shapes appear. One is the specification-section proposal, which quotes the specification's
own numbered subparagraphs back verbatim and attaches a qualified quantity to each, with one price
per section. The other, and the most instructive document in the trade, is the menu quote: one
document carrying two or three complete configurations of the same scope, differing only in which
action class is applied to a given elevation. Where a menu quote exists, there is no single number
that is the bid, and a captured price without its configuration name is worthless.

Restoration proposals routinely state quantities. They also routinely qualify them: an allowance for
a stated area, a quantity the price is limited to, a minimum billable size per repair. Those
qualifications convert what looks like a lump sum into a unit-price contract with a ceiling.

### Exclusion behavior, both work types

Exclusion behavior is bimodal and this split matters more than anything else about proposal shape. A
majority carry an explicit exclusion list, and the longest run past twenty-five numbered items with
a six-part sub-definition of what winter conditions means. A minority carry no exclusions and no
qualifications at all, sometimes across five pages of scope. A bidder with no exclusion list is not
offering broader scope; it is leaving every boundary open, and an estimator has to plug or ask on
every one. Those bidders often rank low and still need the largest adjustments before they are
comparable.

### Revision habits

- The negotiation ladder is the dominant pattern on new work. Four and five rounds over several
  months, the scope letter byte-identical or nearly so, and the number declining. One ladder fell by
  roughly a quarter across its rounds. A reason is essentially never documented.
- Scope can move inside a round presented as a price revision. In one ladder several named rooms
  quietly left the interior list and a generic firewall line took their place, while the covering
  document said nothing. Always diff the scope text, not just the total.
- The revision is frequently not a document. It may be an email sentence, a written confirmation of
  no change, or a scope statement that exists only in the transmittal. One bidder disclosed dropping
  interior thin brick that it had carried the previous round in the body of the covering email and
  nowhere in the attached proposal.
- A revision may move no money at all and exist purely to answer one scope question, surfacing an
  item as an add alternate that was silently absent from the base.
- A breakout restructure can halve a base bid without a single rate being cut, by moving cleaning,
  access and surveying out of the base into a second table. Comparing those two totals is
  meaningless.
- Restoration ladders tend to negotiate access duration rather than rates. One bidder never changed
  a unit rate once its schedule was set, and every subsequent movement came out of staging months
  and survey days.

Two rules follow. Order rounds by the basis document each one cites, never by the letterhead date: a
revised proposal may still cite the superseded set, letterhead years can be wrong by a year, and on
one project the basis was established only because the general contractor emailed to ask which set
the price was based on. And treat any bidder-supplied breakout as a claim to verify. One bidder's
own four-line breakout did not reconcile, the bidder did not notice until challenged, and then could
not correctly re-split two of the four lines.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Masonry scope sheets have run 33, 48, 68 and 77 rows in the leveling view, and the version issued to
bidders on one project ran to about 171 rows because it carried commercial, compliance, schedule,
site-logistics and safety sections the leveling view summarises. Target roughly fifty to eighty scope
lines for a new masonry package on a multifamily building, and sixty to eighty for a facade
restoration, plus the general-conditions and commercial rows.

### The governing rule on restoration: the action is the unit

On an existing wall, the priced unit is not the surface. It is what you do to the surface. The same
elevation, the same area, the same bidder, priced twice in one document: repointing it and replacing
it differ by a ratio of roughly one and three quarters to one, with every other line held constant.
A third round split the elevation by floor band, repointing the lower floors and replacing the upper
ones, and landed between the two. The action class is the pricing axis, and a scope line that names
a wall without naming the action to be taken on it cannot be bid.

Give each of these its own line, because a reasonable bidder could carry any one and exclude the
next:

- Repointing, distinguished by joint type. Face joints, transverse or lead-covered joints, and stone
  mortar joints are separately priced everywhere they appear together, and transverse joint work
  carries a much higher rate per unit than field repointing.
- Remove and replace brick, distinguished by cause. Cracked or broken brick, brick removed to
  remediate corroded steel behind it, and brick at new through-wall flashing are three separately
  priced lines even though the physical operation is similar.
- Rebuild, as distinct from replacement. Rebuilding a displaced or bulging area is a different action
  from swapping units, and it is the line most often excluded on the grounds that the extent is
  unknown.
- Stone replacement, split by depth. Full stone replacement and half stone replacement are separate
  priced lines, and a Dutchman repair, letting a patch into the face of an existing stone, is a third
  action priced by the each.
- Crack repair, split by substrate. On a stone facade a crack may be priced as rout and seal with
  sealant; on a brick facade the same crack may be priced as remove and replace. One bidder states
  this substitution explicitly, and the two treatments are not the same cost.
- Matrix mortar repair, split into spalling and erosion as separate lines.
- Cleaning, split by method and by target. Hot water restoration wash, high pressure wash, carbon
  staining removal on limestone, and test cleaning patches to determine the method are separate
  lines, and the test patches come first for a reason.
- Coatings on existing masonry: elastomeric coating, parge coating, water repellent.
- Sealant replacement and lead strip replacement at joints.
- Protection of things that must survive: existing murals, adjacent landscape, existing marble.
- Chimney and appurtenance demolition with new cap.

Depth and extent qualifiers belong on the line, not in a note. Whether brick replacement is exterior
wythe only or two wythes, whether the backup wall gets any treatment, whether new brick ties into
the backup with helical ties or conventional ties, and whether concrete repair is partial depth or
full depth are each worth a doubling somewhere. Two bidders on one wall assumed one wythe and two
wythes respectively, and neither total announced it.

Minimum billable repair size is a real quantity convention and belongs on the line. Brick repairs
carry a minimum of four square feet per repair and stone spalling and erosion repairs a minimum of
one square foot per repair. A scattering of small defects therefore costs far more than its measured
area, and a scope line stating an area without the minimum understates the work.

### The governing rule on new work: the assembly is the unit

On new construction the discriminating fact is the wall assembly, not the material. The estimator
sheets that work best enumerate the architect's exterior wall assembly codes directly, one row each,
and annotate which ones could not be located on the plans. Give each of these its own line:

- Every exterior wall assembly code that contains masonry, named by its code, with the barrier and
  insulation it carries. Assemblies that are not found on the plans get a row anyway, because their
  absence is the finding.
- Every CMU partition type code, separately from the exterior assemblies.
- Every brick mark, by designation, with its manufacturer, format and colour. Four marks on one
  building is normal.
- Every cast stone or precast mark, by designation, with its profile and whether it carries a wash.
- Thin brick separately from full-bed brick, always, and separately again by assembly code where
  more than one thin brick assembly exists. Thin brick is a rail-mounted kit with its own rail,
  screws, proprietary mortar, building wrap and tape, and it can cost as much per square foot as
  full brick despite using a fraction of the material.
- Stone and granite veneer separately from brick.
- CMU split by the breakouts the owner's bid form demands, which recur across projects: block in the
  residential building, block in the parking garage, exterior brick and stone on the residential
  building, site masonry, and site signage masonry. If those five categories are on the bid form,
  they must be rows on the scope sheet.
- Special shapes, split between thin brick and full-width brick. Both get their own row on a good
  sheet. A shaped brick costs several times a field brick, bidders regularly assert that none are
  shown, and a supplier price list will list them anyway.
- Row lock sills, soldier courses, stack bond and recessed panels, where the elevations show them.
- Accessories as their own block: rebar and joint reinforcement, embeds, elevator shaft items,
  cavity insulation, air vapour barrier, sealing of brick ties, cavity and flexible flashing, metal
  flashing, weeps and drainage.
- Access as its own block, with the method named, and split between facade access and shaft access.
  Whether the shafts are built from inside or outside is a real question with a real cost and an
  estimator asks it by name.
- Mock-up panels, with whether the panel remains as part of the work or is removed by others.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual block courses, bond beams and lintel courses within one wall collapse to the wall.
  Reinforcing spacing, grout strength, mortar type and joint reinforcement are attributes of a line,
  not lines.
- Room by room enumeration collapses to the room group where the assembly is the same, unless the
  general contractor's bid form demands the room-level answer. Bidders who enumerate every room by
  name are describing coverage, not quantity, and the sheet does not need to mirror them.
- Elevation by elevation enumeration collapses to the elevation group where the assembly does not
  change. A bidder may list thirty elevation segments carrying the identical sentence; those are one
  line unless the mark or the assembly changes between them.
- Individual brick and block counts collapse into the mark or assembly line, with the count carried
  as a quantity rather than as rows.
- Cleaning of new work collapses into the veneer line. Cleaning of existing work does not.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
masonry the test fires hardest on three things: the action class on any existing wall, everything in
the furnish-and-install seams of section 5, and access.

Silence is a finding. If an item is one that some bidders carry and others exclude, and a proposal
says nothing about it, that silence is a scope gap to surface, not an inclusion to assume. This
applies with unusual force here because so many masonry proposals carry no exclusion list at all: on
those, every boundary is silence.

Watch for a document that contradicts itself. One proposal included all cells grouted solid at
vertically reinforced cells and bond beams while also excluding grouting solid of CMU. Another
included insulation behind the brick in its scope and excluded insulation in its qualifications.
Both are readable, but only if you keep the qualifier attached to the inclusion.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs, meaning scope no bidder reliably carries that has to be added to every number
before comparing. An estimator adds these as to-value plugs applied identically across all bidders,
which is exactly what a scope line set that names them in advance would prevent.

1. Sill flashing, and metal flashing generally, where the mason carries through-wall flashing but
   not counterflashing, sill pans or copings.
2. Miscellaneous building tie-ins and patching around hoists and staging.
3. Cavity insulation, where the assembly specifies it and a bidder treats insulation as someone
   else's.
4. Relieving and shelf angle installation. Furnished by the metals package and excluded on the
   furnish side by everyone, its installation is carried by some masons, excluded by others, and
   surfaced by a third as an add alternate.
5. Air and vapour barrier behind the veneer, which almost no mason carries and which has to be
   bought somewhere.
6. Joint sealants and caulking at masonry, excluded on new work by essentially everyone.
7. Interior historic stone, marble and tile restoration on a renovation, which one specialist may
   price and the rest ignore.
8. Exterior access on restoration, which swings the number more than any other single item.
9. Winter protection, heat and enclosure, excluded by every bidder seen on both work types.
10. Special shapes, where bidders assert none are shown and the supplier list prices them anyway.
11. Engineering and delegated design for masonry assemblies and precast anchorage, excluded by the
    mason and by the cast stone supplier alike.
12. Concrete panel and non-masonry cladding at garages that sits inside the masonry scope sheet
    because nobody else owns it.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate. Repeat bidders reproduce the same exclusion list on every
project, and the same firm's proposals on two different projects can be word-for-word identical
apart from the project name. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project. Note also that
proposal language travels between unrelated competitors, so a phrase appearing in two bidders'
documents is not corroboration.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other work:

- An exclusion that names a condition the bidder could not locate. Phrases like unable to locate
  conditions, extent of work unknown at this time, none found, or not shown on plans are not
  exclusions at all. They are unanswered questions about the documents, filed in the wrong place.
  When two independent bidders name the same missing thing, that is a strong signal for a request
  for information rather than a leveling adjustment.
- A stated departure from the drawings. One bidder carried a four inch block shelf while noting
  explicitly that the structural drawings show eight inch. Disclosed departures are rare and
  valuable, and they are neither exclusions nor errors.
- An exclusion or inclusion keyed to a specific sheet, detail or mark. When a bidder writes out mark
  numbers or a detail reference, it read the documents and found something.
- A quantity ceiling. Any clause excluding footage that exceeds the quantities given converts every
  stated quantity into a unit-price cap.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order.

- Mobilization count. A bidder may include exactly one mobilization for elevator shafts, or two for
  the project, with remobilizations beyond that compensated. On a phased or delayed job this is the
  most likely change order in the trade.
- Access assumptions. Access for a mixing station, material storage and handling, water and power at
  a reasonable distance, and access for a mast-climbing scaffold at the shafts are each stated as
  preconditions. Where a mason works off a scaffold erected by others, it may reserve a right to
  review and sign off that scaffold before use and to adjust its price if the scaffold is later
  altered, which is a real constraint once a scaffold has been tagged for use.
- Minimum billable repair size, on restoration, as set out in section 3.
- Quantity ceilings, as above.
- Price hold periods. Thirty days is the norm, sometimes requiring a written notice of intent to
  hold a further thirty. One bidder states that after six months from the original bid date,
  increases to labour and material may be applied.
- Labour type and wage basis, stated either as union at prevailing rates or as open shop with no
  predetermined wage rates and no residency requirements.
- Normal working hours, sometimes stated as a specific weekday window.
- Scaffolding for this bidder's use only, no other trades permitted access.
- Silica exclusion zones the general contractor must create and maintain, with the work stopping at
  no cost to the bidder if anyone enters them.
- A markup rate on additional work, commonly stated as a fixed percentage for overhead and profit.

### Commercial terms that change what the number is worth

A minority of bidders attach a commercial addendum that materially changes the value of their price,
and on one proposal the addendum was longer than the scope. Treat these as scope, because they are:
no liquidated, actual, indirect or consequential damages and no penalties for late performance; a
schedule-slip clause converting other trades' delays into a change order; a delay-recovery clause
entitling the bidder to labour, material and equipment costs if the project is delayed beyond a
stated number of days; a statement that schedule-of-values figures are not deduct values; symmetric
back-charge language; capped indemnity that will not extend to the indemnified party's own
negligence; a disclaimer that the bidder is not a design professional and does not warrant the
sufficiency of the plans; and a list of general contractor obligations covering power, lighting,
water, climate control, dust control, dumpsters and a wash station. Two bidders with the same number
and different addenda are not offering the same thing.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

Masonry is a seam-heavy trade because almost everything embedded in a masonry wall is bought from
somebody else and set by the mason. The recurring pattern is furnished by others, installed by the
mason, with the furnish side excluded by everyone. Where a scope line does not say who furnishes and
who installs, it will be answered three different ways by three bidders.

### Steel in and on the wall: the seam that matters most

Relieving and shelf angles, loose lintels, embeds, elevator separator and hoist beams, and
top-of-wall restraint clips all live here, and the market treats them inconsistently:

- Relieving and shelf angles. Furnished by the metals package, with essentially every mason
  excluding the furnish. Installation splits three ways: carried silently, excluded outright
  alongside all welding, or surfaced as a priced add alternate on a linear-foot basis. On one
  project the estimator's own plug rate for installing the angle was twice the rate the bidder
  eventually quoted, which is the size of the uncertainty when nobody states it.
- Loose lintels. Furnish excluded by most; install answered yes by masons who answer a scope sheet
  and unstated by masons who do not.
- Steel lintels at masonry openings. The cleanest statement seen is provided by others, installed by
  us, if required. The conditional matters, because whether they are required is a drawings question
  the mason has already decided.
- Embeds and miscellaneous items to be built into masonry. Furnished by others, install-only by the
  mason, and this one is usually stated cleanly.
- Elevator separator beams, hoist beams and shaft items. Install-only by the mason where a scope
  sheet asks; unmentioned otherwise.
- Top-of-wall clips, angles and lateral restraint at the tops of partitions. Excluded on both sides
  by several bidders. This is a real gap between masonry, metals and drywall.
- Welding. Excluded by name by most masons, which means any connection requiring a weld is not in
  the masonry number even if the piece it connects is.

### Cast stone and precast

The supplier bids material only, delivered to the jobsite, and the boundary is unusually explicit:
embedded anchoring hardware such as inserts and slots is included, but all loose hardware, meaning
grout, shims, pins, anchors, weeps and sealant, is not. Neither is unloading, storage, installation,
field measuring including verify-in-field, or engineering for anchoring, reinforcing and lifting.
Everything after the truck belongs to the mason, and the engineering belongs to nobody unless
someone assigns it.

Two further consequences. The supplier's quote enumerates by mark with a linear-foot quantity, a
profile dimension and a piece count, and it asks the buyer to confirm the mark list is accurate and
complete, which shifts takeoff risk onto the mason. And the supplier's lead time governs the wall:
shop drawings run several weeks from a signed purchase order, and fabrication and first delivery
several weeks more from receipt of all approvals including colour. Late colour selection delays the
facade, not the shop.

### Brick and thin brick material

Brick is usually unselected at bid, so the mason carries an allowance stated per individual brick.
Where the general contractor obtains a supplier price list issued to all bidders, every mason prices
the same material and the differences between their numbers become labour and risk rather than
brick, which is the single most useful thing a general contractor can do to make masonry bids
comparable.

The supplier delivers flatbed direct to the jobsite with the customer unloading, and title passes at
the plant. Partial shipments or phasing incur additional freight, freight is indexed to a published
fuel index and moves with it, and custom material must be paid for whether taken or not, which turns
an over-ordered special shape into pure loss.

Thin brick is a system, not a veneer. Its price list is a kit: rail, wafer screws, proprietary
mortar, application gun, building wrap and wrap tape, each with its own coverage rate. Glazing may
be quoted as an additional cost to be determined. Pricing thin brick as an area of brick misses most
of the assembly, and a shaped or corner unit costs roughly twice a flat.

### The barrier and flashing seam

This is where new masonry leaks scope most reliably. The mason typically carries through-wall
flashing, base-of-wall flashing, transition membrane strips, drip edge, termination bar, weeps and
cavity drainage, and typically excludes the air and vapour barrier, the weather-resistive barrier,
dampproofing, waterproofing, counterflashing, sill pan flashing and coping. The assembly therefore
splits in the middle, and the split is not in the same place for every bidder: one carries the
transition membrane as its own, another excludes all barrier work including the membrane. Where the
architect's assembly calls for a fluid-applied barrier behind the veneer, expect nobody to have
priced it.

Sealing of brick ties where they penetrate the barrier is a small item that a scope sheet asks about
explicitly and that bidders answer inconsistently, including one answer of self-sealing, which is a
product claim rather than a scope answer.

### Elevators and shafts

Three separate items live here and they are commonly conflated. Building the shaft is masonry.
Infilling around elevator door fronts after the entrances are set is a comeback operation that
requires a second mobilization, and a bidder may include exactly one. Running the elevator car
during that work costs money, and masons carve that out to the general contractor by name. Whether
the shafts are built from inside or outside changes the access method and is asked as its own scope
question.

### Other seams

- Painting. Block is assumed smooth and natural in colour and painted by others. Where a coloured or
  architectural block is specified, check that somebody priced it, since coloured mortar and pigments
  are commonly excluded too.
- Tile and interior finishes. Interior brick marks that appear on interior design drawings get
  pulled toward masonry and may be routed to tile by the estimator. Interior fireplace and hearth
  stone is excluded by masons by name.
- Concrete. The backup wall may be block or cast concrete depending on the assembly, and where a
  garage perimeter is precast concrete panel rather than masonry the panel and its flashing still
  land on the masonry scope sheet because no other package claims them. Grouting at steel columns
  and beams is a masonry-shaped item that goes unbid.
- Waterproofing. On restoration the mason usually carries crystalline waterproofing, vault
  penetrations and window perimeter sealant. On new work it carries none of it. The same company may
  file the same document under both a masonry and a waterproofing trade token, which is one bidder,
  not two.
- Roofing. Copper coping, roof flashing and membrane at the masonry interface are excluded by most
  restoration masons while the brick around them is carried.
- Landscaping and sitework. Granite curb, site walls, pavers and the concrete steps up to a podium
  are drawn on landscape sheets and excluded by masons, but they may still appear as a required
  breakout on the owner's bid form.
- Layout. Some masons require benchmarks, control lines and elevations from the general contractor
  before they can lay out, and exclude establishing them.

### Access, again, as a seam

On restoration the access question decomposes into more rows than any other subject: the method
overall, which may differ per elevation; pipe or system scaffold; swing staging; mast climbers;
shoring under mast climbers where they bear on vaulted sidewalks; and providing staging to the
owner's engineer for inspection. A single mast climber or swing stage arrangement may be disclosed
only as an annotation on a drawing sheet bound into the back of a proposal rather than in its text.
Read the attachments.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

### What masons actually price from

On new work, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The exterior wall assembly schedule. This is the primary document. Assembly codes, the barrier
   and insulation each carries, and the substrate behind the veneer all come from here, and the best
   proposals quote the codes back. It is also the document most likely to disagree with the plans:
   on one project the estimator annotated several assembly codes as not found on the plans and one
   as showing a different cladding in the detail than the schedule names.
2. The elevations, for veneer extent, mark distribution, coursing patterns, sills and copings.
3. The structural drawings, for reinforcing size and spacing, grouting requirements and shelf angle
   locations. Masons read structural closely and quote the actual bar sizes and spacings back.
4. The plans, for block locations, shaft and stair enclosures, firewalls and back-of-house rooms.
5. The material and finish schedules, for brick and cast stone mark designations, manufacturers,
   colours and formats.
6. The specification, mostly for block strength, mortar type, grout strength and reinforcement
   standards. Cited by section number, rarely quoted.

On restoration:

1. The elevations, which carry the repair notes keyed to areas of the facade, and which are the
   document nearly every bidder names as its basis.
2. The independent existing-conditions report, where one exists. This is the second document, and it
   is the trade's central documents problem.
3. Details, for through-wall flashing, copings, lead joint covers and cornice conditions.
4. A site walk. Restoration quantities that appear after a walkthrough tend to be the real ones.

### The two-document problem on restoration

Where an engineer's facade condition report and the architect's repair elevations both exist, they
will not agree, and bidders will price different ones. On one project a bidder priced strictly off
the drawings and excluded the report's rebuild and displaced-masonry items entirely, while a
competitor included those same items as a qualified allowance in the base bid. Both called it the
base bid. The estimator's response was correct and worth copying: the first two rows of the scope
sheet, ahead of any scope at all, ask which document each bidder priced, with a note that some
report items had been value-engineered off the elevations. Make the documents conflict a scope row.
It is a request for information upstream, not a leveling problem downstream.

### Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number

- Labour type. Union or open shop, and the presence or absence of a market recovery or wage
  concession on the union side. Restoration in this market skews heavily union; new multifamily work
  skews open shop, and on one new-work list the sole union bidder was the highest at roughly twice
  the low.
- Prevailing wage applicability, residency and workforce goals, and minority or disadvantaged
  business participation requirements. All of them are separately excluded and separately repriced.
- Who owns exterior access, and if the mason does not, what the general contractor will pay for it
  and for how many months.
- Whether the shafts are built from inside or outside.
- How many mobilizations the sequence will actually require, against the one or two a bidder
  included.
- Brick selection and its lead time. Brick has run sixteen to eighteen weeks after approvals on a
  real project, which makes it the long pole and a standing value engineering candidate.
- Cast stone shop drawing and fabrication lead time, which starts only at a signed purchase order
  and runs again from colour approval.
- Insurance basis. Whether an owner-controlled programme applies is asked as a scope row, and where
  it does not, general liability is quoted as a separate add.
- Bond requirement, quoted as an add of roughly one to one and a half percent of contract value, and
  written in by hand at five percent by one bidder who was not otherwise in range.
- Project portal and payment-application fees, quoted as a fraction of a percent of contract value
  with a cap, plus a flat fee per sub-tier subcontractor.
- Warranty duration. A deduct alternate for reducing the warranty from two years to one was declined
  by both bidders asked, which is itself informative.
- Winter. Whether the schedule puts veneer work into cold weather, and who pays for enclosure, heat,
  extra planks on the staging, snow removal from staging, relocation of materials and the production
  the crew loses working cold. Every bidder excludes all of it.
- Site logistics: mixing station location, material laydown, water and power proximity, off-site
  parking, and whether other trades will be allowed on the mason's staging.
- Silica control and exclusion zones, which are the general contractor's duty and which stop the
  work when breached.
- On restoration, whether hazardous materials are present. Bidders price assuming they are not and
  attach an indemnity.

### Set-stage behavior

At design development a mason names materials and locations. By the construction-document round the
same mason names assembly codes, product basis of design and reinforcing spacing, because the
assembly schedule now exists. Do not ask for assembly-level breakouts before the assembly schedule
exists; ask for material-family breakouts instead, and expect the vocabulary to change under you at
the next issue.

Also expect the folder's set stage and the proposal's stated basis to disagree. A bidder may price a
construction-document round off the permit set and say so, or say nothing until asked. A revised
proposal that still cites the superseded set is common enough to check every time.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

### Units the trade thinks in

- Square feet of wall face, for block, brick veneer, thin brick and stone veneer. The dominant unit
  on new work and the one every quantity question is asked in.
- Each, per individual brick, for the material allowance. This is the only comparable material figure
  most masonry bids contain, and shaped or rowlock sill brick carries its own higher rate.
- Pieces, for brick counted rather than measured, which a bidder may switch to mid-negotiation.
- Linear feet, for cast stone bands, copings and cornices, for relieving angles, for lead joint
  covers, for transverse joints, for sealant and for flashing.
- Each, for cast stone pieces alongside their linear footage, for stone window sills, for Dutchman
  repairs, for door openings in block walls and for mock-up panels.
- Square feet, for restoration action classes: repointing, replacement, rebuild, cleaning, coating,
  parging, spall and erosion repair.
- Each, for brick crack repair and replacement on restoration, which is the one restoration line
  regularly counted rather than measured.
- Bags, for mortar, both standard and bulk, and for thin brick proprietary mortar with a stated
  coverage per bag.
- Rolls, for building wrap and tape, with stated coverage.
- Cubes, for brick delivery, at a stated count per cube.
- Hours, for masons, tenders and labourers, and for supervision. A general contractor asks for total
  mason hours and total tender hours as separate scope rows.
- Months, for equipment: telehandler, mortar mixer, mortar silo, and above all staging.
- Twenty-eight day periods, for scaffold rental.
- Man-days, for surveying and for providing staging to an inspecting engineer.
- Percent of contract value, for bonds, for general liability where an owner-controlled programme
  does not apply, and for project portal fees.
- Residential units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package, used even on a facade
  restoration where the connection to units is purely notional. One project instead compared lump
  sums directly.

### The rate hierarchy

Rates per square foot of wall face rank consistently across bidders and projects, and the ranking is
more stable than any individual number:

1. Concrete block backup is the cheapest per square foot by a wide margin.
2. Full-bed brick veneer runs roughly one and a half to two times block.
3. Thin brick runs at or above full-bed brick despite using a fraction of the material, because the
   rail system, its fasteners, its proprietary mortar and its wrap replace what the brick would
   otherwise have been. Assuming thin brick is a saving because it uses less brick is the most
   common wrong intuition in this trade.
4. Stone and granite veneer runs roughly three to six times block, and its rate per square foot rises
   sharply as the quantity falls, because a small stone package cannot absorb its own fabrication,
   handling and setup. A few hundred square feet of granite can carry a rate ten times the brick
   rate on the same building.

On restoration the ranking is by action rather than material. Field repointing is the cheapest per
unit; transverse or lead-covered joint repointing runs well above it; brick replacement runs above
that; and full stone replacement, Dutchman repairs and stone sill replacement are the most expensive
work in the trade by a large margin, running into the thousands per unit. Cleaning is cheap per
square foot and enormous in area, so it lands as a mid-sized number.

### Bidder quantity dispersion, which is the leveling problem

Three bidders pricing one construction-document set stated materially different areas for the same
four assemblies. The spreads ran about one and a quarter to one on block, about one and a quarter on
thin brick, about one and two fifths on brick veneer and about one and two fifths on stone. Their
rates disagreed as well, and the two errors did not cancel: the bidder with the largest brick area
quoted the lowest brick rate. A base-bid comparison therefore hides two compounding differences, and
neither is visible without asking for quantities.

Worse, a bidder's stated quantities are not stable across its own rounds. One bidder answered the
same quantity question two rounds apart with a single total area in one round and a set of mark
areas plus piece counts in the next, differing by a factor of about three. Bidder-stated quantities
cannot be used as a takeoff check unless the round is pinned.

The practical rule: ask every bidder for area by assembly and by mark, ask for total mason hours and
tender hours separately, and treat the answers as claims to reconcile against a real takeoff rather
than as the takeoff.

### Labour

Straight-time rates seen run roughly in the eighties to the low hundreds per hour for masons, the
mid-seventies to mid-eighties for tenders and labourers and operators, and the mid-nineties to about
a hundred for foremen. Premium time runs at one and a half times and double time at twice straight
time, uniformly. Crew sizes on a large multifamily masonry package run around twenty-five, with
total project hours in the tens of thousands, and a general contractor asks for both.

Rates alone do not explain the union versus open shop gap, which appears in the totals rather than in
the posted rates and is the single largest structural driver in the trade.

### Taxes and markups

Sales tax applies to material and not to labour, and careful bidders show it that way, with a zero
labour tax line beside a material tax line. Scaffold quotes tax the rental line only, not the
installation, removal, trucking or consumables. Bonding is applied as a post-tax markup. Additional
work carries a stated overhead and profit markup, commonly a fixed percentage.

### Dispersion and bid list depth

Masonry bid lists run about six to nine bidders per project, which is far shallower than a painting
list. Dispersion by work type:

- New masonry is comparatively tight. On one project the totals spanned a ratio of about one and a
  third to one across six bidders, the narrowest list seen. On another the spread was about two and a
  half to one, but excluding a single outlier it narrowed to about one and three fifths.
- Restoration is wild. On one project the cost per unit spanned a ratio of about five to one across
  the ranked bidders. That is not noise; it is bidders pricing genuinely different scopes, because
  the action classes, the access ownership and the choice of basis document all differ between them.

Two warnings about rankings. First, a furnish-only supply quote will sit at the top of an ascending
ranking as the apparent low bidder, because it is a fraction of the size of a full package. A cast
stone supply quote ranked alongside masonry packages twenty to sixty times its size on one project.
Package breadth has to be a field on the ranking, not something the reader infers. Second, a bidder
whose breakout does not resemble the field, for instance one placing three times as much value in
block as in brick and stone when everyone else does the opposite, has priced a different job, and its
total should be set aside rather than levelled.

### How an estimator actually compares

Base bids are not compared. Base bid plus adjustments is compared, where adjustments are to-value
plugs added identically to every bidder for what the market systematically will not carry. The plugs
that recur are sill and metal flashing, cavity insulation, relieving angle installation, building
tie-ins and patching around hoists, and on restoration, access. The estimator's own plug rate and the
bidder's eventual price for the same item can differ by a factor of two, so a plug is a placeholder
for a question, not an answer.

Where a bidder supplies no single base bid, because it offered a menu, several priced blocks, or a
base plus a second table, the whole value tends to get rolled into the adjustments column, which
means the base bid field is not a like-for-like comparison across the row. Read the ranking, not the
base column.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly three dozen masonry proposals and supplier quotes, four estimator
leveling sheets, one general contractor scope sheet returned by a bidder in full, one owner bid form
filled in by hand, and two exterior access quotes, drawn from four projects in one regional market,
all bid by one general contractor. Three of the four projects are new multifamily residential
construction with structured parking and amenity space; the fourth is a facade restoration and
interior renovation of an existing masonry building, and it is the source of nearly everything this
file says about restoration. The proposals span design development, permit, construction documents
and conformed sets, and several bidders appear on more than one project, which is what makes the
boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later rounds:

- Everything about restoration rests on a single project. The action-class pricing rule is strongly
  evidenced there, including one bidder pricing the same elevation three ways, but it has not been
  tested against a second restoration job, a different architect, or a different engineer's report
  convention.
- Only one full estimator-style takeoff exists, so the finer restoration action classes, half stone
  replacement, Dutchman repairs, stone sill replacement and erosion tooling, rest on that single
  document.
- Only one material supplier price list is available, so the ratios between field brick, special
  shapes and thin brick components rest on one sheet from one supplier at one date.
- Only one cast stone supply quote is available, so the furnish-install boundary in section 5 rests
  on that vendor's terms rather than on the market's.
- Nothing here is tested against commercial, institutional, healthcare, industrial or public
  prevailing-wage work. Every project is private, and prevailing wage appears only as something
  bidders exclude.
- No structural masonry, load-bearing masonry, glass block or masonry in seismic regions appears.
  Every building here uses block as backup, shafts and partitions rather than as structure.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is available, so nothing shows which exclusions survived
  negotiation, which plugs were bought out, or where the access ownership finally landed on the
  restoration job.
- No change order history is available. The claim that mobilization counts, access assumptions and
  minimum repair sizes convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather
  than observed, so treat it as untested.
- The trade has not shown how masonry is packaged when a project carries no exterior veneer at all,
  or when block is bought inside a concrete package rather than a masonry one.
- Cold-weather masonry has not been observed priced. Every bidder excludes winter conditions and no
  document in hand prices them, so the size of that plug is unknown.
- Interior historic stone, marble and tile restoration appears as nine unanswered scope rows and one
  specialist quote. What that work actually costs and who normally carries it is not established.
