# Drywall

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the drywall package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real drywall contractors can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

There is no single anchor section. The package spans Divisions 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 at once, and the
proposals say so: bidders cite 092900 gypsum board, 092116 non-structural metal framing, 092116.23 or
092120 shaft wall assemblies, 054000 cold-formed metal framing, 061000 or 061050 rough carpentry for
blocking, 061600 sheathing, 072100 thermal insulation, 078443 joint firestopping, 079219 acoustical
joint sealants, 079513 interior expansion joint covers, 081113 hollow metal doors and frames for
install labor, 066400 FRP paneling, and 095100 acoustical ceiling tile. One estimator's own drywall
sheet lists fourteen specification sections across six divisions, air barriers and penetration
firestopping and corner guards among them. An estimator files it under a 09 21 code, but the exact
code drifts between 09 2100, 09 2110 and 09 2116 from project to project, and it drifts inside one
office and inside one project: the same estimator codes one job three ways across a precon sheet, a
buyout sheet and a procurement log. The code is a weak identifier. Some bidders still write the old
09250 or 092110 numbering.

The trade token is the stronger identifier and it is not a strong one either. It names a material,
and the material appears in two packages at two scales. See the framing regime below.

Two questions decide what a number means before any physical scope question does: whether the
load-bearing framing is inside the package, and whether the insulation is. Establish both before
comparing anything.

### The framing regime

On a building framed in wood above a podium, the framing carpenter builds the structural walls and
the drywall contractor boards and finishes them, and the drywall contractor builds the metal framing
at the podium, the shafts, the soffits and the ceilings. That is the case this file is calibrated on
and section 5 sets out its seam in detail.

On a building whose upper structure is load-bearing light gauge rather than wood, the framing is
structure, so it is bought as structure. It leaves this package entirely and becomes its own
Division 5 buy, with its own bid list of proprietary system suppliers, panel fabricators, erectors
and turnkey frame divisions, its own leveling sheet, and a furnish-and-install split inside it. Its
scope sheet can run three times the length of the drywall sheet in the same workbook. Every bidder in
that pool excludes drywall, drywall blocking, interior non-bearing partitions, dropped soffits and
ceilings, resilient channel, shaft liner and window blocking by name. The drywall contractor boards
what the system builds.

Four things follow. Where the building type lets the framing leave, they are the most consequential
packaging facts in the trade and they rank ahead of insulation, because the framing moves a number by
a factor of two where insulation moves it by a fraction. Where the framing cannot leave, which is
most jobs, insulation is the larger fact. Section 7 ranks the drivers the same way.

- The split is decided per round, not per project. One project bought the load-bearing framing as its
  own package in one round and back inside drywall in the next round eighteen months later, with no
  framing package at all the second time. A scope list that assumes the framing is always in this
  package will produce a sheet nobody in that pool can answer.
- Which scope leaves depends on which supplier is bought, not on the framing material. An estimator
  comparing competing systems tabulates them on exactly these axes: whether the system carries window
  installation, the exterior walls, the non-bearing partitions, the stair shafts, the elevator
  shafts, the sheathing, the stairs, the floor deck, the deck topping and the balconies. The answers
  differ system by system. One carries exterior walls, non-bearing partitions, sheathing and stairs;
  one carries exterior walls and neither the partitions nor the shafts; one carries the deck and
  nothing else.
- The system pre-decides this package's assembly. A supplier publishes the requirements its product
  imposes on other packages, and three of them land in drywall: resilient channel required at every
  floor and ceiling assembly to hold the listed ratings, a heavier channel grade required at all
  demising and corridor walls, and ceilings required to be a named board type. It also claims
  savings in drywall's own scope, no plumb or level furring at gypsum walls and no dropped soffits.
  An estimator's own three-way scenario model carries the identical drywall line under every regime
  and then adds three named adjustments under the light gauge regimes: a credit for deleting one
  board layer at the exterior walls, an add for resilient channel at corridors, and an add for
  isolation clips and an added board layer at demising. Net, about a twentieth of the drywall number,
  decided in another package. A drywall scope list written without knowing the framing regime is
  written against the wrong assembly.
- The choice is close to cost-neutral at the project level and enormous at the package level. In that
  same model the whole-project trade subtotal moves by well under a hundredth between regimes, while
  the two divisions carrying the frame move by roughly two and a half times and roughly three and a
  half times in opposite directions. You cannot infer the framing regime from a project budget.

The counterparty changes with the regime. Under wood it is a framing carpenter. Under light gauge it
is a system supplier, an erector, and often a deck manufacturer standing behind the supplier, with
all three behind one column. On a fully panelised building the seam is three-party: a manufacturer
furnishes, an erector installs, and a drywall contractor boards and finishes.

### The insulation block

The package as bid in the market is metal framing plus gypsum board plus taping, with insulation
attached as a detachable block. Every company that touches insulation separates it in some way, and
the separation is not one buyer's request: it arrives as an exclusion, a second priced block, an add
alternate, a deduct alternate, a breakout with no price change, and a whole second base bid, across
companies that share nothing.

Insulation in or out is the single largest source of incomparability in the trade, because bidders
differ on whether they carry it at all far more than they differ on what it costs. Bidders on the
same project, reading the same drawings, hand back numbers that are not comparable purely because one
carried it and the other did not, and neither is wrong.

What it is worth is a per-bidder fact and not a market constant. Where a bidder prices its own
insulation as a separable block against its own drywall number, the block runs from about a
twenty-eighth to about three fifths. Most breakouts sit between about a fifteenth and a fifth. The
share is larger where the bidder also carries mineral wool and a vapor retarder, and larger again on
a large podium with a deep insulation schedule, where six independent breakouts on one bid list
cluster nearer a half. It falls to about a twentieth on office core and shell and on conversions of
existing buildings, because the exterior walls already exist and little cavity insulation sits inside
the block at all. On one project two bidders differ by more than two to one on the same wall types.
No breakout seen reaches a doubling.

Two cautions on that number. An estimator's plug for a bidder that omitted the block runs higher than
any bidder's own breakout, up to about four sevenths of the bid it is applied to, and two valuations
of one block on one bid list can differ by two fifths. And insulation is the most rate-dispersed
material in the assembly: across seven named insulation products on one bidder's own rate card the
rates span nearly two to one among themselves and about eighteen to one against the cheapest board
rate. That dispersion is the mechanism behind the ratio's spread.

The block is also detaching. On the newest work thermal insulation is its own package with its own
bid list, entering the drywall ranking only as an other-subcontractor plug, and a buyout sheet asks
on four separate rows whether the batt, the acoustic fiberglass, the spray-applied and a
concrete-faced panel belong to this trade or to that one.

### Collapse attractors

Scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another division or another spec
section.

- Insulation, as above. Usually batt and mineral wool at minimum. Spray foam, blown and
  spray-applied insulation are named and excluded by most bidders carrying batt, and carried inside
  the drywall base by a minority, so the exclusion is about the insulation system rather than about
  foam: a bidder excluding all spray foam can still carry low-expansion foam at door and window
  perimeters. Rigid goes with the envelope except where it sits behind board, and then it goes with
  the board.
- In-wall and in-ceiling blocking, almost always bounded to the bidder's own framing. That bound is a
  framing-trade convention rather than a drywall habit: the load-bearing panel framer writes the
  identical bound in its own scope.
- Hollow metal frame installation, labor only, frames furnished by others. Nearly every bidder
  carries it and nearly every bidder excludes the doors and the hardware. The bound varies more than
  it looks: by substrate, excluded at concrete and masonry openings; by floor; by corridor only; and
  on conversions some bidders carry the doors and hardware too.
- Access panel installation, material by others, usually with a per-panel price and an allowance
  count rather than a schedule. Not reliably kept: some bidders exclude access panels and any device
  penetrating any wall outright, and one carries them at zero pending a schedule.
- Firestopping and acoustical sealant, bounded to the tops and bottoms of the assemblies the bidder
  built. Penetrations by other trades are excluded from every base bid seen.
- Shaft wall assemblies, including shaftliner, C-T studs and J-runners. Carried by nearly every bidder
  that names them, and worth checking rather than assuming: a bidder can name none at all, hand
  named shaft marks to the carpenter, or find the whole core displaced by a bought module.
- Gypsum board ceilings, soffits, suspension grid, resilient channel and sound isolation clips.
- Exterior cold-formed framing and exterior gypsum sheathing at the podium or metal-framed levels,
  usually with delegated-design shop drawings and calculations. Where a panelised system carries the
  sheathing, the drywall bidders carry it too, which is a double count on the same project.
- Fiberglass reinforced plastic panels, wall protection and fabric or acoustic wall panels. Contested
  with painting and specialties, and on some bid lists excluded by every bidder and carried by none,
  which leaves it unassigned.
- Fire extinguisher cabinet installation and corner guard installation, both usually install-only.
- Acoustical ceiling tile, on any building type other than multifamily. See the carve-outs below.
- Rough carpentry as a standing priced line inside the drywall number on office work, at between a
  fourteenth and a fifth of the scope.
- Priming and painting. A base bid can carry one coat of a named primer and sealer on all smooth
  walls, then sanded; an estimate can carry interior painting as a second priced line; and one
  proposal prices painting inside its drywall number at more than a third of a building's total.
- On renovation and conversion work, roof-level framing, decking, coverboard, curbs, parapet plywood
  and penthouse framing, which pulls a substantial amount of Division 5 and 7 into a Division 9 bid.
  On a panelised job a drywall bidder can furnish and install the composite floor deck and pour stop.
- Gypsum floor underlayment. A minority of drywall bidders quote it alongside the drywall package,
  and the same companies also bid it as a standalone trade, so the same scope can arrive twice. One
  file filed under both trade tokens carries a drywall block and an underlayment block under one base
  bid total while specialist underlayment bids sit in the same folder.
- Mock-ups, in-place and exterior, carried in some base bids and excluded by name in others.
- On a conversion, tin ceilings at named ceiling marks, priced as an alternate.

### Specialty carve-outs

Scope that leaves this package even when it looks like drywall.

- Load-bearing light gauge framing and prefabricated structural panels. See the framing regime above.
  This is the largest carve-out in the trade and the file's own trade token does not tell you it has
  happened.
- Acoustical ceiling tile, on multifamily. There it is its own package with its own bid list, bid by
  ceiling specialists who quantify by mark and by square foot with named grid systems. Drywall
  bidders price it anyway, sometimes in the base and sometimes as an alternate, so the same ceiling
  can be carried twice or dropped entirely. Outside multifamily the carve-out does not hold: on
  office core and shell most bidders carry the tile inside the drywall number, on a theatre both
  bidders carry a named acoustical ceiling system in the base, and on a healthcare renovation a
  bidder carries tile at a stated material rate and a stated labor rate. On those building types the
  boundary is drawn by an answer to a request for information rather than by convention, and one such
  answer routed integrated ceiling assemblies out of the ceilings package. Section 5 covers the seam.
- Wood framing of every kind. On a wood-framed or podium building the structural framing belongs to
  the framing carpenter.
- Masonry interior partitions and CMU shaft construction.
- Through-penetration firestopping around pipes, conduit and duct.
- Gypcrete and floor underlayment, which has its own bid list on nearly every project seen.
- Plaster restoration and glass-fiber-reinforced ornamental work on a historic building, which is bid
  as its own numbered package and excluded by the drywall bidders.

## 2. How the market bids

Drywall is not reliably a one-number trade. On most bid lists the default output is two to five
numbers and the segmentation is commercial rather than physical: drywall and framing as one figure,
insulation as another, and then whatever the general contractor asked to see broken out. But the
single undifferentiated lump sum is not always the minority shape. On some bid lists it is modal, and
in any pool carrying structural system suppliers and erectors it dominates. Where segmentation
appears outside the commercial pattern it is by scope family, by building, by floor, or by labor
configuration. The lump sum is the hardest shape to level and it is the shape that makes an exclusion
list decisive, because there is no priced breakout to beat it.

Grain, not number count, is what distinguishes proposals here. Seven distinct shapes appear.

- The house form. A pre-printed matrix listing board by type, stud by size and gauge and spacing,
  insulation by product, and sealant by condition, with every quantity column reading "per partition
  schedule" or "per schedule" and a level of finish stated per board type. This is a deliberately
  quantity-free document that tells you exactly what materials were priced and nothing about how
  much. It is the most informative shape for scope purposes and the least informative for quantity.
- The scope narrative. A bulleted list of furnish-and-install statements organized by building
  element, sometimes keyed to wall-type marks and detail numbers. The common shape.
- The labor-and-material answer grid. A pre-printed table of work families with a tick in a labor
  column and a tick in a material column, sometimes four columns wide with included and excluded
  ticked separately on each side, over sixty rows. This makes the furnish-and-install seam an axis of
  the form rather than a note, and it is the most readable scope document a bid list produces. It
  also fails text extraction completely: the marks detach from the row labels and survive only in a
  render. Bidders abuse their own grids, ticking both columns on one row and ticking a header row
  included while ticking every named item beneath it excluded, so parse a grid against its labels
  rather than literally.
- The spec-section answer. Scope organized section number by section number with an inclusion or
  exclusion carve-out written under each. Uncommon, and the most precise about boundaries.
- The measured takeoff. Pricing by area family or by assembly mark with real quantities, linear feet
  of interior and shaft wall and square feet of ceiling per reflected ceiling plan. Uncommon, and
  worth reading closely when it appears, because it is where the trade's real quantities become
  visible.
- The phase price. The package split by construction phase rather than by scope, with the phase
  prices summing to the same total. Uncommon, and the shape a renovation with enabling work takes.
- The returned scope sheet. The general contractor's own scope form handed back with a Yes, No or
  dollar figure against every row, sometimes with the price written by hand in the margin and no
  proposal letter at all, and sometimes as numbered questions issued and answered by email rather
  than as a form. A pipeline that only reads letterhead proposals will drop real bids, including, on
  one project, the second-highest bid on the board. But on most projects no returned sheet exists at
  all, and the bidders' answers live only as cells in the estimator's own workbook.

Two document classes ride behind any of those shapes and are easy to miss. The takeoff or estimating
workbook annex: a bidder's own priced workbook, a set of color-classified markups over the
architect's sheets, or a digital takeoff export, bound behind a lump-sum letter. It carries the only
real quantities in the trade and it is frequently invisible to text extraction. And the accounting or
estimating platform estimate: an invoicing-platform document with bill-to and ship-to blocks,
product-or-service rows, an expiry date, a consumer financing offer and a pre-filled acceptance line.
Those behave nothing like a construction proposal, carry no exclusion list and no basis document, may
leave the price column blank, and still become ranking columns at face value.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Board types with thickness and rating, and often a manufacturer and product line. Nearly always.
- Stud depth, gauge and spacing, usually as nominal dimensions, sometimes in mils of base metal
  thickness, and occasionally by the industry stud designation code.
- Level of finish by condition. Almost always present in some form, and it is the most consistently
  stated technical fact in the trade. The default pattern is Level 4 at surfaces exposed to view,
  Level 1 or fire tape at concealed and plenum areas, Level 5 only where scheduled to receive wall
  covering or in amenity space, and Level 2 at substrates receiving tile. Some bidders subdivide
  further, distinguishing Level 1 at joints only from Level 1 at joints and penetrations depending on
  whether the wall above the ceiling is fire rated or sound rated. Two exceptions: in a pool where
  much of the bid list is a structural framing bidder rather than a drywall bidder, only about half
  state a level at all; and on office work where the finish is a single coat veneer plaster, the
  whole apparatus is simply absent from the bid.
- Labor type, and here it is stated far more often than in most finishes trades, because it is among
  the largest price drivers. Union, open shop, prevailing wage, or a specific mix, often with the
  geographic zone and the residential-versus-commercial classification on the face of the proposal.
- The basis document, a named set with a date. Nearly always, and worth checking, because it is
  frequently not the set in the folder.
- Whether taxes are included. Commonly.

What a proposal typically will not state, with the caveats each now carries:

- Quantities, in the pricing. Measured quantities in the pricing are uncommon, and where a quantity
  column exists on the bidder's own form it is often filled in with the word "lump", which is at
  least honest. But quantities behind the pricing are less rare than that suggests: seventy pages of
  measured takeoff by wall-type mark, sixteen pages of on-screen takeoff export, fifty-eight pages of
  color-coded markup, and a full component workbook all sit behind lump-sum letters. Ask for the
  takeoff.
- How the number splits between labor and material. Rarely visible, and visible more often than the
  old rule allows: an explicit labor line and materials line per building summing to each building
  total, a full cost disclosure of labor, materials, overhead and profit, insurance, carried
  contingency and material tax, and a tax line that reconciles only if tax was applied to material
  alone. Where it is not printed, the estimator's own derivation from a supply quote is sometimes the
  only place it lives.
- Manhours. Rarely, and no longer only on request for estimating: a general contractor asking for
  hours by craft in order to pursue workforce participation funding gets them, sometimes by email
  that is then stapled to the quote.
- A go-back or punch allowance, unless the general contractor asked. Patching is usually either
  absent or an allowance rather than a priced scope, and on many bid lists it is absent from every
  proposal.
- Escalation. It appears as a disclaimer pushing material escalation onto the owner, as a supplier's
  price-increase letter attached to the proposal, as an included line on the face of a bid and
  disclaimed on the next page, as a stated percentage band applied to a whole stale bid, as a
  material hold to a named date with awards after it re-evaluated, and as a carve-out for new or
  added tariffs.
- Bond, unless requested. See section 4 for what the rate does.
- Change-order labor rates by craft. Quoted by nearly every bidder on some bid lists and by not one
  bidder on others. It is not a market constant and it should not be assumed.

Exclusion behavior splits the market, and the split is wider here than in most trades. Some proposals
carry a single exclusion line and some carry none at all on a full package. Others run past forty. A
one-line list, or no list, is not broader scope: it is every boundary left open, and at leveling each
one becomes a plug or a question. The converse also holds and matters: a thin exclusion list on a
proposal that enumerates every wall type with quantities is not every boundary left open, because the
enumeration carries the boundaries. Read the exclusion list against what the pricing shows, not on
its own.

Beware the self-contradicting proposal, which is common enough to expect. A repeat bidder's printed
exclusion block contradicts its own priced scope table in the same document: excluding wall panels
while the facing page's material table carries a named panel product, excluding an acoustic ceiling
system while the inclusion list carries a named monolithic one, excluding all insulation two pages
after including fiberglass insulation, or carrying one item as a numbered qualification, a numbered
exclusion and a priced add alternate at once. Where the two disagree, the priced breakout is the bid
and the block is stationery. Two refinements. Where the priced breakout is a single lump line, the
rule has nothing to compare against, and the lump line's own description becomes the tiebreaker,
which can favor the exclusion block. And where the identical sentence appears in both the inclusion
list and the exclusion list of an unpriced scope block, there is no answer to pick: record both and
route it upstream.

Revision habits. The governing fact is whether a new set arrived.

- Inside one solicitation the base is treated as fixed and each round is a negotiation instrument.
  Most same-bidder revisions move no money at all. The shapes seen: a round whose only content is an
  added breakout line; a round that bolts on exactly one priced alternate and changes nothing else;
  a round that corrects a malformed printed number; and a round that restates the basis without
  repricing.
- The revision that adds a breakout is the informative one, and it is elicited rather than
  volunteered. The estimator asks by email after the bids arrive and gets the insulation figure back
  as a sentence in a reply or as a reissued proposal with one line added and no price change. A
  leveling sheet carries the note asking for the breakout in one round and a large adjustment in its
  place in the next, with the bidder moving up the ranking. The breakout is the estimator buying
  comparability rather than a price, and where the driver is not comparability at all it can be a
  funding requirement.
- The base moves when a new set arrives, when a whole labor market changes, or when a scope class
  leaves or enters. Movements at a new set run from a couple of percent to about a quarter, in both
  directions. The large unexplained fall, a quarter off a base with no scope change, no stated reason
  and an unchanged letterhead date, does occur and is not the norm.
- A reason is documented where the general contractor asks and almost never where it does not. Asked
  what was behind a delta, a bidder names material price increases, revised unit layouts and assembly
  changes, and column wraps and amenity ceilings now included because they were not clearly
  identified in the earlier set, and the stated increase reconciles to the difference exactly.
- A revision may quietly shrink the base scope and re-offer the removed block as an alternate. The
  base falls and reads as a discount, when a whole partition class left the package. Only the
  alternate list reveals it. The mirror shape is worse: two named products drop a grade into the base
  and reappear as adds while the total falls.
- The dangerous direction is an inclusion leaving a scope sentence while the price rises. A package
  sentence naming metal framing, board, insulation and tape in one round names metal framing, board
  and tape in the next; a fire caulk inclusion line is simply absent; no exclusion names either
  departure, no alternate re-offers either, and the price rises by about a third, so the movement
  reads as ordinary escalation. One word can do it: inserting "interior" into an exclusion sentence
  moved a whole exterior wall class into a supplier's package, and narrowing "walls" to "interior
  walls" in a breakout sentence changed what a stable figure covered by two fifths.
- A revision can move nothing visible at all. A price that does not move while two whole scope blocks
  enter the inclusion list, absorbed by scaling every scope row up in proportion and deleting the
  commercial lines, is caught only by an inclusion-list diff. A markup rate held at the same stated
  percentage while what it covers shrinks from taxes, insurance, overhead and profit to overhead and
  profit only is caught by no diff at all except the commercial-terms sentence.
- A revision may be a labor substitution rather than a price change, most often swapping open-shop
  tapers for union tapers and stating the delta.
- A revision is frequently not a document. A letter saying the new documents do not change the price
  is a complete revision, and one PDF often carries both the letter and the original estimate it
  confirms. Email answers are leveling inputs, not correspondence: a deduct for removing erection, a
  shaft-wall rate per square foot, an install rate that exists nowhere else, a crane deduct with its
  schedule value, a manhour count, and a whole value-engineering menu all arrive that way and are
  carried into leveling sheets as scope rows.
- Revision labels are unreliable. The filename label and the document's own revision number can be
  off by one; both rounds of a pair can carry the same date and the same proposal number; a bidder's
  labels can restart between rounds so a later round's R0 carries the earlier round's R1 figures; a
  revision label can have been written by the general contractor's own filing clerk inside the
  document; and one page of a proposal can carry a different date from the next. On one project five
  offers from one bidder share one letterhead date and one cited basis, so neither instrument orders
  the ladder and only the scope narrative and the numbers do.

Diff round over round on six axes, never on the total: the exclusion list, the scope sentences, the
inclusion list, the unit column, the commercial-terms sentence, and any disclosed labor-hour block. A
two-word change to a scope sentence can more than double a number while the total diff and the
exclusion diff both see nothing, and a descope can be visible only as a fall in a base laborer hour
count exactly equal to a new alternate's hours.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Two different documents get called the scope sheet, they are not the same instrument, and they are
not counted on the same basis, so check which one a row count refers to before believing it. The
sheet issued to bidders as a bid form runs long: from about a hundred and seventy numbered rows to
past three hundred, with about a hundred and fifty of them carrying substantive scope once the
commercial, schedule, safety, coordination and submittal blocks are set aside. Roughly one hundred
twenty to one hundred sixty scope lines is the right target for a full multifamily drywall package,
and it describes that instrument.

The leveling sheet the estimator actually ranks on is a different document and it is usually much
thinner. Counted the same way, in substantive rows, it runs from zero to about a hundred and fifty,
and more often than not it runs under fifty. One carries no scope definition at all: its
qualification block still reads write qualifications here and its exclusion block still reads write
exclusions here, above a three-column ranking. Sheet depth tracks how far into buyout the round is
and how much time the estimator had, and at bid stage in current practice it is falling. Treat the
row target as a specification for what a sheet should ask, not as a description of what sheets do.

The sheet changes between issues in both directions, and the change is the signal. It grows through
leveling and is pruned at buyout, roughly doubling into the first buyout pass and falling back at the
second, and it resets when a project is rebid. Those buyout figures are the sheet's own total row
count rather than a count of substantive rows, which is why they run past the substantive ceiling
above: on one project the same sheet goes from about a hundred and twenty rows at bid stage to more
than two hundred and sixty at first buyout and under a hundred and eighty at the second, while its
bidder pool falls from fifteen columns to three. Elsewhere it grew about a sixth over seven months,
and every added row was a gap an earlier answer exposed: lites inside doors, a heated plenum at the
garage ceiling, an insulation board at one detail, impact resistant board at corners, painting of
patch work. It also shrinks: two wall-extent
rows folded into notes on other rows, which stops the question being put to every bidder; a row
reduced from a priced plug to the words "with painter" with the money gone and no note recording the
routing; and a whole row set held stable while every answer, note and plug from the previous round is
dropped and one decided answer regresses to an open question. Where a sheet gains rows inside one
solicitation, every row after the insertion renumbers, so row numbers are not comparable between two
bidders on one project and a diff of omitted row numbers silently compares the wrong rows.

A thin sheet is a warning and not the diagnosis. The failure is the unentered plug. One sheet with
every row this section asks for records in its own cells that one bidder excludes insulation and that
another bidder's insulation content is worth a fifth of its total, and still enters zero adjustment
against all six bidders. Another sheet, one of the thinnest seen, carries a single large
adjustment and produces the tightest spread. Rows are necessary and not sufficient.

### The partition-type question

The received idea is that drywall subcontractors price all interior partitions per the type schedule
as nearly one answer, so a scope list should not enumerate partition types. That is half right, and
the half that survives has a different reason than it used to.

The pricing half holds for most of the market. Most proposals, in every shape, point at the partition
schedule rather than reproducing it. The house form writes "per partition schedule" in every quantity
cell. The narrative form writes "all interior LGMF walls according to wall types". The spec-section
form names 092116 and stops. The one place a wall type earns its own row in those proposals is where
a specific detail is contested.

The enumeration half is wrong. Several unrelated bidders enumerate the schedule exhaustively and
unprompted, and some of them price it that way. One prices twenty-five named wall types and six named
ceiling types individually, each with its own measured area, board layer count, insulation product
and thickness, waste factor, labor crew rate and material rate per component, and was ranked first of
six on normalized cost. One prices twenty-one wall marks and four ceiling marks the same way behind a
two-line letter. One writes twelve framing lines each naming its partition marks with stud depth,
gauge and spacing, then lists twenty-four marks that receive sheathing. Others measure by mark and
price a lump: a component takeoff enumerating every mark with runners, studs, board in sheets, batt,
bridging and sealant beneath it; seventy pages of measured takeoff by mark and unit type; sixteen
pages of on-screen takeoff over the architect's own sheets.

The variable is the bidder's own estimating system, not the project and not the building type. System
output forms enumerate and letterhead narratives point at the schedule, both shapes are in the market
at once, and the estimator sees whichever the bidder's software prints. The same bidder that
enumerated twenty-five wall types on one project wrote a four-line narrative with no quantities on
two others. So:

- A sheet asking for a price per partition type is answerable by a minority and unanswerable by most
  of a bid list, which is why the schedule still collapses for pricing.
- A sheet asking for a quantity per partition type is answerable by more of the market than expected,
  and worth asking for. Measuring by type and pricing by type are separate behaviors.
- Where a bidder does publish a per-type takeoff, read it for ownership rather than for price. It is
  the only place the framing boundary becomes readable line by line, and the answer is not constant
  within a wall family: in one such takeoff some marks carry board, insulation, vapor barrier,
  resilient channel and tape with no studs at all, meaning the framing is someone else's; some carry
  metal stud with top and bottom track alongside the board; and one carries wood studs and double
  wood plates at the same linear foot rate the bidder uses for metal.

It remains true that what varies bidder to bidder is rarely which partition types are included. It is
whether the framing of that family is in this package at all, whether the framing is theirs or the
carpenter's or a panel supplier's, whether the insulation inside the partition is theirs, whether the
blocking inside it is theirs, what level of finish each face receives, whether the head-of-wall and
base-of-wall sealant and firestop are theirs, and whether the partition runs to deck or stops at the
ceiling. Those seven questions, asked once per wall family rather than once per wall type, are the
correct grain. A wall family here means a group that shares a construction and an ownership answer:
interior metal-framed non-load-bearing partitions, load-bearing metal-framed walls, wood-framed unit
and demising partitions, corridor and demising walls, shaft walls, exterior cold-formed backup walls,
and furred walls at existing or masonry construction.

The seven questions are necessary and not sufficient, and they are a residential podium list. On
office work the ownership questions that decide the money are which of acoustical ceiling, rough
carpentry and door and frame install sit inside the package, and the deck height. On a multi-building
job they are whether insulation is in, whether painting is in, and whether the garage and clubhouse
ceilings are in. Ask the seven, then ask what else this building type moves.

So: collapse the partition schedule for pricing, ask for its quantities, and expand the ownership
questions.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- The commercial frame, before any physical scope: base bid basis, whether an owner-controlled
  insurance program is assumed, warranty duration, bond, sales tax and tax-exempt status, project
  portal fees, and whether the price assumes union, open shop, prevailing wage or a stated mix. Where
  an issued sheet exists it opens this way, and these rows decide more of the ranking than most of
  the physical ones. Many leveling sheets carry none of it, which is a defect in those sheets.
- Compliance and workforce, as its own block on any publicly funded or institutional project: a
  residents jobs policy with stated workforce hour goals, minority, women-owned, disadvantaged and
  veteran-owned business participation for the bidder and its subcontractor tiers, an experience
  modification rate, criminal and sex-offender background checks on the crew with verification costs,
  domestic content, and environmental product declarations. These select the bid list before price
  does.
- Insulation, broken by material and by location, not as one row. Batt, mineral wool, spray foam,
  blown, spray-applied cementitious, rigid, insulated sheathing, vapor retarder, thermal break,
  fireblocking, and separately for ceilings and for walls. Bidders carry some and exclude others in
  the same sentence, and this block is the largest single source of incomparability in the trade.
- Level of finish, as its own block, by area and condition: exposed to view, concealed and plenum,
  above acoustical ceilings at rated walls, above acoustical ceilings at sound-rated walls,
  substrates receiving tile, surfaces receiving wall covering, amenity and critical-light space. Add
  the triggers a specification actually uses, because bidders cite them: feature walls and partitions
  at or above a stated continuous unbroken length, and surfaces scheduled to receive paint at or
  above a stated gloss level. Add moisture, which drives Level 5 as often as wall covering does, at
  garage ceilings and at moisture-resistant panels. Carry a Level 3 alternate row, because general
  contractors ask for one. And note that two bidders bound Level 4 by height above finished floor
  rather than by area or condition, which no finish-level model anticipates.
- Wall family, per the argument above, with the seven ownership questions attached.
- Shaft walls as their own line, separate from partitions, because the assembly, the studs and the
  finish level all differ and because whether the shaft extends through the floor-ceiling assembly is
  a structural question a bidder will qualify. Unlike partitions, shaft walls need their marks
  enumerated where the assembly changes hands: one bidder assigned seven named gypsum shaft-wall
  marks, two shaft assemblies encasing wood partitions above the podium and a named drawing to the
  rough carpenter, and excluded horizontal shaft assemblies at a named detail. One shaft-wall row
  cannot hold that.
- Ceiling assemblies, detail by detail. This is the part of a drywall scope sheet that legitimately
  runs long: draft stops, cove lighting framing and plywood, perimeter lighting, preformed curved
  ceilings, garage ceilings, window shade pockets, drapery pockets, rounded ceilings, sound isolation
  hangers, spring isolators, resilient channel versus sound clips. Each is answered differently by
  different bidders and several are answered No by bidders who answer Yes to everything else. On the
  one bidder workbook that decomposes the package, ceilings are more than half the direct cost and
  interior partitions about a sixteenth, so this is where the money is.
- Blocking, broken by what it supports and by which walls it sits in: bath accessories and grab bars,
  casework and shelving, mirrors and artwork, televisions and brackets, window treatments, mailboxes,
  handrails and railings at ramps, elevator door jambs, smoke curtain housings, kitchen islands and
  countertop brackets, and roof and curb blocking. Almost every bidder qualifies blocking to its own
  framing, so a single blocking row will always be ambiguous. Where a panelised system builds the
  walls, blocking splits three ways: metal blocking as shown to the panelizer, all wood blocking to
  nobody, and blocking for future accessible units to nobody.
- Sealant and firestop, broken by condition: head of wall, base of wall, drywall to drywall joints,
  at windows, at exterior doors, slab edge, acoustical at demising and corridor perimeters, and MEP
  penetrations. The last one is a separate row because it is excluded from every base bid seen. Add a
  row for exposed caulking, which is excluded by name as a category distinct from firestop and
  acoustic sealant.
- Air sealing and compartmentalization as its own block on any project with an envelope performance
  requirement, which is now most of them. The densest real sheet runs it to nine rows, each cited to
  a sheet or detail: picture-framing all studs wood and metal, air seal between board and studs and
  between stud and sheathing around all openings, air sealing between studs at sill and top plates,
  at trusses, at rim joists and blocking, at shafts, at typical walls, and a per-residential-unit
  compartmentalization rate. One block is not enough.
- Expansion joints, which one real sheet runs to eight rows: furnish separately from install,
  vertical separately from horizontal, at a fire wall door sill, at jambs and head, two named gasket
  products, and the blocking, framing, shaft liner and insulation behind them.
- Grade floors, each citing the specification paragraph that sets them and each carrying a priced add
  and a priced deduct: the premium resilient channel with its no-substitutions note, and the stud
  gauge at non-load-bearing walls. This is the highest-value row shape in the trade, because it
  converts a silent product downgrade into a number, and both sides of the market now price it.
- Installs, each with a count: hollow metal welded frames, hollow metal knock-down frames, doors and
  hardware, access panels flanged and taped-in, fire extinguisher cabinets, corner guards, expansion
  joint covers. Every issued sheet carries these as counted rows with a unit price, and the unit
  prices seen for access panels alone span more than a fivefold range, so the count and the rate both
  have to be on the sheet.
- Window and balcony-door installation, which is a drywall alternate on some projects at more than a
  tenth of the base bid and appears on no drawing as drywall scope.
- Specialty finishes by mark: acoustical gypsum board, felt panels, fabric-wrapped and acoustic
  panels, FRP, wall protection, abuse-resistant and impact-resistant board, and a light textured
  finish where a drawing or an owner's exhibit calls for one on a project whose bidders otherwise
  exclude textured finishes by name. Answered No by most bidders and carried by a few, which is the
  definition of a row that must exist.
- Mock-ups, split by kind: an interior in-place mock-up, an apartment unit mock-up named as the
  instrument that establishes the acceptable level of finish, a visual exterior mock-up, and an
  exterior performance mock-up. One proposal carries the first three and excludes the fourth, three
  bidders exclude mock-ups by name, and the obligation is sometimes in the owner's clarifications
  exhibit rather than in the drywall documents.
- Exterior and roof-level work, on any building with a metal-framed portion: balconies, canopies,
  bump-outs and soffits, parapets, hung parapets, penthouse framing, decking, coverboard, curbs and
  pipe portals. Bidders split sharply here and several exclude the lot.
- The existing-conditions block on renovation and conversion work: patching and repair of existing,
  furring and boarding the far side of existing-to-remain construction, wrapping existing plaster
  columns and beams, topping off existing walls and columns, infilling existing shafts, and prep of
  existing surfaces to receive new finishes. On one project a single alternate for wrapping existing
  beams was worth more than a third of the base bid and on another about a seventh with its plywood
  leg adding a fourteenth on top, so this block is not a detail. Section 3's gap list carries the rest
  of it.
- Hoisting, staging, loading and cleanup, as its own block, and specifically whether the labor doing
  the loading and cleanup is union and who supplies it.
- Patching and go-backs, with an explicit stated basis, per section 6.
- The bidder's assumed stud deflection limit, varied by what the wall receives. One bidder states the
  loosest limit for paint, tighter for tile and tightest for stone. It decides what stud a bidder
  buys, it is invisible in any total, and no other proposal states it.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual partition types, for pricing. Collapse to wall family, and ask for quantities by mark
  separately.
- Room by room and unit by unit. No document seen prices a single room, and unit-type variation shows
  up only as a total count.
- Board manufacturer, corner bead, trim, control joint accessories, mesh, tape, compound, screws and
  fasteners. These are attributes of a line. Where a bidder mentions corner bead or tear-away bead at
  all it is as a note under the board table, never as a priced item.
- Level 4 areas within one finish region. The finish level is the enumeration axis, not the surface.
- Ceilings, soffits, fur-downs and bulkheads within one area and one assembly. They collapse to one
  line unless the assembly or the isolation detail changes, in which case the detail is the line.
- Stud gauge and spacing within one wall family, unless the schedule itself changes them, in which
  case the wall family has already split.

Layer count is the exception that used to sit in this list. It is an attribute of a line most of the
time and a priced item often enough to ask: a second layer of sheathing at load-bearing walls quoted
as a rate per square foot, a sheathing adder as a separate purchasable line, one or two layers to
deck as a stated variable, abuse and impact resistant board carried outer layer only, resilient
channel split out as its own priced element at about a ninth of a total, and an estimator's own model
carrying a credit for one deleted board layer at the exterior walls against an added layer at
demising. Where the layering is what a substitution changes, it is a line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, carry it
in part, or be silent about it, and those outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers.
Applied to drywall, the test fires hardest on ownership rather than on geometry: on every line in
section 5, on the insulation block, and on the counted installs. It fires weakly on partition
geometry, which is why the type schedule collapses for pricing.

The fourth outcome, carried in part, is real and the three-outcome form misses it. One bidder carries
one coat of tape at penetrations in rated and sound walls and excludes the sealant, so the
penetration is neither included nor excluded but split by operation. One carries the framing for cove
and perimeter lighting with the lighting excluded. One carries a recessed television niche for
framing and blocking only. A partial answer must name the operation boundary, not the geometry.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. Insulation is the clearest case. A proposal that
says nothing about insulation on a project whose wall types carry it is not including it, and a
proposal that says nothing about blocking in wood walls almost certainly means metal walls only.
Silence in this trade defaults toward exclusion far more reliably than in trades where the package
boundary is obvious, and estimators enact that rule: one deducts each bidder's own stated insulation
value where one exists and enters nothing for the bidder that was silent.

### The structural gap list

Scope that no bidder carries, and that has to be added to every number before comparing. This is the
plug list, and a scope line set that names these explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging in
advance. It is an instruction and not a description of practice: most leveling sheets enter one
plug class at most, and several enter none.

Standing, on any building type:

- Firestopping, sealing and acoustical sealing of MEP penetrations. Excluded from every base bid
  seen, on every project, by every bidder. The most reliable gap in the trade, and it is no longer
  unpriceable: two competitors on one project carry it as a named priced alternate within about five
  percent of each other, at roughly one to one and a half percent of a drywall base, and it can be
  bought back rather than plugged.
- Interior non-load-bearing partition framing. Which package builds it is unresolved at leveling on
  most projects, and the estimator's own cell against that row is frequently a question mark or an
  instruction to ask. See section 5.
- The insulation block, by material and by location. The largest plug and the only one most sheets
  enter. Note the direction runs both ways: subtracting what a broad bidder carried is the same
  arithmetic as adding what a narrow bidder omitted, and one estimator normalizes downward across a
  whole sheet.
- Counted installs at a rate: hollow metal frames, access panels flanged and taped-in, fire
  extinguisher cabinets. No schedule exists at bid time, so the bidder authors a count and the
  estimator authors another. Two independent counts is normal and three occurs.
- Patching, repair and go-backs, with the basis the building dictates. Excluded on renovation by the
  drywall bidder and by the painter both.
- Air sealing and compartmentalization, on any envelope-performance project. Carried in a base by
  some, priced as an alternate by others, and plugged per residential unit by the estimator against
  every column including the bidder that states it is already included.
- Grade floors: the premium resilient channel, and the stud gauge at non-load-bearing walls. A
  twenty-gauge equivalent stud can be about a third thinner in base metal thickness than conventional
  twenty gauge.
- Level 5 at wall covering locations, and the wallcovering termination trims, fry reglets and corner
  trims that go with it. Excluded outright by a minority of drywall bidders and by the painter, so
  both can end up assigned to nobody.
- Blocking inside walls this trade did not frame.
- Garage, basement, retail and back-of-house ceilings.
- Spray, blown, rigid and spray-applied insulation, and the acoustic fiberglass, wherever a thermal
  insulation package exists to argue with.
- Slab-edge firestop.
- Textured finishes, wherever a drawing or an owner's exhibit calls for one.
- Expansion joints, and the blocking, framing, shaft liner and insulation behind them.
- The engineer's stamp on framing submittals, and delegated design for cold-formed framing.
- The uniformed fire watch for chop saws, the non-working steward, and the control lines. Labor and
  site obligations that attach to this trade's own work and land on the general contractor if nobody
  prices them. Stewards are owned by craft: a bidder can include the carpenter steward and exclude
  the laborer steward in the same document.
- The commercial adders: bond, general liability where there is no owner-controlled program, warranty
  term, sales tax, tariffs, and the project portal fee.
- Workforce and diversity participation goals, where a stated goal is a bid condition. One bidder
  prices a fifty percent participation goal at about four percent of its base.

On a conversion or any building with existing fabric to meet, add:

- Tin ceilings and the surface-mounted furring for them. Answered No by every bidder on one project,
  with the estimator plugging an allowance per building level onto every column.
- Existing plaster and lath, and patching of existing surfaces.
- The fire rating of an existing terra cotta ceiling system. The general contractor carries a
  separate allowance for it, above the trade rather than inside it.
- Topping off existing walls and columns.
- Furring to existing exterior walls, column enclosures, wrapping existing beams, and infill where
  construction was removed.
- Frame installation in concrete and masonry walls, excluded by name and materially large on an
  existing concrete-framed building.
- Seismic anchorage into existing masonry, with new blocking to match existing joist depth and pull
  tests of drilled and epoxied anchors. This lands in the drywall tab on one project while the
  structural package excludes the same two details by name, at a different count. It is a documented
  reassignment rather than a gap and therefore a live double-count risk.

Every item in that block is either excluded by every bidder that names it or carried by exactly one,
which is what makes each of them a plug rather than a preference.

On a building framed in load-bearing light gauge or prefabricated panels, add:

- Blocking and drywall nailers inside supplied panels, excluded by every panel supplier by name and
  assigned by one of them to the drywall contractor, while every drywall bidder bounds its blocking
  to what is shown.
- Extra studs where board spacing needs them. One supplier states in writing that it will not
  guarantee stud locations suit board spacing.
- Backer rod and caulking between panels, and the panel-to-panel joint itself, excluded by every
  supplier and claimed by nobody.
- Sheathing joint and penetration treatment, staggered joints between floors and panels, patching
  over screw heads, and treatment around openings.
- Sheathing at slab edge, and edge-of-slab firestopping at load-bearing walls.
- Window blocking and flashing at panelised walls.
- Insulation inside box headers and tubes in load-bearing walls.
- Panel attachment fasteners and anchors of every named class.
- Temporary support for deck flutes cut before the concrete pour.
- Power supply to the crane, and the crane itself.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Repeat bidders reproduce the same exclusion block on every project, and they make up a large share of
any drywall bid list, so the presence of a line carries no project information. Unrelated competitors
share proposal forms, terms and exclusion wording down to the perimeter clearance clause, the
snow-depth line, the seven-day material validity, the retainage term and an identical alternate title
for the same plug, and their prices for that plug land within about five percent of each other. Two
unrelated bidders in a structural pool carry a word-for-word identical non-obligation sentence with
only the company name changed. One bidder quotes the identical blended change-order hourly rate on
two projects two years apart, unescalated. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

Boilerplate can imitate project signal, and this is the trap. One bidder's hoisting exclusion names
the three specific floors a forklift can reach, and the identical parenthetical naming the same three
floors appears on its proposal for an unrelated building of a different height. A reader who treats a
specific floor list as evidence that the bidder walked the site will be wrong. On the same form the
labor-zone field does change between projects, so one field on one page carries project signal and
its neighbor does not.

Boilerplate also contradicts itself, and it does so identically across projects. One bidder's
inclusion bullet claims sealants and firestopping at the tops of walls and at penetrations while its
own exclusion block carries no sealants at drywall and no fire proofing, on four unrelated projects,
in the same words, against competitors who all exclude penetrations. That is a boilerplate
contradiction, not a project read, and it is a contested reading to route upstream rather than an
inclusion to level. The same bidder's blocking bullet answers the same question oppositely on its two
projects.

Two shapes that look like an alias and are not. A company that renames itself between two rounds of
one project, estimate number and scope otherwise unchanged, which any name-keyed ranking reads as two
bidders. And two bids on one project from two different companies a round apart, signed by the same
estimator and carrying the same numbered qualification and exclusion structure, the same phrasing and
the same spelling errors, because the estimator changed firms between rounds and took the template.
Leveling those two as independent market opinions is wrong.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- An exclusion pinned to a specific wall-type mark or detail number. When a bidder excludes the
  sandwiched plywood at one wall type, the soffit at one detail, a named ceiling hanger connection,
  interior wall bridging on a named sheet, or all board between three named wood-framed wall types
  with the work reassigned to the framer by name, it read the set and found something it will not do.
- A stated assumption about a condition the drawings do not fix. A bidder publishing its assumed deck
  heights floor by floor converts an invisible assumption into a checkable one. On a conversion this
  is the single most useful thing a drywall bidder can disclose, and it is becoming less rare. On a
  panelised job the analogue is a published crane type, duration, foundation bearing pressure and
  power supply, and both bidders who did it did it only because they were asked.
- A quantity or count that is not in the documents: numbers of door frames, access panels, tubs,
  skylights, columns to wrap, linear feet of beam to wrap, hollow metal frame counts written out in
  words and figures. These are the bidder's takeoff leaking through, and they are among the few real
  quantities you will get.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the documents, such as installs carried at zero because no
  door schedule, access panel schedule or window count exists in the set; closet-shelving blocking
  refused because the plans show none; every unit drop ceiling and soffit excluded because no
  reflected ceiling plans exist; FRP excluded because it is in the specifications and not on the
  drawings, or because it appears in no finish schedule at all. When a bidder refuses to price rather
  than guessing, that is a documents problem to fix, not a bidder to penalize. Each of those is a
  pre-written request for information.
- A scope line that discloses its own ambiguity while carrying the work. One bidder carries the
  corridors and writes in the same line that the assembly is not clear. That is priced work and an
  open question at once, and it is RFI-grade.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: an operating tenancy, a historic element, a
  podium level, a second building.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Labor type, stated as union, open shop, prevailing wage, or a named mix by operation. Among the
  largest drivers in the trade, and see sections 6 and 7.
- A geographic labor zone and a residential versus commercial rate classification, stated on the face
  of the proposal. Bidders on one project can state different zone numbers for the same town, and
  both zones are rate-setting.
- A continuous schedule without demobilization or remobilization, sometimes with the start and
  substantial completion months named. Phasing a job that was priced continuous is a change, and one
  bidder writes phasing into its exclusions outright.
- Site access for material handling: a stated perimeter clearance in feet for forklift and boom lift
  with the price subject to change if it is not provided, a dedicated hoist, a loading dock, or an
  open window on each floor to load full-height board through. Every bidder assumes one of these and
  several make it explicit. One loads through a window removed and replaced by others, which is a
  cost nobody else names.
- A delivery window, in one case between four and seven in the morning, for full-height stud and
  ten-foot board.
- Sequencing conditions written as scope: fire taping above ceilings completed before the mechanical
  and electrical rough; shaftwall extending continuously through the floor-ceiling assembly with the
  structure required to accommodate it; units released fully framed with the mechanical rough
  complete and inspected; floors released in stacked sequences; permanent lighting functioning before
  the final coat, which puts a critical-light finish condition in the electrician's hands.
- Welded hollow metal frames on site prior to framing, or the price changes. Two bidders on two
  projects state it. It is a gate on another package's delivery, and violating it converts framing
  into rework.
- Level of finish defaults, as set out in section 3. These are the bidder's interpretation of the
  specification and they vary. One bidder defines Level 4 against a named industry finish standard
  and states that no skim coat is included at Level 4.
- Joint compound type. Qualifying ready-mix or all-purpose compound in place of the specified
  setting-type compound is a market habit rather than a one-bidder note, seen from several bidders
  across projects. It reads as a minor note and it is a specification deviation.
- Stud gauge and product equivalence. Bidders substitute equivalent-gauge products, offer a board
  brand as an equal on the strength of a UL listing, and swap resilient channel grades. The most
  checkable form states both thicknesses: a twenty-gauge equivalent about a third thinner in base
  metal thickness than conventional twenty gauge. Each of these is priced as a deduct or an add
  somewhere, and both sides of the market now price the two named grade floors.
- A specified installation method refused by note number: laminating drywall with joint compound and
  shooting drywall onto masonry, excluded by citing the partition general note that specifies it.
- Material escalation, either disclaimed onto the owner or evidenced by an attached supplier letter
  announcing a double-digit steel increase with quotes valid for a month. Steel stud pricing moves,
  and proposal validity periods in this trade run as short as seven days for that reason, with
  fifteen days common on supply quotes.
- Tax-exempt status, where the bidder assumes taxable and states that the line comes out if the owner
  is exempt.
- Whether itemized line prices are commitments. A common disclaimer says line prices are for
  reference only and are void if the whole scope is not awarded, which means the breakout you are
  leveling on is not a unit price.
- Bonding capacity as an eliminating gate. One bidder discloses a single-project bonding limit below
  its own base bid, with a total bonded aggregate under twice it, and offers to raise capacity if the
  project is split into phase-based contracts. No leveling sheet has a row for it.
- A discount conditional on a multi-trade award across several packages, which makes any
  single-package ranking conditional in a way a qualification cannot express.
- Conditional inclusions: scope lines qualified as "if specified" or "if included". They read as
  included to anyone skimming and commit to nothing.
- Excluded-with-a-standing-offer: building envelope insulation excluded with the note that pricing is
  available on request. Neither an exclusion nor an alternate.
- An exclusion withdrawn by email at no cost. A bidder excludes Level 5 in writing, is asked what it
  is worth, and answers that the plans were not specific and to consider it included as long as it is
  not extensive. The leveling sheet can still carry the printed exclusion and a plug against it, so
  the concession never reaches the ranking and the bidder is penalized for scope it has given away.
- A requirement placed on the general contractor as a quality condition rather than a convenience:
  electricity and heat furnished throughout the process.
- Bond rates. Most bid lists cluster between about one percent and one and a half percent of
  contract, and one bid list spans about four to one from well under one percent to two and a half,
  with a verbal quote elsewhere at three to four. What actually defeats a single plugged rate is not
  the market range but that the rate moves with contract size inside one bidder's own pricing: one
  bidder quotes about one percent on the larger building and about one and four tenths on the smaller
  in one document. Carry each bidder's stated rate.
- The standard of acceptance for taping. One scope sheet requires the drywall contractor to correct
  work and taping until approved by the painter, which puts the acceptance test in another trade's
  hands rather than in a finish level.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This trade is defined more by its seams than by its materials. Every one below is genuinely contested,
not merely adjacent.

### The framing seam, which matters most

On any podium or wood-framed multifamily building the package splits along a line that is drawn
badly or not at all. The framing carpenter builds the structural wood walls, and the drywall
contractor boards and finishes them. The drywall contractor builds the metal framing at the podium,
the shafts, the soffits and the ceilings. Everything the drywall contractor qualifies flows from that
line: blocking is its own framing only, sheathing is at cold-formed framing only, window and door
returns are inside metal walls only, and any shimming or added framing at rough openings the
carpenter framed is excluded.

Three separate things get collapsed into the idea that the drawings do not draw the line, and they
need different handling.

- Where the drawings do address it, the disciplines can disagree. One bidder writes that the
  architectural drawings show all first-floor units as podium units while the structural drawings
  show the majority of those units within the wood frame, and states that its delineation is taken
  from the structural drawings. A second writes that the interior wood-framed walls are determined by
  the architectural wall types and the bearing walls shown on the structural narrative plans. A third
  reads the regime off the set and states it floor by floor. Which discipline a bidder trusts decides
  what package it priced. That is a documents conflict to route upstream, not a leveling judgment,
  and it is the highest-value thing a drywall read can find on a podium building.
- Where the drawings are silent about ownership rather than about construction, the line follows the
  buyout rather than the construction. One bidder confirmed in writing that the non-bearing metal
  partitions would simply move into its rough framing proposal if the drywall package went elsewhere.
  Another carried the wood-framed-area non-bearing partitions as wood inside its drywall package. A
  third had the general contractor instruct that the base price include metal in lieu of wood. The
  same company can hold both sides of the seam in two proposals filed under one trade token.
- Where the structure is light gauge, the counterparty is not a carpenter at all. See section 1.

Non-load-bearing interior partitions are the contested zone and both trades bid them. What the market
does not have is a settled price for that, and the received idea that it does is wrong. Where the
alternate names one tightly defined wall class, two bidders can land within about three percent of
each other. Where the question is asked open, four bidders answer four ways: one includes it and
discloses a material and labor split, two exclude it and price adds that differ from each other by
about two and a half times, and a fourth prices its base assuming those walls are wood and by others.
Four bidders answering one clearly defined top-floor descope alternate still spread about two and a
half to one. Those are four different packages under one name, and the spread is a definition failure
rather than a market price.

Read the carpenter's side before leveling either. The carpenter's alternate for non-load-bearing wood
framed walls states that its carried spacing is twenty-four inches on center and that this produces a
reduced value against a typical sixteen-inch-on-center metal assembly, which by itself makes the two
alternates non-comparable on their face. That same alternate draws a finer boundary than a drywall
sheet usually carries: it covers unit interior partitions and wood-framed shaft walls only, structural
walls meaning demising and corridor walls are outside it, and all blocking associated with the
non-load-bearing walls is inside the same breakout.

The estimator asserts an assignment and the bidders do not follow it. On one framing package's own
sheet the notes read "assume by drywall" and "not in contract, by the drywall subcontractor" while
the drywall sheet in the same workbook leaves the same question open, and it is still open on that
project's next round two years later. On another the sheet row carries six different answers from six
bidders: two explicit instructions to ask, one phone confirmation, one cross-package inference from
the same bidder's own carpentry quote, one estimator valuation, and one bidder simply assuming wood.
Under a light gauge regime an estimator's own model carries a stated fraction of the non-bearing
walls, about a third, because the system takes the rest. Get this decided before you level anything.
The base bid of a bidder who assumed wood framing and the base bid of one who assumed metal are not
the same package.

Beware also that a proposal for the wrong trade arrives under this token, and it is not an oddity. A
wood framing proposal, a light gauge panel erection quote, a complete structural steel and tower crane
and cladding and window package, a plaster and restoration package, and a rough carpentry package all
arrive filed under drywall. Trust the document, not the folder.

### Painting

Painting's boundary with drywall is contested in both directions, and it can collapse entirely.

- Preparation and skim coating. Painters disclaim responsibility for drywall quality and exclude wall
  patching, skim coating and excessive prep. Drywall bidders variously carry patching as an allowance,
  price it at a stated rate, or exclude patching of existing walls entirely. On a renovation, patching
  of existing is excluded by the drywall bidder and by the painter both, which makes it an unassigned
  gap.
- Painting of patch work. At least one drywall bidder prices not only the patching but the painting
  of its own patch work, at roughly half its patching rate. That work is also inside the painter's
  scope on the same project, so it can be bought twice.
- The seam can collapse. One base bid carries one coat of a named primer and sealer on all smooth
  walls, then sanded. One estimate carries interior painting as a second priced line. One drywall
  proposal prices painting at more than a third of a building's total. That is not a seam, it is a
  package boundary that moved, and any sheet that ranks it against drywall-only bids ranks two
  different scopes.
- Level 5 at wall covering locations. This is a drywall row on every issued sheet, and it is the
  finish the wallcovering installer's warranty depends on. Some drywall bidders exclude Level 5
  outright.
- Wallcovering termination trims, fry reglets, outside corner trims and inside corner trims. The
  painting side records these as excluded by the painter and belonging to drywall, installed before
  the wallcovering. From this side they are excluded by name and detail number in one round and
  priced as a trim alternate in the next, and on one project excluded by the drywall bidder outright,
  which leaves them assigned to nobody. Put them on their own line.
- FRP, textile, felt and acoustic panels. The painting side records the estimator assigning these to
  drywall. From this side, some drywall bidders carry FRP by manufacturer and product code in the
  base, one carries acoustic panels in named rooms, and others exclude wall protection and fabric
  panels wholesale, while a printed exclusion block can exclude FRP on one page while the pricing
  table carries it on another. On some bid lists every bidder excludes it and the estimator's own
  cell is a question mark.
- A cross-package deletion can delete drywall scope with no drywall adjustment. A painting-sheet
  deduct removing the wall covering also removes the reason the Level 5 finish and the wallcovering
  trims exist, and no drywall column moves.
- Acceptance. One scope sheet makes the drywall contractor correct its taping until the painter
  approves it, which is the only place in either trade where the seam is written as an acceptance
  test rather than a scope split.

### Acoustical ceilings

On multifamily, acoustical ceiling tile is bid as its own package with its own bid list, and the
specialists who bid it quantify by mark and by square foot with named grid systems, which is nothing
like the drywall shape. Drywall bidders price it too: some carry the complete system in the base under
the ceiling spec section, some carry it as a priced alternate, and most exclude it while still
carrying the framing, blocking, pockets and access-panel cuts around it.

Outside multifamily the package boundary moves. On office core and shell most bidders carry the tile
inside the drywall number, twice as its own priced line and once with named grid and tile products and
attic stock. On a theatre both bidders carry a named acoustical ceiling system in the base and the
leveling sheet lists suspension systems and integrated ceiling assemblies as this package's sections.
On a healthcare renovation a bidder carries tile at a stated material rate and a stated labor rate
inside the drywall bid. On those types the boundary is set by an answer to a request for information,
and one such answer routed integrated ceiling assemblies out of the ceilings package entirely.

Three consequences hold everywhere. Tile is a boundary to declare, not a scope to assume. Drywall's
grid, hangers, blocking and shade-pocket framing stay with drywall even when the tile does not, so do
not delete the ceiling rows when you move the tile: one bidder prices the tile as one alternate and
the drywall pre-rock behind it as a second alternate of comparable size, which is that rule made
concrete. And a swap from acoustical tile to gypsum board in a corridor is priced as an add for
drywall rather than as a delta from the ceiling package, so level it as two separate scopes.

### Other seams

- Panelised structure, where the drywall package meets a manufactured system. The panel-to-panel
  joint, sheathing joint and penetration treatment, drywall nailers inside supplied panels, window
  installation whose price depends on whether the window goes in at the factory or on the building,
  temporary support for deck flutes, engineered control lines, and the crane. Section 3's gap list
  carries the full set. The same windows cost about two thirds as much per unit installed in the
  factory inside a bearing panel as in a mixed factory-and-field arrangement, and several times more
  again installed in the field by a different bidder. No drawing says where any of it happens.
- Hollow metal frames, doors and hardware. Frames are furnished by the door supplier and installed by
  drywall, nearly always, and usually bounded to metal-framed walls. The bound varies: by substrate,
  excluded at concrete and masonry openings; by floor, first floor only; by corridor only. Doors and
  hardware are excluded by most and offered back as a counted alternate by some, and on conversions
  carried outright by a minority. Frame grouting or stuffing is excluded by name. Split jamb interior
  unit frames go to another trade, and unit entry knock-down frames can go to finish carpentry.
- Access panels. Material by others, install by drywall, and the price depends on type: taped-in
  panels cost more than flanged and are quoted as an upcharge per panel, running about half again to
  about twice the flanged rate. Bidders carry allowance counts because no schedule exists at bid
  time, and the estimator carries its own count and rate. Two independent counts of the same panels
  is normal and three occurs, with the outlier about a quarter below the other two.
- MEP penetration firestopping. Excluded from every base bid seen. Drywall seals the head and base of
  the walls it built and stops, and one bidder answers the row with the words "head and base only,
  penetrations by others", which is the whole seam in six words. Whether anyone else picked it up is
  a question worth asking on every project, and on one project the general contractor's own answer to
  the owner assigns it to the mechanical, electrical and plumbing trades. It is now purchasable as a
  named alternate on both sides, as an add for all wire and pipe penetrations or as a deduct against
  a base that carries top-of-wall and slab-edge firestop. One bidder splits it by operation, taping
  the penetration and excluding the sealant.
- Insulation. It splits four ways in practice: batt and mineral wool with drywall; spray and blown to
  a specialty applicator, though a minority carry closed-cell and spray-applied foam inside the
  drywall base; rigid and thermal-break insulation with the envelope, except where the rigid sits
  behind board and then it goes with the board; and stud-pack, jamb and header insulation
  factory-installed by a panel supplier. Structural thermal break is excluded by everyone. A bidder
  can carry batt in the base and exclude all other insulation in the same document, and one splits it
  inside a single bid by carrying wall insulation and excluding all ceiling insulation. On one job the
  same spray foam scope sits in three places at once: a drywall base, a specialist's own tab, and a
  separate applicator's bid.
- The vapor retarder splits by side of the assembly rather than by trade: the interior side carried,
  the exterior side excluded, in one document. A smart vapor retarder under the interior board at
  exterior walls appears in several base bids.
- Gypsum floor underlayment. Bid as its own trade, and also quoted inside drywall proposals by the
  companies that do both, with a stated number of pours and pre-pours and a remobilization fee per
  extra pour day. The double arrival is proven and not merely a warning: one document filed under both
  trade tokens carries a drywall block and an underlayment block under one base bid total while five
  specialist underlayment bids sit in the same project's folder. The framing regime also changes the
  underlayment: one thickness of gypcrete and sound mat under a wood frame, a thicker one with more
  mobilizations under a light gauge deck.
- Misc metals and structure. On renovation and roof-level work the drywall contractor installs
  decking and coverboard while the connection angles remain misc metals, and the drywall bidder will
  say so. On a panelised job the direction inverts and a drywall bidder furnishes and installs the
  composite floor deck and pour stop.
- Masonry. CMU partitions and elevator shafts are excluded by drywall and belong to the mason, and
  furring at masonry walls is a drywall row that at least one bidder offered to delete as a deduct.
- Tile. Taping at tile substrates is excluded by name by one bidder and assigned to the tile
  installer. No scope sheet asks who tapes them.
- Plaster and ornamental restoration, on a historic building. The dominant contested seam there. One
  bidder carries plaster walls at a named wall type inside its drywall bid, one excludes all plaster,
  plaster patching, repair and restoration and all patching, and one bids plaster and plaster systems
  as its own numbered proposal. Both drywall bidders also exclude the glass-fiber-reinforced
  ornamental work and the estimator asks for a deduct alternate to compare it against the finish
  carpentry package, so it is an open gap with a named alternative counterparty and no bidder.
- Acoustical sealant that must be done before or during wood framing leaves the drywall package by
  name, which splits the sealant scope on a sequence boundary rather than a location one.
- Labor jurisdiction over material handling. Not a scope seam but priced like one, and it appears on
  no drawing. The instruments vary: two priced union adds, one for cleanup only and one for cleanup
  and off-loading, differing from each other by about seventy percent to about a factor of two; two
  complete alternative base bids differing only in whether the laborers are union, about a twentieth
  apart; a whole-bid labor configuration; an adder per square foot per building zone; a handwritten
  deduct in a value-engineering call; and a steward carried or excluded by craft. The magnitude
  scales with access difficulty rather than contract size, from about a seventieth of a large
  downtown conversion to about a tenth or an eighth of a package elsewhere, and the larger add can be
  several times and up to about nine times the same bid list's penetration firestop alternate. A
  detail that decides who is even paying: on one project the loading laborers are supplied by the
  board supplier, not by the drywall subcontractor. A union grant can appear as a negative line
  between a proposal's total and its final total.
- Corner guards, fire extinguisher cabinets and expansion joint covers. Install-only, material by
  others, and carried by some bidders and excluded by others.
- Column enclosures install-only, curtainwall mullion mates inside the drywall package, and toilet
  accessory installation. Single-bidder seams that appear on conversions.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What drywall contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The partition type or wall type schedule. This is the primary document, and it is what every
   "per partition schedule" and "per wall types" reference points at. Stud depth, gauge, spacing,
   layer count, rating and insulation all live here. If the schedule is incomplete or the gauges are
   still delegated to a specialty engineer, the bid becomes a basis-of-design bid with a change order
   attached, and bidders say so. Where the drawings carry no wall tags at all, a bidder assigns every
   untagged wall to one type and says so.
2. The reflected ceiling plans and the ceiling assembly details. The second document, and the source
   of the long tail of ceiling rows in section 3. Bidders cite individual detail numbers here more
   than anywhere else in the set. Where there are none, the responses are not comparable at any
   price: one bidder excludes every unit drop ceiling and soffit by name for that reason and another
   reconstructs the whole ceiling scope from a horizontal assemblies sheet, mark by mark, level by
   level, room by room.
3. The interior assembly details and the fire rating sheets, for rating continuity, drywall lids and
   fire tops at the head of non-bearing walls, and rating maintenance behind recessed items. Whether
   a rating applies to a whole floor-and-ceiling assembly or to the ceiling membrane alone is
   resolved in writing by one bidder as a numbered qualification and addressed by no competitor.
4. The structural drawings, which are first-class wherever the framing regime is in play and not only
   on a podium. They and the architectural set can disagree about which floors are wood and which are
   not, and a bidder will say which one it followed. Structural bidders price off the structural
   framing plans and off their own marked-up versions of them.
5. Unit plans and the unit count, for repetition and for the per-unit rates that patching and
   go-backs are quoted in. The unit count itself is contested: bidders and general contractors
   disagree about it and one general contractor's own workbook uses a count that differs from the
   project record, which matters because leveling sheets normalize on it.
6. The finish schedule, but only for the marks that change the drywall scope: wall covering locations
   that require Level 5, tile locations that require backer and Level 2, FRP and wall protection
   marks, and specialty panel marks.
7. The door schedule and the access panel schedule, for the counted installs. Where these do not
   exist, bidders either carry an allowance count or refuse to price the line.
8. The specification, cited by section number more than by content, and used mainly to settle finish
   levels, board types, stud thickness and firestopping standards. On some projects there is no
   specification book at bid time at all, and a bidder derives its own section list from the
   drawings and says so.
9. The owner's own documents, which can carry drywall scope the drywall documents do not. An owner
   clarifications exhibit sets amenity drywall at Level 4, requires an apartment unit mock-up as the
   instrument establishing the acceptable level of finish, declares a named unit finish schedule to
   supersede all other documents for unit finishes, and grants a blanket basis-of-design substitution
   license. Bidders on that project carry Level 5 at amenity, against that exhibit.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff. Measured takeoffs exist and are more available than
they look, but the normal pricing method is wall family plus area plus assembly plus judgment.

A drawing set can also be unreadable in monochrome. One architect's title block warns that the
legibility of performance criteria and detailing scope requires the drawings be viewed or printed in
color, and suppliers define their own package boundaries by color-coded markups whose legend colors
are near-identical. A monochrome print of either the contract set or a bidder's own scope drawing
loses the package boundary.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- The framing regime and who owns the non-load-bearing partitions. On a building where the framing
  can leave the package this is the largest single variable, worth a factor of two on a bid.
- Labor basis. Among the largest single drivers, and the leveling sheets track it on every bidder,
  but it does not order the ranking and it must never be assumed to. Union bidders occupy both ends
  of a fifteen-column sheet; an open-shop bidder sits third of six above two union bidders; on one
  project the two union bidders rank last and on another the lowest bidder is union with two open
  shop bidders above it. On one project there is no overlap at all, three open shop below four union
  with a step of about a third between them, and that is a correct reading of that bid list and not a
  rule. The premium is real and, by one estimator's own cross-trade analysis, about a quarter for
  this trade, near the bottom of a range running to about two thirds across the other trades on the
  same tab. Scope completeness swamps it.
- The operation-by-operation labor mix, which is the reason the direction does not hold. The package
  is several labor markets at once, and a bidder can price layout, stud install, blocking, pre-rock
  and hanging at union rates while pricing fire taping, finish taping, patching and insulation open
  shop, and beat a pure open-shop bidder. This is documented, not inferred: one cost breakdown labels
  eleven operations individually, five at union rates and four at open shop, with the two ceiling
  operations union and patching and insulation open shop; another splits by craft and classification
  at once, carpenters and tapers at residential union rates and laborers at commercial rates; another
  states union carpentry wages, a union wood-frame taper rate and open shop laborers in one sentence.
  Moving tapers alone from union to open shop is worth nearly nine percent of a whole package. A
  leveling field with one labor value per bidder cannot represent any of it, every sheet has exactly
  one such field, and the values in it are demonstrably wrong: the same bidder's identical
  three-market sentence is recorded as open shop on one project and union on another by the same
  office; a bidder that states no labor type at all is recorded as union on one project and open shop
  on another; and one bidder's two complete base bids, one of them explicitly non-union, are both
  recorded as union in the same cell.
- The rate classification, commercial versus residential, and the geographic labor zone. What the
  multiplier is applied to decides its size. On a bare labor rate for the same two operations it runs
  on the order of two thirds, applied as one flat multiplier across operations rather than as an
  operation-specific difference. On a labor-and-material composite square foot rate it falls to about
  a seventh, because material does not change classification. On totals it does not reliably show at
  all: two bidders carrying opposite classifications land within about half a percent of each other.
- Prevailing wage applicability, and any project workforce or residency requirements. Do not assume
  its direction either: on one project the general contractor asks for a union residential carpenter
  rate and for deducts to prevailing wage for tapers and laborers, which makes prevailing wage the
  cheaper basis there, and a bidder that moved carpenters from prevailing wage up to a union rate cut
  its total by about a sixth in the same revision.
- The labor basis of the solicitation itself, which is a live negotiation and not a property of the
  drawings or the bidders. On one project the general contractor is union-signatory and is petitioning
  the union for relief on the drywall and ceilings packages on the grounds of the project size and of
  being the only signatory firm bidding, so which bidders are eligible is open while bids are being
  taken. On another the general contractor solicits at the residential rate, a bidder replies that it
  doubts that rate is achievable, and the estimator tells a colleague not to let that bidder spend
  much time on the bid.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program is in place. Bidders quote general liability as a
  percentage of contract to be added back if it is not, and the percentage varies. It can also be
  coupled to a scope alternate, so the insurance value changes depending on which labor alternate is
  taken and cannot be plugged at one figure.
- Bond requirement and rate, per section 4.
- Warranty duration. One year is the market default and two years is a priced add, sometimes with a
  matching deduct offered to go back down, and sometimes restructured by the general contractor's
  direction and complied with. Whole bid lists carry no warranty statement at all.
- Sales tax treatment and the owner's tax-exempt status. Some bidders include tax, some exclude it,
  and at least one applies it only to the material portion, which on that bid was under half the
  total. A supply quote's computed tax can imply a taxable base several percent below the proposal
  value while a competitor applies tax to its whole subtotal.
- Steel material escalation and the validity period of the price. Material validity in this trade
  runs as short as seven days, and supplier increase letters travel with the proposals.
- Lead time, which is not only a fabricate-to-order trade's axis. One bidder warns of long lead times
  for many gypsum products and for mineral wool.
- Hoisting, staging and material handling: whether there is a hoist, a dock, a crane, an open window
  on each floor, a perimeter clear enough for a boom lift, and whether the drywall contractor or the
  general contractor is providing it. A tower crane supplied by the general contractor in place of a
  bidder's mobile crane is worth a deduct of about three percent of the drywall package and three to
  four weeks of schedule, stated in writing. Also whether the laborers doing it are union and who
  supplies them.
- Schedule continuity and phasing. Bids are priced on a continuous schedule and demobilization is a
  change. One bidder prices the whole package by phase, which is the only way phasing becomes visible
  before it becomes a claim.
- Deck heights, on renovation and conversion. Whether an existing building's floor-to-deck dimensions
  match the assumption is worth a lot in stud and board. Publishing the assumption is becoming less
  rare: two bidders now do it on existing buildings, and on office work one bidder states a deck
  height on all three of its scopes as routine practice.
- The condition of what the demolition contractor leaves. One bidder assumes clean and straight
  edges, which is not a given.
- Whether patching and go-backs are expected of this trade, and on what basis.
- Compliance, which on current work is priced scope and a bid-list filter rather than paperwork. A
  city residents jobs policy with stated workforce hour goals for residents, minority workers and
  women; minority, women-owned, disadvantaged and veteran-owned business participation for the bidder
  and its subcontractor tiers, with a stated participation goal priced by one bidder at about four
  percent of base; an experience modification rate; criminal and sex-offender background checks on
  the crew with all verification costs included; a project portal fee stated as a fraction of a
  percent of contract value up to a cap plus a flat charge per subcontractor tier; environmental
  product declarations; a domestic content requirement asked and answered on a proposal face; and
  building information modelling participation with a record model. Most of a current bid list
  carries a business certification on the face of the proposal, on the letterhead, in the signature
  block, on the base-bid line, in the transmittal email, or as a certificate bundled into the PDF
  with granted and expiring dates, and some of those expiry dates fall inside the anticipated
  construction period. A leveling column header can carry the certification. Certification selects
  the bid list before price does.
- A building energy or green certification requirement, which reaches into drywall through the air
  sealing and compartmentalization block, because the compliance-carrying layer at the head of wall,
  at the slab edge and around every opening is drywall's. It appears as a priced package label, as a
  named sealant scope inside a base bid, as a two-word parenthetical that moves a price about five
  percent, as a named passive-building standard stated as included, and as a standing row on a
  general contractor's scope sheet. It is the reason the air-sealing block on the densest current
  sheet runs nine rows rather than one.
- Whether the drywall contractor is expected to produce coordination drawings, models, record
  drawings or as-builts. Answers split hard and the estimator plugs it at five figures against the
  bidder that excluded modelling.

Patching and go-backs deserve their own note, because it is the drywall analogue of the painter's
punch problem. A stated patching allowance is present in only a minority of proposals and is absent
from whole bid lists. The basis follows the building, not the trade. On multifamily it is expressed
per apartment in hours, and where a bidder states one it runs at roughly six hours per apartment for
patching and roughly three for painting that patch work; a second bidder on a different project
states the same six and three, prices both, and the two figures reconcile against its own hourly
rate. On a conversion the repeating unit is the floor and not the apartment, because patching follows
the existing structure rather than the unit fit-out, and the bases seen are a capped allowance, a
total man-day count, man-days per floor, a two-day allowance per floor, and hour allowances tied to a
named ceiling type on a named floor. On office work it is an allowance in man-days because there are
no apartments to divide by. On healthcare it is an allowance nested inside a phase price, in one
phase roughly seven tenths of that phase, or an eighty-hour allowance with no per-unit basis at all.
Any drywall scope line set should carry a patching and repair line with an explicit basis, should
name the denominator the building dictates, and should state whether painting of the patch is inside
it.

Set-stage behavior. At schematic and design development, bidders openly price around missing
information and say so: the framing is a basis-of-design carry pending delegated design, the installs
are carried at zero because no schedule exists, the blocking split is flagged as to be confirmed with
the general contractor, and one bidder tells the estimator outright to inflate its rates for a
schematic estimate. Treat those as honest rather than as gaps in the bid. By permit, construction-
document and conformed sets the same bidders switch to citing wall-type ranges, detail numbers and
counted quantities.

Reading the basis is harder than it looks and the failures are systematic. A proposal's stated basis
set and its folder's set stage disagree often: bids priced two rounds behind the folder appear on
several projects, and on more than one the bidder cited a set older than any other bidder on the same
job, in one case across three consecutive rounds. Also seen: a basis date five years earlier than any
set on the project; a bidder calling the design development set a permit set in two consecutive rounds
while citing the right date both times; a cited basis four weeks later than the set's own issue date;
a proposal citing a plan set dated after the proposal itself; a date written in day-month order so it
reads as a different month than a competitor's citation of the same set; and a bidder's own form
leaving every basis field blank so the only dated basis is in the covering emails. On one project four
dates are in circulation for what everyone calls one construction-document set, and the estimator's
own buyout sheet cites the drawings and the specifications three days apart. Read the date off the
document, treat the set-stage label as a claim, and where a cited basis is contradicted inside the
same file, let the correcting correspondence govern. Where two documents cite one basis and are days
apart, the cited basis orders nothing and only the letterhead date and the filename do.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet of board and of ceiling area, the underlying unit for almost everything.
- Linear feet of wall, for interior partitions and separately for shaft wall. The natural unit for
  framing, and the one a takeoff-style proposal reports. An estimator derives it the same way, wall
  family by wall family, as linear feet of wall times floor-to-floor height for single-side square
  feet, split into demising, corridor, elevator shaft and exterior.
- Square feet for insulation, quoted as a rate per square foot when a bidder is asked to justify the
  insulation block, and by product and thickness on a full rate card.
- Each, for the counted installs: hollow metal frames, access panels, fire extinguisher cabinets,
  corner guards, tubs receiving backer, skylights, columns to wrap, windows split by installation
  method. This is where unit prices actually live in this trade, and the dispersion is severe. Access
  panel install runs across more than a fivefold range between the cheapest flanged rate and the
  highest estimator plug.
- Linear feet, for existing beams to wrap on renovation, and for resilient channel.
- Hours, for change-order and time-and-material rates, quoted by craft and grade on some bid lists
  and by nobody on others.
- Crew hours by craft for the base bid, and hours against each alternate. New, and the reason is not
  estimating: a general contractor asking for hours by craft to pursue workforce participation
  funding gets them. This hands the trade a normalizing unit it has never had. Dividing a base bid by
  disclosed hours gives an all-in rate per manhour, and two independent bidders agree on that rate
  within a few percent while their totals differ by a fifth. Craft hours diverge as much as prices do
  and not in the same direction: one bidder low on carpenters and high on laborers, the other the
  reverse, spanning about one and seven tenths on carpenters and more than two on laborers. Where
  hours are published against alternates, the implied all-in rate runs from roughly the base-bid rate
  to about twice it, tracking material content rather than labor.
- Hours or man-days per apartment, per floor, or per phase, for patching and for painting of patch
  work, per section 6.
- Total manhours for the project, asked for on the scope sheet and answered rarely.
- Percent of contract, for bond, for general liability if there is no owner-controlled program, and
  for the project portal fee.
- Percent of direct cost, for a bidder's combined markup, and watch what it covers rather than its
  rate.
- Pours and pour-days, where floor underlayment is bundled, with a remobilization fee per extra day.
- Per residential unit, for air sealing and compartmentalization on an envelope-performance project.
- Square feet of exterior wall surface, distinct from square feet of board; square feet of gross
  building area as a whole-frame rate; linear feet of wall panel; per module per floor; square feet
  of floor area per week as a production basis; and pounds of the heaviest crane pick, all on a
  panelised job.
- Gross square feet, square feet of a defined framed area, and residential units, as the normalizing
  denominators.

That last point is worth stating plainly, and it is worse than a matter of judgment. There is no
single leveling currency in this trade, and the denominator is not stable inside one office, inside
one project, inside one workbook, or inside one sheet. The choice does follow the building loosely:
office conversions normalize on gross square feet, historic conversions and wood-framed podiums on
residential units. Beyond that it moves. One project carries four different areas across its
workbooks, two of them differing by about a quarter, so one bidder's rate per square foot in one
round is not comparable to the same bidder's rate in the next round of the same project. One workbook
normalizes the drywall tab per gross square foot and the thermal insulation tab beside it per
residential unit. One tab changes denominator mid-leveling and two tabs of one workbook end on two
different denominators, so rates are not comparable between two halves of one trade family on one
project. Two copies of one sheet carrying the same date, the same bidders and the same bids use gross
areas differing by about a seventh, and the copy whose filename says it is the memorialized one is not
the one that matches the working file. And a three-phase total gets normalized on the gross square
feet of one phase. Name the denominator's quantity source, check it against the workbook's own project
data, and never carry a rate across rounds without checking that the divisor did not move.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Whether the load-bearing framing is inside the package. Where the building type allows it to leave,
   this moves a number by a factor of two and nothing else comes close.
2. Whether insulation is inside the package. The largest adjustment wherever the framing cannot
   leave the package, which is most jobs, and the one most sheets actually enter.
3. Labor basis and the operation-by-operation mix of union, open-shop and prevailing-wage crews. On
   one conversion the estimator's single adjustment for full union carpenters, tapers and laborers is
   about two thirds of the low bidder's base bid and is not about scope at all, and one estimator
   maintains two complete leveling workbooks for one project identical in every scope cell and every
   bid, differing only in the labor regime, about one and three quarters apart.
4. The rate classification and zone, commercial versus residential, on the affected operations.
5. Whether the non-load-bearing interior partitions are framed by this trade, by the carpenter, or by
   a panel supplier.
6. Total board and ceiling area, driven by unit count, ceiling heights and how much amenity and
   back-of-house area the building carries. Ceilings, not partitions, are where that money is: on the
   one bidder workbook that decomposes the package, ceilings are more than half the direct cost and
   interior partitions about a sixteenth.
7. Assembly complexity: double and triple layer assemblies, rating count, shaft wall extent, and the
   number of distinct ceiling assemblies in the reflected ceiling plans.
8. Acoustic isolation strategy. Resilient channel versus sound clips is a real substitution with a
   real price, eliminating isolation clips at unit ceilings shows up as a value-engineering deduct,
   and on a panelised job the choice is made in another package.
9. On renovation and conversion, the extent of existing construction to wrap, patch, furr, top off or
   infill. A single existing-conditions alternate can exceed a third of a base bid.
10. Phase character, which sets the labor rate independently of the assembly. One bidder's wall crew
    rate on temporary-wall and renovation phases is about two and two thirds its new-build rate, and
    its ceiling crew rate likewise, which is why small enabling and temporary phases price out at
    multiples of their square footage.
11. Exterior cold-formed framing extent, and whether delegated design and stamped calculations are in
    or out.
12. Steel material pricing and escalation exposure.
13. Level of finish extent, particularly how much Level 5 the wall covering schedule creates.
14. Hoisting, access and whether union laborers must handle material.
15. Counted installs, mainly access panels and door frames, at unit prices that vary widely.
16. Patching and go-backs.
17. Bond, warranty term, insurance basis, tax treatment, portal fees and workforce participation.

What a full rate card shows, from the few proposals that publish one. Tape and finish is a small
fraction of the assembly: its material rate is about a twelfth of the board material rate on one card
and its combined rate about a quarter of the board rate and under a tenth of the labor rate on the
same square foot on another. The two crew labor rates differ by nearly half between walls and
ceilings. Metal stud and top and bottom track can price per linear foot at exactly the rate the same
bidder uses for wood studs and plates. A uniform waste factor of about five percent is applied to
every material row, and a combined markup for overhead, profit, tax and insurance is applied to
direct cost at the bottom. An estimator's own plugs run the same way and give a square-foot ladder:
podium-level board, framing and insulation at roughly twice the rate of wood-framed-level board and
insulation on the same project, and non-bearing framing at wood levels at roughly a third of the
board-and-insulation rate over the identical area. Three rates, one building, one trade.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within-project normalized spreads run from about
one and a fifth to about four times, and bidder counts of real companies run from two to fourteen.
Neither figure means much on its own, and four cautions matter more than the range.

- Ranking rows overstate the pool badly. Fifteen rows can cover seven companies, eleven rows six
  companies, and twenty rows can carry eighteen that read no bid. One company can hold three columns,
  two of them same-date and a few hundred apart at the total; revision labels can be inverted so the
  later number carries the earlier label; a misfiled duplicate under a second trade token can become
  a column of its own under a misspelling of the company name; and a ranked column can have no
  document anywhere in the bid folder, which is the mirror of a priced offer no ranking ever saw.
  Priced offers that no ranking ever saw are also real, and one of them would have ranked first below
  the selected bidder.
- A bimodal field is a definition failure, not a competitive result. On the widest spread in the
  corpus the field splits into a low band and a band more than twice as high with nothing between
  them, and the gap tracks whether insulation and full framing are in the number and whether the
  labor is union. That is several scopes wearing one name. Route it to scope definition, do not
  compare it.
- A wide spread can be a packaging artefact the breakouts dissolve. Three suppliers' printed totals
  spread by about a third; once the structural steel one of them silently carried is broken out, two
  of the three sit within half a percent of each other. Interpret a spread with its cause in both
  directions.
- A tight spread proves nothing about scope. The tightest spreads seen sit on the densest,
  most citation-heavy scope sheets, which is scope definition compressing spread. But they also sit
  on the thinnest sheet over the largest package, and on a sheet carrying three unanswered questions
  in the estimator's own cells and two bidders answering one numbered alternate with opposite signs.
  Four of six bidders on one project sit within about a seventh of each other while one of them has
  painting inside its number. Convergence is also selective: two bidders agree within a few percent
  on all five residential buildings of one project and diverge by two to four times on the clubhouses
  and the garages, because repetitive work converges and one-off amenity work does not.

The estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus adjustments, where adjustments
are entered per bidder to cover what that bidder left out or carried extra. Where the adjustment is
entered it decides the order, without exception: a single plug on one row moves a bidder from first
to third and changes the selection; one bidder's identical stated price occupies three ranking rows
with three different adjustments spanning about one and seven tenths and moving it from fifth to
ninth of eleven; the base-bid low bidder is not the adjusted low bidder; and on a buyout sheet the
selected bidder's base bid is the lowest of three and its adjusted total the highest.

But adjustment is not the practice, only the right practice. Most leveling sheets enter adjustments
on a minority of their columns, several enter none at all and rank the field on raw
base bids while their own scope cells carry question marks, and several enter exactly one plug class
ever: insulation. Everything else in section 3's gap list is entered on no sheet in some whole
practices. The insulation plug itself is applied inconsistently: correctly to every column at each
bidder's own stated value on one sheet, entered against one bidder from an outside package's bid
while a second bidder's own stated value more than twice that plug is written into the same row and
never added on another, known and valued and never applied across two rounds on a third, and at a
figure reconciling to neither of a bidder's two stated breakouts nor their sum on a fourth. The
direction also runs both ways: on one project the adjustments are mostly subtractions, stripping
insulation out of the broad bidders rather than plugging it into the narrow ones, which is the same
arithmetic and the opposite instruction. Read section 3's gap list as a specification for what a
sheet should ask, because the scope engine's value is highest exactly where the estimator has none.

Selection is decided by scope completeness rather than by price, in both directions. The low column
is selected roughly as often as not. Where it is passed over it is passed over for having no readable
scope: a one-sentence scope statement with no exclusion list, a reconstructed verbal with no document
at all. Where it is taken it can be the shortest and least specific proposal in the pool, which is
what happens on a sheet that does not read scope. A general contractor's own budget carried in the
ranking is not a reliable anchor either: it sits about two fifths below the low bid on one project,
within one percent of it on another, about a tenth above it on a third, above six of eleven ranking
rows on a fourth, and on one project it is a schematic-stage figure carried unchanged through two
later rounds seven months apart, where it is the lowest column and the selected bidder the highest.
Sometimes it carries no number at all while occupying two of a sheet's three selection slots.

Finally, read the printed total against the breakout in both directions. It can roll alternates and
siblings in. An estimating system can add a deduct alternate to the base and print the sum, so a
sheet that carries that total and records no alternate ranks the bidder too high and a place too low.
A total can be post-markup while its own segment lines are pre-markup, so the segments fall about a
fifth short, and the same bidder uses both conventions on different projects under the same markup
sentence. A total can exceed its own priced breakout by about a tenth because an unpriced line sits
in the table, which is the inverse hazard and means a scope nobody priced on paper. A proposal can
print a total on two pages with different figures, only one of which reconciles to the sum of its
blocks. And a bid can carry a nine-row breakout with an entirely blank price column under a total.
Two coupled hazards go with it: mutually exclusive configurations summed as if they were additive
deducts, which counts one choice up to three times, and two deducts marked pick one, where summing
the ladder overstates the achievable reduction.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file rests on two hundred and thirty-six proposal-level records, forty-five estimator leveling
and buyout evaluation records, and fourteen returned scope sheet records, read from real bid folders
and real bid comparison workbooks across twenty-five projects. Every document in the reading cut was
opened, two hundred and twenty-four of them, and none was skipped or unopenable. More than forty
distinct bidding companies appear, counted by normalizing company names down to their distinguishing
words, which merges spelling variants and may merge a few genuinely different firms. Around one
thousand two hundred and thirty priced or quantified lines are captured alongside, in square feet,
linear feet, each, hours, man-days, pours, per-module and lump sums.

Solicitation moments were counted by clustering each project's own free-text set-stage strings into
the round at which the market was asked to price, ignoring variation in the cited basis document and
ignoring bidder-side stage labelling, since a bidder calling one set a permit set and another calling
the same set design development is one moment and not two. A later negotiation pass that produced
priced returns, a buyout or a value-engineering round, counts as its own moment. On that method the
twenty-five projects carry forty-one solicitation moments, from one to five each. The forty-five
evaluation records are counted separately, because a leveling sheet is often reissued within one
moment and one project can carry three.

Building types: seventeen new-construction podium multifamily, which is the majority and the
calibration case; three office buildings converting to residential; one historic building converting
to apartments; one office core and shell; one ten-storey hotel whose primary structure is
prefabricated light gauge wall panels on a composite deck; one healthcare building with renovation,
enabling and temporary phases; and one historic theatre restoration. Latest bid years run from 2022
to 2026, and documents inside them cite sets back to 2019. Proposals span conceptual, schematic
design, design development, permit, construction documents, conformed and re-issued sets, guaranteed
maximum price rounds, buyout and value engineering.

Four of the twenty-five projects are the four this file was originally calibrated on. Confirmations
sourced only to those four are re-readings of the original evidence rather than corroboration, and
they are not stated at more strength here than they were. Everything about prefabricated and
panelised construction, and everything from the fifteen projects in the two breadth reads, is new
evidence.

The bidder pool repeats heavily, with about half the companies appearing on more than one project.
That repetition is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4
testable, and it is also the largest limit on how far the frequencies here generalize: where this file
says a bidder does something, it is sometimes the same bidder observed across several jobs rather
than the market. The company count is an upper bound for the reasons section 4 gives, and a lower
bound at the same time: one operation trading under two names is documented in the shared signatory,
the shared telephone line and an invitation forwarded from the other company's own domain, and a
company that renamed itself between two rounds reads as two bidders to any name-keyed ranking, so
nobody has checked the rest.

What this file cannot yet claim:

- It is one regional market, through one general contractor, with one estimating team's workbook
  templates. Every leveling observation is that team's practice, and section 7 says explicitly where
  a described practice is that team's habit rather than a market fact. Nothing here is tested against
  another market or another buyer.
- Institutional, laboratory, industrial and public prevailing-wage interiors remain untested. The
  healthcare project supplies a full bid list, a hundred-and-seventy-row sheet and abuse-resistant
  board named and excluded, but lead-lined assemblies and clean-room finishes appear nowhere.
- There is almost no buyout evidence. One executed buyout outcome exists: a package bought from a
  company that appears in no precon ranking and has no document in the bid folder, at about four
  fifths of the budget the selected precon bidder had set, on an owner allowance contract, with the
  gypsum concrete split out separately under a concrete division code. No signed subcontract, no
  schedule of values and no change order history is anywhere in the corpus, so nothing here shows
  which exclusions survived negotiation, whether the insulation ended up inside the package, who
  actually took the non-load-bearing partitions, or whether any plug was the right size. Everything
  in sections 3 and 7 about plug sizes is a bid-side observation.
- The claim that the sequencing, access, schedule-continuity, frame-delivery and deck-height
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed in the field.
- The fully collapsed package is now shown twice and is still thin. One subcontractor bids rough
  framing, air and vapor barrier, window and door installation, siding and drywall on one project and
  states manhours for all five; a second, asked directly, answered in writing that it had included
  the wood-framed-area non-bearing partitions as wood; and on a panelised job a drywall contractor
  furnishes and erects the structural walls, the composite deck, the crane and the windows in one
  printed total with the drywall work under half of it. What is still not shown is a drywall
  contractor framing a wood structure on a conventional job.
- The corpus has not shown a project with no podium and no metal-framed portion, so the pure
  wood-frame and pure concrete cases are both only partially evidenced. The opposite case, a building
  metal-framed throughout, is now covered by one hotel.
- The corpus has not shown exterior insulation and finish systems or rainscreen backup detailing
  beyond one bidder carrying that system at abandoned elevator penthouses, so the envelope seam is
  still mapped mostly from this side.
- Acoustical ceiling tile is no longer only a boundary, because non-multifamily bidders price the
  system inside the base, but no specialist ceiling proposal was read in this pass and the ceilings
  package still deserves its own file. Gypsum floor underlayment is likewise treated only as a
  boundary, though the double arrival from one bidder is now proven rather than warned about.
- The panelised and prefabricated evidence rests on one hotel plus scattered structural offers on
  four podium projects. It is enough to say the package boundary moves and to name the gaps it
  leaves; it is not enough to give frequencies within that building type.
- The office core-and-shell finding and the recency finding are confounded, because the one office
  project is also the oldest in the set. Read the office differences as building type and not as a
  change over time.
- Nothing here shows how a split furnish-and-install structural buy actually performs. The
  install-only prices behind the ranked combinations were assembled by the estimator from suppliers'
  own quantity clarification sheets rather than from the contract documents, and two of them priced
  against two different suppliers' systems, so they were not rankable against each other and were
  ranked anyway.

A note on reading the documents themselves, because it changes what is knowable. A significant
minority of these files have no text layer at all, and a further set carry checkbox matrices whose
column marks vanish under text extraction while every row label survives. A text-only read of those
proposals inverts answers by omission and looks perfectly clean while doing it. In one reading pass
alone it would have dropped a complete twenty-five-line bid with two priced insulation breakouts, a
whole revision of the largest package on its project, an email-only value-engineering menu and a
manhour disclosure. In another it would have dropped a bidder's entire original proposal, bound
image-only in front of an email thread that extracts cleanly, and with it the highest number in a
pool of ten. A page that extracts to zero characters must be checked for ink before it is called
blank; the check is cheap and it is the check that caught that error. Render anything whose extracted
text looks structurally thin, and render anything whose grain is a grid.

This file carries a structural gap list, in section 3, in three parts: the standing list, the
conversion and existing-fabric list, and the panelised list. The standing list is a specification for
what a scope sheet should ask, not a description of what the leveling sheets in this corpus actually
plug.
