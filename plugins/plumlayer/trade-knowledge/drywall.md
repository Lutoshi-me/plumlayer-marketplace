# Drywall

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the drywall package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real drywall contractors can bid it and a real estimator
can level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

There is no single anchor section. The package spans Divisions 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 at once, and the
proposals say so: bidders cite 092900 gypsum board, 092116 non-structural metal framing, 092116.23 or
092120 shaft wall assemblies, 054000 cold-formed metal framing, 061000 or 061050 rough carpentry for
blocking, 061600 sheathing, 072100 thermal insulation, 078443 joint firestopping, 079219 acoustical
joint sealants, 079513 interior expansion joint covers, 081113 hollow metal doors and frames for
install labor, 066400 FRP paneling, and 095100 acoustical ceiling tile. An estimator files it under a
09 21 code, but the exact code drifts between 09 2100, 09 2110 and 09 2116 from project to project, so
the code is a weak identifier and the trade token is the strong one. Some bidders still write the old
09250 or 092110 numbering.

The package as bid in the market is metal framing plus gypsum board plus taping, with insulation
attached as a detachable block. That block is the single most important packaging fact in the trade.
Insulation in or out changes the number by roughly a factor of two on wood-framed multifamily and by
around a fifth on concrete and steel work. Bidders on the same project, reading the same drawings,
will hand back numbers that differ by that much purely because one carried insulation and the other
did not, and neither is wrong. Establish whether insulation is in the package before comparing
anything.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another spec section:

- Insulation, as above. Usually batt and mineral wool at minimum. Spray foam, blown insulation and
  spray-applied cementitious insulation split off separately and often go to a specialty applicator.
- In-wall and in-ceiling blocking, but almost always bounded to metal-framed walls only.
- Hollow metal frame installation, labor only, frames furnished by others. Nearly every bidder
  carries this and nearly every bidder excludes the doors and the hardware.
- Access panel installation, material by others, usually with a per-panel price and an allowance
  count rather than a schedule.
- Firestopping and acoustical sealant, but bounded to the tops and bottoms of the assemblies the
  bidder built. Penetrations by other trades are excluded by essentially every bidder seen.
- Shaft wall assemblies, including shaftliner, C-T studs and J-runners. Carried by every bidder seen.
- Gypsum board ceilings, soffits, suspension grid, resilient channel and sound isolation clips.
- Exterior cold-formed framing and exterior gypsum sheathing at the podium or metal-framed levels of
  a wood-framed building, usually with delegated-design shop drawings and calculations.
- Fiberglass reinforced plastic panels, wall protection and fabric or acoustic wall panels. Contested
  with painting, and the estimator's own notes tend to land them here.
- Fire extinguisher cabinet installation and corner guard installation, both usually install-only.
- On renovation work, roof-level framing, decking, coverboard, curbs, parapet plywood and
  penthouse framing, which pulls a substantial amount of Division 5 and 7 into a Division 9 bid.
- Gypsum floor underlayment. A minority of drywall bidders quote it alongside the drywall package,
  and the same companies also bid it as a standalone trade, so the same scope can arrive twice.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like drywall:

- Acoustical ceiling tile. Treated as its own package with its own bid list and its own leveling
  sheet, and bid by ceiling specialists who quantify by mark and by square foot. Drywall bidders
  price it anyway, sometimes in the base and sometimes as an alternate, so the same ceiling can be
  carried twice or dropped entirely. Section 5 covers the seam.
- Wood framing of every kind. On a wood-framed or podium building the structural framing belongs to
  the framing carpenter, and the drywall bidder boards and finishes what the carpenter builds.
- Masonry interior partitions and CMU shaft construction.
- Spray foam, blown and spray-applied insulation, which bidders name and exclude even when they
  carry batt.
- Through-penetration firestopping around pipes, conduit and duct.
- Gypcrete and floor underlayment, which has its own bid list on every project seen.

## 2. How the market bids

Drywall is not a one-number trade. The default output is two to five numbers, and the segmentation is
almost always commercial rather than physical: drywall and framing as one figure, insulation as
another, and then whatever the general contractor asked to see broken out. A single undifferentiated
lump sum happens, but it is a minority shape and it is the hardest to level.

Grain, not number count, is what distinguishes proposals here. Six distinct shapes appear:

- The house form. A pre-printed matrix listing board by type, stud by size and gauge and spacing,
  insulation by product, and sealant by condition, with every quantity column reading "per partition
  schedule" or "per schedule" and a level of finish stated per board type. This is a deliberately
  quantity-free document that tells you exactly what materials were priced and nothing about how
  much. It is the most informative shape for scope purposes and the least informative for quantity.
- The scope narrative. A bulleted list of furnish-and-install statements organized by building
  element, sometimes keyed to wall-type marks and detail numbers. Common.
- The spec-section answer. Scope organized section number by section number with an inclusion or
  exclusion carve-out written under each. Rare, and the most precise about boundaries.
- The measured takeoff. Pricing by area family with real quantities, linear feet of interior and
  shaft wall and square feet of ceiling per reflected ceiling plan. Rare, and worth reading closely
  when it appears, because it is the only place the trade's real quantities become visible.
- The phase price. The package split by construction phase rather than by scope, with the phase
  prices summing to the same total. Rare.
- The returned scope sheet. The general contractor's own scope form handed back with a Yes, No or
  dollar figure against every row, sometimes with the price written by hand in the margin and no
  proposal letter at all. Common enough that a pipeline which only reads letterhead proposals will
  drop real bids, including, on one project, the second-highest bid on the board.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Board types with thickness and rating, and often a manufacturer and product line. Nearly always.
- Stud depth, gauge and spacing, usually as nominal dimensions and occasionally by the industry stud
  designation code.
- Level of finish by condition. Almost always present in some form, and it is the most consistently
  stated technical fact in the trade. The default pattern is Level 4 at surfaces exposed to view,
  Level 1 or fire tape at concealed and plenum areas, Level 5 only where scheduled to receive wall
  covering or in amenity space, and Level 2 at substrates receiving tile. Some bidders subdivide
  further, distinguishing Level 1 at joints only from Level 1 at joints and penetrations depending on
  whether the wall above the ceiling is fire rated or sound rated.
- Labor type, and here it is stated far more often than in most finishes trades, because it is the
  largest price driver. Union, open shop, or a specific mix.
- The basis document, a named set with a date. Nearly always, and worth checking, because it is
  frequently not the set in the folder.
- Whether taxes are included. Commonly.
- Change-order labor rates by craft. Commonly.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Quantities. Measured quantities are rare, and where a quantity column exists on the bidder's own
  form it is often filled in with the word "lump", which is at least honest.
- How the number splits between labor and material. Rarely visible.
- Manhours. Very rarely, though the general contractor's scope sheet asks for it and a few bidders
  answer.
- A go-back or punch allowance, unless the general contractor asked. Patching is usually either
  absent or an allowance rather than a priced scope.
- Escalation. When it appears it is a disclaimer pushing material escalation onto the owner, or a
  supplier's price-increase letter attached to the proposal rather than a number in it.
- Bond, unless requested, and when quoted the rate ranges widely enough that it cannot be assumed.

Exclusion behavior splits the market, and the split is wider here than in most trades. Some proposals
carry a single exclusion line. Others run past forty. A one-line exclusion list is not broader scope,
it is every boundary left open, and at leveling each one becomes a plug or a question. Beware also of
the opposite failure: a repeat bidder's printed exclusion block can contradict its own priced scope
table in the same document, excluding a product the pricing pages explicitly carry. Where the two
disagree, the priced table is the bid and the block is stationery.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs:

- Most change price only, with the scope text byte-identical. Movements are large: a quarter off a
  base bid with no scope change and no stated reason is normal. A reason is almost never documented.
- A revision may move no money at all and still be the informative one, because what it adds is a
  breakout: an insulation figure, a unit-framing figure, a labor-per-operation split. The breakout is
  the point. The estimator immediately uses it as the adjustment that makes the bidder comparable to
  those who excluded the same block, so a revision that restructures without repricing is doing the
  leveling work.
- A revision may quietly shrink the base scope and re-offer the removed block as an alternate. The
  base bid falls and reads as a discount, when what actually happened is that a whole partition class
  left the package. Only the alternate list reveals it. Compare base bids across rounds without
  reading the alternates and you will misread the movement entirely.
- A revision may be a labor substitution rather than a price change, most often swapping open-shop
  tapers for union tapers and stating the delta.
- A revision is frequently not a document. A letter saying the new documents do not change the price
  is a complete revision, and one PDF often carries both the letter and the original estimate it
  confirms.
- Revision labels are unreliable. The filename label and the document's own revision number can be
  off by one, and both rounds of a pair can carry the same date, so nothing inside the documents
  distinguishes them.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Drywall scope sheets are the largest in the finishes division. Sheets seen have run 26, 43, 82 and 139
scope rows, and the versions issued to bidders as bid forms have run past three hundred numbered rows
once the commercial, schedule, safety, coordination and submittal blocks are included. Target roughly
one hundred twenty to one hundred sixty scope lines for a full multifamily drywall package. The
thinnest sheet seen, at twenty six rows, was the one where the estimator entered no adjustments at all
and ranked packages whose insulation content differed by nearly a factor of two against each other,
which is exactly the failure a thin sheet produces.

Note also that the scope sheet grows between issues. The same project's sheet gained rows between one
bidder's copy and the next, and every added row was a gap that an earlier answer had exposed: lites
inside doors, a heated plenum at the garage ceiling, an insulation board at one detail, impact
resistant board at corners, painting of patch work. A scope list for this trade should be treated as
something that learns from the answers.

### The partition-type question

The received idea is that drywall subcontractors price all interior partitions per the type schedule
as nearly one answer, so a scope list should not enumerate partition types. The corpus supports that,
with an important qualification.

It is true for the body of the work. Essentially every proposal, in every shape, points at the
partition schedule rather than reproducing it. The house form writes "per partition schedule" in every
quantity cell. The narrative form writes "all interior LGMF walls according to wall types". The
spec-section form names 092116 and stops. Even the one proposal with a real measured takeoff collapsed
all interior partitions into a single measured line. No proposal seen prices partition type W3
separately from partition type W4. Enumerating the type schedule as scope rows will produce a sheet no
bidder can answer.

It is false at the boundaries, and that is where the money is. What varies bidder to bidder is never
which partition types are included, it is: whether the framing is theirs or the carpenter's, whether
the insulation inside the partition is theirs, whether the blocking inside it is theirs, what level of
finish each face receives, whether the head-of-wall and base-of-wall sealant and firestop are theirs,
and whether the partition runs to deck or stops at the ceiling. Those six questions, asked once per
wall family rather than once per wall type, are the correct grain. A wall family here means a group
that shares a construction and an ownership answer: interior metal-framed non-load-bearing partitions,
wood-framed unit and demising partitions, corridor and demising walls, shaft walls, exterior
cold-formed backup walls, and furred walls at existing or masonry construction.

So: collapse the partition schedule, and expand the ownership questions.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- The commercial frame, before any physical scope: base bid basis, whether an owner-controlled
  insurance program is assumed, warranty duration, bond, sales tax and tax-exempt status, project
  portal fees, and whether the price assumes union, open shop, or a stated mix. Every estimator sheet
  seen opens this way, and these rows decide more of the ranking than most of the physical ones.
- Insulation, broken by material and by location, not as one row. Batt, mineral wool, spray foam,
  blown, spray-applied cementitious, rigid, insulated sheathing, thermal break, fireblocking. Bidders
  carry some and exclude others in the same sentence, and this block is the largest single source of
  incomparability in the trade.
- Level of finish, as its own block, by area and condition: exposed to view, concealed and plenum,
  above acoustical ceilings at rated walls, above acoustical ceilings at sound-rated walls, substrates
  receiving tile, surfaces receiving wall covering, amenity and critical-light space. One estimator
  sheet carries this as an explicit block and it is the right pattern.
- Wall family, per the argument above, with the six ownership questions attached.
- Shaft walls as their own line, separate from partitions, because the assembly, the studs and the
  finish level all differ and because whether the shaft extends through the floor-ceiling assembly is
  a structural question a bidder will qualify.
- Ceiling assemblies, detail by detail. This is the part of a drywall scope sheet that legitimately
  runs long: draft stops, cove lighting framing and plywood, perimeter lighting, preformed curved
  ceilings, garage ceilings, window shade pockets, drapery pockets, rounded ceilings, sound isolation
  hangers, spring isolators, resilient channel versus sound clips. Each of these is answered
  differently by different bidders and several were answered No by bidders who answered Yes to
  everything else.
- Blocking, broken by what it supports and by which walls it sits in: bath accessories and grab bars,
  casework and shelving, mirrors and artwork, televisions and brackets, window treatments, mailboxes,
  handrails and railings at ramps, elevator door jambs, smoke curtain housings, kitchen islands and
  countertop brackets, and roof and curb blocking. Almost every bidder qualifies blocking to metal
  walls only, so a single blocking row will always be ambiguous.
- Sealant and firestop, broken by condition: head of wall, base of wall, drywall to drywall joints,
  at windows, at exterior doors, slab edge, acoustical at demising and corridor perimeters, and MEP
  penetrations. The last one is a separate row because it is excluded by essentially every bidder.
- Air sealing and compartmentalization as its own block, separate from acoustical sealant, on any
  project with an envelope performance requirement.
- Installs, each with a count: hollow metal welded frames, hollow metal knock-down frames, doors and
  hardware, access panels flanged and taped-in, fire extinguisher cabinets, corner guards, expansion
  joint covers. Every estimator sheet carries these as counted rows with a unit price, and the unit
  prices seen for access panels alone span more than a fourfold range, so the count and the rate both
  have to be on the sheet.
- Specialty finishes by mark: acoustical gypsum board, felt panels, fabric-wrapped and acoustic
  panels, FRP, wall protection, abuse-resistant and impact-resistant board. Answered No by most
  bidders and carried by a few, which is the definition of a row that must exist.
- Exterior and roof-level work, on any building with a metal-framed portion: balconies, canopies,
  bump-outs and soffits, parapets, hung parapets, penthouse framing, decking, coverboard, curbs and
  pipe portals. Bidders split sharply here and several exclude the lot.
- The existing-conditions block on renovation work: patching and repair of existing, furring and
  boarding the far side of existing-to-remain construction, wrapping existing plaster columns and
  beams, infilling existing shafts, and prep of existing surfaces to receive new finishes. On one
  renovation a single alternate for wrapping existing beams was worth more than a third of the base
  bid, so this block is not a detail.
- Hoisting, staging, loading and cleanup, as its own block, and specifically whether the labor doing
  the loading and cleanup is union.
- Patching and go-backs, with a stated hours-per-apartment basis, for the reasons in section 6.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual partition types. Collapse to wall family, as argued above.
- Room by room and unit by unit. No document seen prices a single room, and unit-type variation shows
  up only as a total count.
- Layer counts, board manufacturer, corner bead, trim, control joint accessories, mesh, tape,
  compound, screws and fasteners. These are attributes of a line. Where a bidder mentions corner bead
  or tear-away bead at all it is as a note under the board table, never as a priced item.
- Level 4 areas within one finish region. The finish level is the enumeration axis, not the surface.
- Ceilings, soffits, fur-downs and bulkheads within one area and one assembly. They collapse to one
  line unless the assembly or the isolation detail changes, in which case the detail is the line.
- Stud gauge and spacing within one wall family, unless the schedule itself changes them, in which
  case the wall family has already split.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
drywall, the test fires hardest on ownership rather than on geometry: on every line in section 5, on
the insulation block, and on the counted installs. It fires weakly on partition geometry, which is why
the type schedule collapses.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. Insulation is the clearest case. A proposal that
says nothing about insulation on a project whose wall types carry it is not including it, and a
proposal that says nothing about blocking in wood walls almost certainly means metal walls only.
Silence in this trade defaults toward exclusion far more reliably than in trades where the package
boundary is obvious.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the trade that must pick the work up, and if
no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| --- | --- |
| Firestopping, sealing and acoustical sealing of MEP penetrations (excluded by every bidder seen) | The penetrating trade, a firestopping specialist, or unassigned. The most reliable gap in the trade |
| Wood framing of any kind, sill plates, nailers, shear wall sheathing, plywood subfloor (most bidders) | Framing carpenter |
| Blocking within wood-framed walls (most bidders, usually as a qualification rather than an exclusion) | Framing carpenter, and the single most common qualification in the corpus |
| Doors and hardware installation, with hollow metal frame install usually kept (most bidders) | Door and hardware installer, or a priced alternate back to this bidder |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Repeat bidders reproduce the same exclusion block on every project, and they make up a large share of
any drywall bid list, so the presence of a line carries no project information. Two bidders in the
corpus share nearly identical proposal forms, terms and exclusion wording, down to the perimeter
clearance clause and the snow-depth line. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- An exclusion pinned to a specific wall-type mark or detail number. When a bidder excludes the
  sandwiched plywood at one wall type, or the soffit at one detail, it read the set and found
  something it will not do.
- A stated assumption about a condition the drawings do not fix. The strongest example in the corpus
  is a renovation bidder publishing its assumed deck heights floor by floor, which converts an
  invisible assumption into a checkable one. On a renovation this is the single most useful thing a
  drywall bidder can disclose, and almost nobody does it.
- A quantity or count that is not in the documents: numbers of door frames, access panels, tubs,
  skylights, columns to wrap, linear feet of beam to wrap. These are the bidder's takeoff leaking
  through, and they are the only real quantities you will get.
- An exclusion justified by absence from the documents, such as installs carried at zero because no
  door schedule, access panel schedule or window count exists in the set. When a bidder refuses to
  price rather than guessing, that is a documents problem to fix, not a bidder to penalize.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a post office in operation, a historic
  element, an elevator bay, a podium level, a second building.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Labor type, stated as union, open shop, or a named mix by operation. The largest driver in the
  trade, and see section 7.
- A geographic labor zone and a residential versus commercial rate classification. Bidders state a
  zone on the face of the proposal, and the commercial rate for the same operations runs on the order
  of two thirds higher than the residential rate. This is invisible on any drawing.
- A continuous schedule without demobilization or remobilization, sometimes with the start and
  substantial completion months named. Phasing a job that was priced continuous is a change.
- Site access for material handling: a stated perimeter clearance for forklift and boom lift, a
  dedicated hoist, a loading dock, or an open window on each floor to load full-height board through.
  Every bidder assumes one of these and several make it explicit.
- A delivery window, in one case between four and seven in the morning, for full-height stud and
  ten-foot board.
- Sequencing conditions written as scope: fire taping above ceilings completed before the mechanical
  and electrical rough, or shaftwall extending continuously through the floor-ceiling assembly with
  the structure required to accommodate it.
- Level of finish defaults, as set out in section 3. These are the bidder's interpretation of the
  specification and they vary.
- Joint compound type. At least one bidder qualifies ready-mix all-purpose compound in place of the
  specified setting-type compound. It reads as a minor note and it is a specification deviation.
- Stud gauge and product equivalence. Bidders substitute equivalent-gauge products, offer a board
  brand as an equal on the strength of a UL listing, and swap resilient channel grades. Each of these
  is priced as a deduct or an add somewhere in the corpus.
- Material escalation, either disclaimed onto the owner or evidenced by an attached supplier letter
  announcing a double-digit steel increase with quotes valid for a month. Steel stud pricing moves,
  and proposal validity periods in this trade run as short as a week for that reason.
- Tax-exempt status, where the bidder assumes taxable and states that the line comes out if the owner
  is exempt.
- Whether itemized line prices are commitments. A common disclaimer says line prices are for
  reference only and are void if the whole scope is not awarded, which means the breakout you are
  leveling on is not a unit price.
- The standard of acceptance for taping. One scope sheet requires the drywall contractor to correct
  work and taping until approved by the painter, which puts the acceptance test in another trade's
  hands rather than in a finish level.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This trade is defined more by its seams than by its materials. Every one below is genuinely contested
in the corpus, not merely adjacent.

### Metal versus wood: the seam that matters most

On any podium or wood-framed multifamily building the package splits along a line the drawings do not
draw. The framing carpenter builds the structural wood walls, and the drywall contractor boards and
finishes them. The drywall contractor builds the metal framing at the podium, the shafts, the soffits
and the ceilings. Everything the drywall contractor qualifies flows from that line: blocking is metal
walls only, sheathing is at cold-formed framing only, window and door returns are inside metal walls
only, and any shimming or added framing at rough openings the carpenter framed is excluded.

Non-load-bearing interior partitions are the contested zone, and both trades bid them. The drywall
contractor offers to frame them in light gauge as an add. The wood framer offers to frame them in wood
as an alternate, and will note that its carried spacing is wider than the metal equivalent, which
makes the two alternates non-comparable on their face. On one project two drywall bidders priced this
same alternate within a few percent of each other, so the market has a settled view of what it is
worth. What it does not have is a settled view of whose it is. Get this decided before you level
anything, because the base bid of a bidder who assumed wood framing and the base bid of one who
assumed metal are not the same package.

Beware also that a wood framing proposal can arrive filed under the drywall trade, precisely because
it carries this alternate. Trust the document, not the folder.

### Painting

Painting's boundary with drywall is contested in both directions and appears from both sides of the
corpus.

- Preparation and skim coating. Painters disclaim responsibility for drywall quality and exclude wall
  patching, skim coating and excessive prep. Drywall bidders variously carry patching as an allowance,
  price it at a stated hours-per-apartment rate, or exclude patching of existing walls entirely. On a
  renovation, patching of existing is excluded by the drywall bidder and by the painter both, which
  makes it an unassigned gap.
- Painting of patch work. At least one drywall bidder prices not only the patching but the painting
  of its own patch work, at a stated hours-per-apartment rate roughly half its patching rate. That
  work is also inside the painter's scope on the same project, so it can be bought twice.
- Level 5 at wall covering locations. This is a drywall row on every estimator sheet, and it is the
  finish the wallcovering installer's warranty depends on. Some drywall bidders exclude Level 5
  outright.
- Wallcovering termination trims, fry reglets, outside corner trims and inside corner trims. The
  painting side records these as excluded by the painter and belonging to drywall, installed before
  the wallcovering. From this side, they are excluded by name and detail number in one round and
  priced as a trim alternate in the next, which confirms both that they are drywall's and that they
  are routinely not in the base bid. Put them on their own line.
- FRP, textile, felt and acoustic panels. The painting side records the estimator assigning these to
  drywall. From this side, some drywall bidders carry FRP by manufacturer and product code in the
  base, one carries acoustic panels in named rooms, and others exclude wall protection and fabric
  panels wholesale, while a printed exclusion block can exclude FRP on one page while the pricing
  table carries it on another. Contested within a single document, let alone between trades.
- Acceptance. One general contractor's scope sheet makes the drywall contractor correct its taping
  until the painter approves it, which is the only place in either trade where the seam is written as
  an acceptance test rather than a scope split.

### Acoustical ceilings

Acoustical ceiling tile is bid as its own package with its own bid list on every project seen, and the
specialists who bid it quantify by mark and by square foot with named grid systems, which is nothing
like the drywall shape. But drywall bidders price it too: some carry the complete system in the base
under the ceiling spec section, some carry it as a priced alternate, and most exclude it while still
carrying the framing, blocking, pockets and access-panel cuts around it. Three consequences. First,
ACT is a boundary to declare, not a scope to assume. Second, drywall's grid, hangers, blocking and
shade-pocket framing stay with drywall even when the tile does not, so do not delete the ceiling rows
when you move the tile. Third, a swap from acoustical tile to gypsum board in a corridor is priced as
an add for drywall rather than as a delta from the ceiling package, and one bidder annotated it that
way explicitly. Level it as two separate scopes, not as one substitution.

### Other seams

- Hollow metal frames, doors and hardware. Frames are furnished by the door supplier and installed by
  drywall, almost universally, and almost always bounded to metal-framed walls. Doors and hardware are
  excluded by most and offered back as a counted alternate by some. Frame grouting or stuffing is
  excluded by name at least once. Split jamb interior unit frames go to another trade.
- Access panels. Material by others, install by drywall, and the price depends on type: taped-in
  panels cost more than flanged and are quoted as an upcharge per panel. Bidders carry allowance
  counts because no access panel schedule exists at bid time, and the estimator carries its own count
  and rate. Two independent counts of the same panels is normal here.
- MEP penetration firestopping. Excluded by every bidder seen. Drywall seals the head and base of the
  walls it built and stops. Whether anyone else picked it up is a question worth asking on every
  project, and one bidder answers the row with the words "head and base only, penetrations by others",
  which is the whole seam in six words.
- Insulation. Split three ways in practice: batt and mineral wool with drywall, spray and blown to a
  specialty applicator, and rigid and thermal-break insulation with the envelope. A bidder can carry
  batt in the base and exclude all other insulation in the same document.
- Gypsum floor underlayment. Bid as its own trade, and also quoted inside drywall proposals by the
  companies that do both, with a remobilization fee per extra pour day and a stated number of pours.
  Watch for the same scope arriving under two trade tokens from the same bidder.
- Misc metals and structure. On renovation and roof-level work the drywall contractor installs
  decking and coverboard while the connection angles remain misc metals, and the drywall bidder will
  say so.
- Masonry. CMU partitions and elevator shafts are excluded by drywall and belong to the mason, and
  furring at masonry walls is a drywall row that at least one bidder offered to delete as a deduct.
- Labor jurisdiction over material handling. Not a scope seam but priced like one. Base bids carry
  non-union laborers for off-loading and cleanup, and union laborers for cleanup only, or for cleanup
  and off-loading, are two separately priced adds. The two adds differ from each other by roughly a
  factor of two, and the larger one can exceed the entire firestopping alternate. This exists on
  multiple projects and multiple bidders and appears on no drawing.
- Corner guards, fire extinguisher cabinets and expansion joint covers. Install-only, material by
  others, and carried by some bidders and excluded by others.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What drywall contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The partition type or wall type schedule. This is the primary document, and it is what every
   "per partition schedule" and "per wall types" reference points at. Stud depth, gauge, spacing,
   layer count, rating and insulation all live here. If the schedule is incomplete or the gauges are
   still delegated to a specialty engineer, the bid becomes a basis-of-design bid with a change order
   attached, and bidders say so.
2. The reflected ceiling plans and the ceiling assembly details. The second document, and the source
   of the long tail of ceiling rows in section 3. Bidders cite individual detail numbers here more
   than anywhere else in the set.
3. The interior assembly details and the fire rating sheets, for rating continuity, drywall lids and
   fire tops at the head of non-bearing walls, and rating maintenance behind recessed items.
4. Unit plans and the unit count, for repetition and for the per-apartment rates that patching and
   go-backs are quoted in.
5. The finish schedule, but only for the marks that change the drywall scope: wall covering locations
   that require Level 5, tile locations that require backer and Level 2, FRP and wall protection
   marks, and specialty panel marks.
6. The door schedule and the access panel schedule, for the counted installs. Where these do not
   exist, bidders either carry an allowance count or refuse to price the line.
7. The specification, cited by section number more than by content, and used mainly to settle finish
   levels, board types, stud thickness and firestopping standards.
8. The structural drawings, on any building with cold-formed framing, roof-level work or a podium.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff. Measured takeoffs exist but are rare, and the normal
method is wall family plus area plus assembly plus judgment.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Labor type. The largest single driver, and the leveling sheets track it on every bidder. Open-shop
  bidders occupy the bottom of the ranking on most projects and union bidders the top, with no
  overlap on some. But it is not a single switch: the package is several labor markets at once, and a
  bidder can price layout, stud install, blocking, pre-rock and hanging at union rates while pricing
  fire taping, finish taping, patching and insulation open shop. That bidder can beat a pure
  open-shop bidder. A leveling field with one labor value per bidder cannot represent this, and it is
  the most consequential thing the drawings do not say.
- The rate classification, commercial versus residential, and the geographic labor zone. Worth on the
  order of two thirds on the affected operations.
- Prevailing wage applicability, and any project workforce or residency requirements, which bidders
  answer Yes or No to on the scope sheet and price accordingly.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program is in place. Bidders quote general liability as a
  percentage of contract to be added back if it is not, and the percentage varies.
- Bond requirement and rate. Quoted rates in the corpus span from well under one percent to nearly
  three percent of contract, so a bond plug carried at a single rate will be wrong for most bidders.
- Warranty duration. One year is the market default and two years is a priced add, sometimes with a
  matching deduct offered to go back down. The delta is material.
- Sales tax treatment and the owner's tax-exempt status. Some bidders include tax, some exclude it,
  and at least one applies it only to the material portion, which on that bid was under half the
  total.
- Steel material escalation and the validity period of the price. Material validity in this trade
  runs as short as a week, and supplier increase letters travel with the proposals.
- Hoisting, staging and material handling: whether there is a hoist, a dock, a crane, an open window
  on each floor, a perimeter clear enough for a boom lift, and whether the drywall contractor or the
  general contractor is providing it. Also whether the laborers doing it are union.
- Schedule continuity and phasing. Bids are priced on a continuous schedule and demobilization is a
  change. One bidder prices the whole package by phase, which is the only way phasing becomes visible
  before it becomes a claim.
- Deck heights, on renovation. Whether an existing building's floor-to-deck dimensions match the
  assumption is worth a great deal in stud and board, and only one bidder in the corpus published
  what it assumed.
- The condition of what the demolition contractor leaves. One bidder assumes clean and straight
  edges, which is not a given.
- Whether patching and go-backs are expected of this trade, and at what hours-per-apartment.
- Whether the general contractor will require project-portal and payment-application fees, which show
  up as a small fixed fee plus a per-sub-tier charge on every project here.
- Whether the drywall contractor is expected to produce coordination drawings, BIM models, record
  drawings or as-builts. Answers split hard and one bidder prices RCP coordination drawings as a
  five-figure line.

Patching and go-backs deserve their own note, because it is the drywall analogue of the painter's
punch problem. A stated patching allowance is present in only a minority of proposals. Where a bidder
does state one, it is expressed per apartment in hours, and the corpus shows patching at roughly six
hours per apartment and painting of that patch work at roughly three. An estimator sheet carries a
patching row and plugs it when the bidder is silent. Any drywall scope line set should carry a
patching and repair line with an explicit hours-per-apartment basis, and should state whether painting
of the patch is inside it.

Set-stage behavior. At schematic and design development, bidders openly price around missing
information and say so: the framing is a basis-of-design carry pending delegated design, the installs
are carried at zero because no schedule exists, the blocking split is flagged as to be confirmed with
the general contractor. Treat those as honest rather than as gaps in the bid. By permit,
construction-document and conformed sets the same bidders switch to citing wall-type ranges, detail
numbers and counted quantities. Note also that a proposal's stated basis set and its folder's set
stage disagree often in this trade: bids priced two rounds behind the folder appear on more than one
project, and on one of them the bidder cited a set older than any other bidder on the same job.
Always read the basis date off the document.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet of board and of ceiling area, the underlying unit for almost everything, and the basis
  of the ceiling and soffit line in the one measured takeoff seen.
- Linear feet of wall, for interior partitions and separately for shaft wall. The natural unit for
  framing, and the one a takeoff-style proposal reports.
- Square feet for insulation, quoted as a rate per square foot when a bidder is asked to justify the
  insulation block, and the same bidder will quote competing insulation products at rates differing by
  nearly a factor of two.
- Each, for the counted installs: hollow metal frames, access panels, fire extinguisher cabinets,
  corner guards, tubs receiving backer, skylights, columns to wrap. This is where unit prices actually
  live in this trade, and the dispersion is severe. Access panel install alone runs across more than a
  fourfold range between bidders and estimator plugs.
- Linear feet, for existing beams to wrap on renovation.
- Hours, for change-order and time-and-material rates, quoted by craft and grade. Quoted by nearly
  every bidder.
- Hours per apartment, for patching and for painting of patch work.
- Total manhours for the project, asked for on the scope sheet and answered rarely.
- Percent of contract, for bond, for general liability if there is no owner-controlled program, and
  for the project-portal fee.
- Pours and pour-days, where floor underlayment is bundled, with a remobilization fee per extra day.
- Gross square feet, square feet of a defined framed area, and residential units, as the normalizing
  denominators. All three are used, and which one is chosen depends on the building.

That last point is worth stating plainly. There is no single leveling currency in this trade. One
estimator normalized one project on gross square feet, another on gross square feet less an excluded
existing tenancy, another on square feet of wood-framed area, and another on residential units. The
denominator is a judgment about what drives the work in that building, not a fact off the cover sheet,
and drywall costs expressed per unit and per square foot are not interchangeable even within one
estimator's own practice.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Whether insulation is inside the package. Larger than any physical variable. Roughly a doubling on
   wood-framed multifamily.
2. Labor type and the operation-by-operation mix of union and open-shop crews.
3. The rate classification and zone, commercial versus residential.
4. Whether the non-load-bearing interior partitions are framed by this trade or by the carpenter.
5. Total board and ceiling area, driven by unit count, ceiling heights and how much amenity and
   back-of-house area the building carries.
6. Assembly complexity: double and triple layer assemblies, rating count, shaft wall extent, and the
   number of distinct ceiling assemblies in the reflected ceiling plans.
7. Acoustic isolation strategy. Resilient channel versus sound clips is a real substitution with a
   real price, and eliminating isolation clips at unit ceilings shows up as a value-engineering
   deduct.
8. On renovation, the extent of existing construction to wrap, patch, furr or infill. A single
   existing-conditions alternate can exceed a third of a base bid.
9. Exterior cold-formed framing extent, and whether delegated design and stamped calculations are in
   or out.
10. Steel material pricing and escalation exposure.
11. Level of finish extent, particularly how much Level 5 the wall covering schedule creates.
12. Hoisting, access and whether union laborers must handle material.
13. Counted installs, mainly access panels and door frames, at unit prices that vary widely.
14. Patching and go-back hours per apartment.
15. Bond, warranty term, insurance basis and tax treatment.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest normalized bid ran at about 1.8 times, 2.6 times, 2.7 times and 3.5 times on the
four projects. Bidder counts per project ran from six to fourteen. Two further observations matter
more than the spread itself. The low bidder frequently does not win: on one project the selected
bidder ranked eleventh of fourteen on normalized cost, which is what happens when scope completeness
rather than price decides. And the general contractor's own budget, carried in the ranking alongside
the bids, sat about forty percent below the low bid on one project and above two thirds of the field
on another, so a drywall budget carried from history is not a reliable anchor.

One more pricing convention worth knowing: an estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base
bid plus adjustments, where adjustments are entered per bidder to cover what that bidder left out.
On the most carefully leveled project in the corpus those adjustments ranged from a large subtraction
to a large addition on bids within the same order of magnitude, and the adjustment, not the bid, set
the final order. The plugs to expect are insulation in or out, non-load-bearing partition framing,
MEP penetration firestopping, access panel and door frame installs at a counted rate, patching and
go-backs per apartment, wallcovering trims and reglets, garage and back-of-house ceilings, spray and
specialty insulation, blocking in wood walls, level 5 at wall covering, and the commercial adders of
bond, general liability, warranty term and portal fees. A scope line set that names all of those
explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging for it, and is the difference between a sheet that ranks
bidders and a sheet that merely lists them.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly three dozen drywall-package proposals spanning more than a dozen
bidding companies, plus four estimator leveling sheets and the general contractor's own scope sheets
as returned and answered by bidders, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional
market, all bid by one general contractor. The proposals span schematic design, design development,
permit, construction-document and conformed sets, and include several same-bidder revision pairs and
one four-round negotiation sequence, so the revision behavior in section 2 rests on observation rather
than inference. Several companies appear on more than one project, which is what makes the
boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable. Two of the bidders are the same
operation under two names, and one file filed under this trade is a wood framing proposal, both of
which are reflected in the corpus hazards noted throughout.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work, in one market, through one general
  contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors, institutional, healthcare, laboratory,
  industrial or public prevailing-wage work, where abuse-resistant board, lead-lined assemblies,
  impact walls and clean-room finishes would all change the package.
- One of the four projects was a renovation and conversion of an existing building. The
  existing-conditions block in section 3 and the deck-height and plaster-wrap material in sections 4
  and 6 rest largely on that one project.
- Only two proposals carried real measured quantities, so every linear-foot and square-foot convention
  in section 7 rests on those documents. Unit prices are better evidenced, because the counted
  installs appear in many proposals and on every estimator sheet.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, whether the insulation ended up inside the package, or who actually took the
  non-load-bearing partitions.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the sequencing, access, schedule-continuity
  and deck-height qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language
  rather than observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- Acoustical ceiling tile is treated here only as a boundary. The specialist ceiling proposals in the
  corpus were read only far enough to establish that the boundary is real and that the specialists bid
  by mark and quantity. The ceilings package deserves its own file.
- Gypsum floor underlayment is likewise treated only as a boundary, though it appears bundled inside
  drywall proposals often enough that it may belong in a shared section later.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the drywall contractor also framed the wood structure, so
  the fully-collapsed package is untested.
- The corpus has not shown exterior insulation and finish systems, direct-applied exterior finish, or
  rainscreen backup detailing beyond the point where every bidder excludes it, so the envelope seam is
  mapped only from this side.
- The corpus has not shown a project without a podium or metal-framed portion, so the pure wood-frame
  and pure concrete cases are both only partially evidenced.

This file carries no structural gap list. The first corpus round did not draw one out for this
trade; a later round writes it where the corpus supports one.
