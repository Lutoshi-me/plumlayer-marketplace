# Insulation

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the insulation package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real insulators can bid it and a real estimator can level it.

One thing to settle before anything else. This trade is two grain regimes under one division, and they
behave nothing alike:

- Cavity insulation. Batt, blown and sound attenuation material stuffed into framed assemblies. Driven
  by area and by wall and floor marks, priced by the square foot in the bidder's head, bid by
  contractors who do nothing else.
- Applied assemblies. Sprayed foam, spray-applied fiber, coatings and sheet membranes on decks, slabs
  and sheathing. Driven by access, sequencing and product choice, priced per location, bid by
  applicators who arrive with a rig and a hose.

A scope list that treats them as one thing will collapse the part that needs enumerating and enumerate
the part that needs a single line.

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Section 072100 (Thermal Insulation) is the anchor and is the section a proposal names when it names one
at all, which is less often than in most trades. Three other sections sit close enough that their work
routinely arrives inside an insulation bid or leaves it:

- 072700 Air Barriers. Bid as its own package by its own kind of contractor, and also delivered as a
  by-product of spray foam. A sheet-membrane bidder and a foam bidder are answering the same question
  with different materials, and only one of them will call it insulation.
- 078100 Applied Fireproofing. A separate section with a separate scope, but the same sprayed-coatings
  applicators sell both, and a leveling sheet may pair insulation and fireproofing as one breakout
  family on its bid form. A proposal headed as a fireproofing bid can carry a spray insulation line
  worth several times its fireproofing lines.
- 098100 and the acoustic sections. Spray-applied fiber products file under both Division 7 and Division
  9 in their own manufacturer literature. One product, two divisions, which is exactly why the assembly
  gets claimed by two packages.

### The fact that dominates this package

Insulation sits in or out of the drywall package, and nothing else about this trade moves as much money.
A drywall bidder will carry batt, sound attenuation blanket and air sealing inside its Division 9
proposal as a separately priced add. The same bidder, at the same labor basis, bidding the same work
standalone a few weeks later, has been seen at roughly twice that add. An estimator's own leveling note
on a batt row can read that the batt might be in Division 9 and needs confirming.

So the first scope decision is not which insulation, it is whose. Ask it explicitly, ask it early, and
carry the answer as a scope line, because the number depends on it more than on the product, the area or
the labor market.

### Kinds of company, which is the second thing to establish

Competitors here sell genuinely different things, and a ranking that ignores that is meaningless. On one
project a leveling sheet ranked nine entries spanning roughly twelve times bottom to top, and the bottom
six were spray-only, single-product or air-barrier-only offers rather than whole-package bids. Kinds
seen:

- Full-package batt and foam contractors. Carry the whole cavity scope plus the small foam locations.
  The only bidders whose number is a whole answer.
- Spray polyurethane foam specialists. Foam and its coatings, nothing else. Explicitly exclude all other
  insulations, primers and vapor barriers.
- Spray-applied fiber applicators, licensed to one product line. One assembly, one number.
- Sheet-applied air barrier installers, who also sell spray foam as a substitute for their own membrane
  and for rigid board.
- Aerosol envelope sealers, who seal the finished envelope to an airtightness target and carry no
  insulation at all.
- Drywall contractors, carrying insulation inside Division 9.
- Sprayed-coatings applicators who sell fireproofing and spray insulation off one rig.
- Material suppliers, who sell rigid board and accessories with no labor.
- Brokers, who submit an insulation contractor's takeoff under their own letterhead.

Two more identity traps. Insulation businesses merge, and the merged company keeps quoting under the old
name for a while, with the same salesperson and the same boilerplate; a bid list showing three names can
be one company. And the company name does not predict the trade: spray foam has been quoted by a
business whose email domain is a painting company and by another whose domain is a firestop company.

### Collapse attractors

Scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits elsewhere:

- Air sealing at the framing stage. Draft stop foam at door and window jambs, and gasket foam at wall
  plates, jack studs and framing gaps, are near universal in a full-package bid and usually priced as
  their own small block. Air sealing after drywall is just as universally excluded.
- Low-expansion foam at window and door perimeters. Carried by most full-package bidders and excluded by
  most foam specialists, who name it as by others. One foam specialist puts it in the base while its
  direct competitor holds the identical work as a priced alternate.
- Vapor retarders and their tape. Carried with the exterior wall batt.
- Parapet cavities. Almost always in the package, but the material is contested (see section 5).
- Thermal barrier and ignition barrier coatings over exposed spray foam. Always sold by whoever sprays
  the foam, because the code requires the pair.
- Acoustic and sound attenuation batt. The same batt in a different wall, and the reason Division 9 has
  a claim on it.
- Firestopping, sometimes. More often excluded (see section 5).

### Specialty carve-outs

Scope that leaves this package even though it is insulation:

- Rigid and continuous exterior insulation. Excluded outright by full-package batt bidders, who say so
  in the same sentence every time. It rides the cladding backup assembly and is bought in the siding
  package. A leveling sheet has routed a facade insulation row to siding explicitly.
- Rigid board under slabs on grade and at foundation walls. Bought as material from a construction
  accessories supplier and installed by whoever places the slab. A leveling sheet has routed it to the
  concrete package. The insulation contractor never touches it.
- Roof insulation above the deck. Excluded by name by insulation bidders; it belongs to roofing.
- Pipe and duct insulation. This is mechanical insulation, a different trade with different labor and a
  different section. It is not in this package and should not appear in this scope list. Note the
  boundary explicitly so nobody looks for it here.
- Duct sealing. Also mechanical, and confusing because it shares the words "air sealing" with envelope
  work and shares nothing else. It is priced per air-handling system and per site day.
- Structural thermal breaks at balcony and canopy connections. Thermal envelope scope living in Division
  5, taken off the structural sheets, priced per connection point and supplied as material to the
  miscellaneous metals contractor.
- Applied fireproofing, as its own section, even where the same company sprays both.

## 2. How the market bids

Proposal structure varies more in this trade than in any other. The same package can arrive as two
paragraphs and one number, or as forty takeoff rows carrying area, framing member, spacing, depth,
material, R-value and square feet per building. Both are normal.

The organizing grain, when there is one, is product family crossed with location. Underneath that,
bidders diverge:

- Full-package contractors segment into priced phases by scope family: roof cavities, thermal at
  exterior walls, garage or plenum ceiling, acoustic, acoustic within units, parapets, miscellaneous
  roof details. Phase sizes span four orders of magnitude on a single proposal, so phase count says
  nothing about where the money is. Inside each phase, every product is listed against a work area and,
  where the bidder has read the schedule, against a wall or ceiling mark. Prices attach to phases, never
  to marks.
- Spray specialists list locations with product, thickness and R-value, and give one number.
- One bidder segmenting by building is enough to make a project incomparable, because the full-package
  bidders on the same job segment by assembly family instead. Two orthogonal segmentations on one
  ranking cannot be reconciled without going back to both bidders.

What a proposal will usually state:

- Product family, thickness and R-value per location. This is the most reliable content in the trade and
  is far better than what most trades give you.
- The basis document with a date. Nearly always, and it is often not the set the folder implies.
- Wall, floor, ceiling and roof marks, in the full-package bids. Mark lists can run to thirty entries.
- Whether tax is included. Stated more often here than in most trades.
- The sheet or detail number for the small foam locations: roof drains, penetrations, anchors, sleeper
  curbs, dunnage flashing.

What a proposal will usually not state:

- Quantities. One genuine measured takeoff has been seen across the whole corpus. A second bidder lists
  square footages with no rates against them. Everyone else gives none.
- Labor market. Stated by a minority, and an estimator records it for every bidder regardless, because
  it is the largest single price driver.
- Insurance basis, controlled-insurance-program credits, and bond. These come back by email, quoted as a
  rate per thousand of contract value, and only after the general contractor asks.
- Warranty duration. Asked for and sometimes refused rather than priced.
- Who owns the airtightness or air barrier testing. Nobody carries it, and the silence is unanimous.
- Prevailing wage applicability, except as a negative.

### Menus, and why a base total is not an offer

Several bidders present a base total with adds and deducts below it, each with a line for the buyer to
initial. The base is the midpoint of a menu rather than the offer. One base total has been seen with
four adds and two deducts under it, two of the deducts being mutually exclusive answers to the same
assembly. A printed total that includes an optional phase is also common, and leveling that printed
total against another bidder's base overstates the bid.

The same warning applies in reverse. One bidder, offered two labor markets, prints two complete priced
configurations of one scope with no single bid; a captured price without its configuration is unusable.

### Revision habits

Revisions in this trade are usually document events rather than negotiation events, which is the
opposite of what several other trades do. Mechanisms seen:

- Scope entry as a price rise. The commonest by a distance. Between rounds the mark list grows, thermal
  and acoustic split further, new locations appear, and the number goes up. A four percent round-over-
  round move has been seen carrying a real thickness increase, a material change at the parapets and two
  new locations. Round deltas are a poor proxy for scope change here.
- Scope departure as a price fall. A whole phase can vanish between rounds with no note, taking a
  sixth of the bid with it, while the insulation specification at the exterior wall simultaneously goes
  up. Read the phases, not the totals.
- Product package substitution. See section 7; this is where the largest single move lives.
- Breakout restructure. A round where no money moves and one line is split into two. In this trade the
  restructure is often accompanied by an attached answered scope sheet, which is the real content.
- Package departure. A drywall bid becoming a standalone insulation bid. Never compute a delta across
  that boundary.
- Comeback absorption. A general contractor issues a written list of demands, typically five: hold
  pricing to a date and confirm current tariffs are covered, put a longer warranty in the base with a
  deduct for the shorter one, price the current set with value engineering listed separately, quote the
  insurance-program credit, and quote bond if the contract exceeds a threshold. The number comes back
  slightly lower with the tariff exposure absorbed, and all five answers live in the email rather than
  in the proposal.
- Alternate-only revision. Base byte-identical, a value engineering page appended.

Order rounds by the cited basis document, never by folder or letterhead. Bids priced off an earlier set
than their folder implies are common and are usually stated plainly on the last page.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Insulation scope sheets have run 18, 20, 21 and 41 rows. Target roughly thirty-five to fifty-five lines
for a full multifamily package. The thinnest sheets seen are the ones where the estimator had to invent
the largest plugs, because they had no row for the batt that might be in the drywall package and no row
for the coating over the foam.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Product family, always, and before anything else: batt, blown or dense-pack, mineral wool, spray
  polyurethane foam (open cell and closed cell separately), spray-applied fiber, rigid board, sheet air
  barrier, aerosol envelope sealing, canned foam. These do not substitute for one another at the same
  price and the drawings frequently do not say which is required.
- Thermal and acoustic as separate answers, even on adjacent walls in the same area. Every full-package
  bidder splits them, and the split is the most useful segmentation any bidder offers, because the
  acoustic block is where the discretionary scope lives.
- In-unit acoustic partitions as their own block, separate from the rest of the acoustic scope. Bedroom,
  bath, laundry and mechanical closet walls are frequently not called out on the drawings for insulation
  at all, and bidders handle that by pricing them as an option, folding them into a base and saying so in
  a covering email, or splitting them into a phase the general contractor can delete whole. In mineral
  wool rather than fiberglass those same walls have been quoted at over twice the price.
- Each sprayed or applied assembly, by name, with the product family stated. The garage or plenum ceiling
  over unconditioned space is the single most contested line in this trade and deserves a row that names
  what is being priced.
- The thermal barrier or ignition barrier coating over exposed foam, as its own line, distinct from the
  foam. It is a code requirement, not a finish, and the choice of coating product has been worth around a
  seventh of an entire insulation bid.
- Assembly marks from the wall, floor, ceiling and roof type schedules, grouped by product and thickness
  rather than listed individually. See what collapses, below.
- Exterior wall cavity insulation, separated by framing material where it changes: wood stud, metal stud
  and masonry back-up get different products even at the same R-value. One project puts mineral wool at
  the metal-framed level one and blown fiberglass on every level above it, on the same elevation.
- Parapet and stem wall cavities, with the material named.
- Rim joist, band joist and box sill, separately from the walls above and the floors below. This can be
  its own phase worth a tenth of a bid.
- Small foam locations as a group with the details listed: roof drains, roof penetrations, roof anchor
  details, sleeper curb bases, dunnage flashing. Individually tiny, collectively real, and each one is
  keyed to a specific detail.
- Ceiling insulation extent at basement, podium and amenity levels. Bidders routinely carry only the
  ceilings above the residential units and hold the rest as an optional phase, because the drawings do
  not settle it.
- Draft stop and air infiltration as one block, with its cutoff stated. Every bidder that carries it
  excludes air sealing after drywall, so the line has to say pre-rock.
- Window and door perimeter foam, its own line, because it sits in the base for some bidders and is a
  priced alternate for others.
- Vapor retarder and its tape.
- Shaft walls and prerock upgrades. These appear as new rows in later rounds, which means each one is a
  gap the earlier round exposed.
- Interstitial and non-combustible fill at floor cavities, if the project has them. This is a recurring
  alternate on multiple projects, sometimes framed against a fire protection tradeoff, and no bidder
  carries it in a base.
- Envelope airtightness, as a performance line with its target stated. Either a whole-building air
  changes per hour figure or a per-dwelling-unit compartmentalization figure, and the two are different
  scopes bought from different people.
- The air barrier itself, with its type stated: sheet membrane, fluid applied, or foam acting as the
  barrier.
- Testing and inspection, its own line, because the answer is currently nobody.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual wall marks carrying the same product at the same thickness within the same area. A bidder
  listing thirty marks is grouping them into four or five products underneath; mirror the products, cite
  the marks as attributes.
- Layer counts. Two layers of one batt to fill a cavity is one line, not two.
- R-value, facing and thickness. These are attributes of a product line. They vary continuously across
  the building and enumerating them explodes the sheet without distinguishing bidders.
- Room-by-room and unit-by-unit enumeration. No document seen prices a single room. Levels collapse into
  a level range unless the assembly actually changes at a level, which at the podium it usually does.
- The individual small foam details, once they are a named group.
- Mobilization and demobilization, unless the mobilization count is itself in dispute, in which case it
  belongs in section 4 as a qualification rather than as scope.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be silent about
it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In this trade the test fires
hardest on three things: which package carries the cavity batt, which product family answers each
sprayed assembly, and whether the code covering over the foam is in or out.

It also tells you when silence is a finding. Cavity insulation is silent-by-default work: nobody sees it
after the drywall goes up, and a bidder saying nothing about a wall type is much more likely to have
missed it than to have included it. Treat silence on a named assembly as a gap to surface, never as an
inclusion to assume.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs. Scope that no bidder reliably carries and that an estimator adds to every number
before comparing:

1. The cavity batt itself, whenever any bidder on the list is a spray-only or single-assembly
   specialist. This plug has been seen at two to three times the size of the spray bids it was added
   to, applied at a rule-of-thumb rate against the whole building area.
2. The thermal or ignition barrier coating over exposed foam, whenever a bidder priced foam and did not
   name the coating.
3. Rigid insulation at slab on grade, foundation perimeters and below-grade walls. Excluded by every
   full-package bidder seen.
4. Continuous or rigid exterior insulation at the cladding backup.
5. Non-combustible or interstitial floor cavity fill.
6. Envelope airtightness testing and air barrier inspection. Installers are certified, inspections are
   not carried, and thermal imaging and whole-building infiltration testing are excluded by name.
7. In-unit sound walls not called out on the drawings.
8. Window and door perimeter foam, whenever the low bidder is a foam specialist.
9. Firestopping, unless a bidder's optional firestop line was actually bought.
10. Ceilings at basement, podium and amenity levels beyond the residential footprint.
11. Insurance program credits and bond, which are never in the printed number.

A scope line set naming all eleven is doing the estimator's plugging in advance.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

| Excluded by the insulator | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| All rigid insulation, wherever it occurs (full-package bidders, near universal) | Siding or cladding package at the exterior; concrete package below slab; frequently unassigned in between |
| Roof insulation above the deck (commonly) | Roofing contractor |
| Draft stop, fire block, fire stop and fire rated caulking unless specifically listed (printed house terms on the franchise-branch forms) | Firestopping contractor, drywall, or distributed to each trade |
| Air infiltration sealing after drywall (every bidder that carries pre-rock air sealing) | Drywall contractor, or unassigned |
| Foam sealant around windows, gaps and door frames (most spray foam specialists) | Full-package insulator, window installer, or unassigned |
| Self-adhesive flashings and transition membranes (most spray foam specialists) | Air barrier installer or waterproofing |
| Through-wall flashings, counter flashings, metal back dams at sills (air barrier bidders) | Masons, typically |
| Primers, thermal barriers and vapor barriers, where the bidder is foam-only | Whoever carries the rest of the package |
| Intumescent or other thermal and ignition barrier coatings, on the stated ground that drywall will cover the foam (some foam bidders) | Nobody, correctly, where drywall does cover it; a real gap where it does not |
| Third party quality control, conformance testing, air leakage and infrared testing (near universal) | General contractor, and frequently unassigned |
| Trade association certification inspections (foam and air barrier bidders, universally) | Frequently unassigned |
| Shop drawings for sprayed foam, if required (foam bidders, universally) | Frequently unassigned |
| Mock-ups (near universal) | General contractor |
| Substrate preparation, priming and patching (spray-applied assembly bidders) | General contractor or the preceding trade |
| Heat, heat containment and ventilation (sprayed assembly bidders) | General contractor |
| Power, water and hookups (sprayed assembly bidders) | General contractor |
| Out of sequence work (sprayed assembly bidders) | Chargeable back to the general contractor |
| Permit, temporary heat, electric, dumpster and trash chute (the Division 9 crossover bidders) | General contractor |
| Police and fire details (commonly) | General contractor |
| Prevailing wage rates (some bidders, always as a negative) | Not applicable, or repriced |
| Naming the buyer as additional insured, wrap program participation, broad indemnity (the franchise-branch printed terms) | Unresolved, and a real commercial problem |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of that table is boilerplate. The franchise-branch forms carry the same printed terms on every
project, and two entirely unrelated competitors on one bid list have been seen reproducing an identical
optional firestop paragraph word for word, misspelling and all. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not
for the project.

What is project signal:

- An exclusion justified by absence from the drawings. One bidder excluded mineral wool outright on the
  stated ground that its locations could not be verified from the architectural plan set. That single
  sentence is a pre-written request for information and is the most valuable line in the document.
  Route it upstream rather than absorbing it at leveling.
- A conditional alternate framed as a question about the documents. A parapet priced as foam only if the
  parapets are foam and not mineral wool tells you the drawings do not settle the parapet material, and
  a competitor pricing the parapets as foam in its base without comment confirms it.
- An exclusion or inclusion naming a specific detail or sheet. Bidders that quote detail numbers have
  read the details.
- Any bidder stating that its scope is contingent on review of final construction drawings, when the set
  it priced is already a construction set. That is a bidder telling you it does not trust the set.
- A disclaimer that disappears between rounds while the price does not move. The exclusion left the
  printed page and stayed in the number. Leveling off the printed exclusion list will now miss it
  entirely. Level off the priced breakout instead.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the price into a change order.
Sprayed and applied assemblies carry far more of these than cavity work does, and every one of them is a
site condition invisible on a drawing.

- Minimum working area and substrate temperature, typically forty to forty-five degrees, with heat and
  containment by others. In a winter schedule this is not a formality.
- Electrical service, specified in volts, phase and amps, at named levels. Sprayed assemblies need
  dedicated power at every floor they work on.
- Staging adjacent to the building. One foam specialist states that three hundred feet of spray hose is
  its maximum reach, which decides where the truck parks and therefore whether parts of the building can
  be reached at all.
- Heated storage for equipment and drums through the winter.
- A named mobilization count, with a stated fee for each additional one. Bids have been seen at one, two
  and three mobilizations for comparable scope, and additional mobilization fees are quoted flat.
- Floors clear, vacated and unstocked, with no other trades present during application. One aerosol
  sealer requires the floor reserved for its event to be emptied the day before and charges by the hour,
  by role, when it is not.
- Proper construction sequence, which in this trade has teeth. See the sprayed-deck seam in section 5.
- Shaving sprayed foam flush to the stud face, which is real labor on a renovation and is sometimes an
  inclusion and sometimes not.
- Foam covered by a thermal barrier, typically drywall, assumed rather than verified. If the drywall
  does not arrive where the bidder assumed it would, the coating is an extra.
- Spray stops present around window and door openings before the foam arrives, by others.
- Metal studs braced, with the bidder disclaiming warping caused by expanding foam.
- Price validity windows of thirty to sixty days, and firm-price periods after which current pricing
  applies.
- Tariff, duty and trade-regulation escalation clauses, which have appeared recently on forms that did
  not carry them a round earlier, and raw-material escalation clauses triggered at a stated percentage
  move.
- Commercial terms that materially change what the number is worth: refusal to be named as additional
  insured, refusal to join a wrap insurance program, indemnity narrowed to the bidder's own negligence,
  a stated general liability limit, no retention permitted, and an explicit rejection of pay-when-paid.
  These sit in printed terms that nobody reads and they can be the reason a low bid is not buyable.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The drywall seam, which decides the package

Cavity insulation, sound attenuation blanket, pre-rock air sealing and often firestopping can all be
carried by the drywall contractor. On an answered scope sheet a drywall bidder has claimed, all with a
yes: provide all insulation and sound attenuation sealant where specified; acoustic batt insulation at
ceiling assemblies; glass fiber sound attenuation blanket; all insulation installed to a stated
installation grade; air sealing and compartmentalization throughout the building envelope; sealing
between gypsum board and studs at all walls; sealing between studs and sheathing at all openings;
firestopping; and fire caulking at all rated assemblies. Every one of those is claimed by a different
bidder on the same project.

Three consequences.

First, ask the question before you write the scope list, and write the answer as a line. A leveling
sheet that has to ask it later ends up carrying a rule-of-thumb batt plug across the entire building
area, which is a guess standing in for a decision.

Second, the price of the identical work depends on where it sits. Standalone, the same bidder at the
same labor basis has been seen at roughly twice its own in-package add.

Third, labor market runs per work group, not per proposal. A drywall bid can be union on framing, hang
and pre-rock and open shop on taping, patching and the insulation add. Insulation is often the open-shop
line inside an otherwise union package, and a leveling sheet with one labor column per bidder cannot
express that.

### The code covering over spray foam

Exposed spray polyurethane foam requires a thermal barrier, and in some conditions an ignition barrier
as well; a specification can call for both. Where drywall covers the foam, the drywall is the barrier and
no coating is needed, and a bidder will say so and exclude the coating. Where the foam stays exposed,
which is exactly the garage ceilings, plenums and soffits over unconditioned space, a sprayed or rolled
coating must go over it and the foam bidder sells it.

This is not a detail. The coating has its own thickness, its own R-value, its own product and its own
color, different bidders name three different products for it, and switching from a named product to an
approved alternative has been offered as value engineering worth roughly a seventh of an entire
insulation bid. Give the coating its own scope line, name the assembly it covers, and never let it ride
inside the foam line.

Note also that a suspended acoustical ceiling does not count as a thermal barrier. One bidder spells
that out, which means somebody has argued the point.

### The contested assembly: the ceiling over unconditioned space

The garage or plenum ceiling under conditioned space is answered by at least five different product
families, and they do not cost the same. Across one project's bid list the same assembly was priced as
spray-applied cellulose to a high R-value, as a proprietary spray-applied fiber, as open cell foam plus
intumescent coating, as closed cell foam plus a thermal barrier coating, and as an unnamed spray
insulation from a fireproofing applicator. Low to high across those answers ran to roughly three times.

The specification names one of them. Bidders substitute freely, sometimes refusing the named product
outright and offering their own as at least equal in any category. An estimator's value engineering
block on that project consisted entirely of the three substitutes, all presented as deducts against the
specified product, which tells you the specified product is the expensive answer and everybody knows it.

Write this assembly as its own scope line with the product named and a note that substitutions are
expected. Then read every proposal for which product it actually priced, because the printed total will
not tell you.

### Sprayed decks and the MEP sequencing fight

Every sprayed assembly under a deck, whatever the material, imposes the same condition: ducts, pipe,
conduit, equipment and non-structural walls must not be installed before the spray. Applicators state
that MEP hangers and clips should be installed first but must not extend more than about a foot below
the structure, and that work installed before the spray may prevent access to complete the work. One
applicator excludes any return to sprayed floors to reach inaccessible areas.

This is a scope gate rather than a qualification. Once the underside of the deck is populated, the
assembly can no longer be delivered as bid at any price, and it becomes patch work at a day rate. Put
the sequencing requirement on the scope line and coordinate it as a real constraint.

### Air sealing, air barriers, and who owns the test

Three distinct things travel under overlapping names:

1. Framing-stage air sealing. Canned and gasket foam at plates, jack studs, framing gaps, and door and
   window jambs. Carried by the full-package insulator, always bounded to before drywall.
2. The air barrier proper. A sheet or fluid membrane over the sheathing with flashings at openings,
   transitions, wall base and wall top. Bid as its own section by its own contractor. Closed cell spray
   foam can substitute, in which case the foam is the air barrier, the moisture barrier and the
   insulation at once, and it also displaces rigid board. Which of the two is being bought changes which
   trade carries the envelope.
3. Aerosol envelope sealing of the finished space, applied after firestop and before finishes, sealing
   to a measured airtightness target. Bought from an energy consultant or a licensed applicator. Priced
   per square foot of envelope or per dwelling unit.

The testing gap is total and worth stating plainly. Air barrier installers are certified, and then
exclude inspections. Foam bidders exclude all patching and testing. Sprayed assembly bidders exclude
testing and require the general contractor to schedule the independent testing agency. Whole-building
infiltration testing and thermal imaging are excluded by name. Nobody in this package owns the test that
proves the package worked. Carry it as its own scope line and assign it deliberately.

One more asymmetry. Aerosol sealing only closes what manual sealing left behind: penetrations over about
half an inch have to be repaired first, by others, and the applicator charges by the hour when they are
not. The scope line has to name who does the manual sealing.

### Firestopping

Genuinely contested, and answered three ways. Franchise-branch insulation forms exclude draft stop, fire
block, fire stop and fire rated caulking by printed house terms unless specifically listed. Other
insulation bidders offer firestopping as a detachable priced option, one per dwelling unit and one as a
lump, using identical boilerplate that excludes putty pads and collar ties. Drywall bidders claim it on
answered scope sheets. And a general contractor's own qualification on a leveling sheet has read that
firestopping is included with each trade rather than bought as a single-source package, knowingly not
meeting an insurer's single-source requirement.

So the answer is a project decision, not a trade convention. Ask which model is being used, and if it is
distributed, put a firestopping line on every trade's scope list rather than one line on this one.

### Continuous and rigid insulation, in two directions

Rigid board leaves this package twice and lands in two different places.

At the exterior, continuous insulation rides the cladding backup assembly and is bought in the siding
package. Insulation bidders exclude all rigid insulation in the same breath every time. A specification
can name a semi-rigid rainscreen mineral wool at cavity walls with a basis of design, and a leveling
sheet will still route that row to siding.

Below grade, rigid board under slabs on grade and at foundation walls is bought as material from a
construction accessories supplier alongside waterstop, vapor retarder and cure blanket, and it is
installed by the concrete trade. Its price is set by compressive class rather than by R-value: the
higher-strength board runs around a third to a half more per square foot than the standard board, so a
scope line reading only "two inch rigid" is unbiddable.

### Windows, doors and the perimeter

Perimeter foam at window and door openings sits in the base for full-package insulators and outside it
for most foam specialists. One foam specialist prices window, pocket and perimeter foam inside its base
while its direct competitor holds the identical work as a priced alternate worth around a sixth of its
base. Comparing those two base bids without moving that scope is a false comparison, and it is exactly
the kind of thing a printed total hides.

Related, and separate: interior-side caulking at window and door jambs along exterior walls has been
offered as an add sized in tubes rather than linear feet, and is not the same work as the foam.

### Structural thermal breaks

Thermal break pads at balcony, canopy and shelf angle connections are part of the thermal envelope and
are bought nowhere near this package. They are taken off the structural sheets, counted per connection
point, priced per plate, supplied as material and installed by miscellaneous metals.

They carry a documents trap worth knowing. Drawings routinely name a proprietary product and no material
type, and the material classes differ by around five times in price for the same plate size. A supplier
will assume the high strength composite used in bolted steel moment connections and note that a
structural foam pad, if acceptable, costs a fraction. That is an unresolved specification question worth
a request for information, not a bidder preference.

### Mechanical insulation, stated so nobody looks for it here

Pipe, duct and equipment insulation is a different trade in a different division with different labor.
It is not in this package. Duct sealing is likewise mechanical, priced per air handling system and per
site day against a duct leakage class. Both have been filed under insulation and air sealing tokens.
Note the boundary on the scope list and route them to mechanical.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

The set will give you assemblies, marks, R-values and details. It will not give you any of the
following, and each one moves the number.

What insulators actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The wall, floor, ceiling and roof assembly type schedules. This is the primary document. Marks come
   from here and the full-package bidders reproduce them at length.
2. The exterior wall type sheets and their section details, for the cavity depth, the framing material
   and the vapor retarder.
3. Named details for the small foam locations: roof drains, penetrations, anchors, curbs, parapets,
   soffits, rim and band joist conditions. These are cited by sheet and detail number more precisely
   than anything else in the trade.
4. The specification, for the acceptable product families, the R-values and the barrier requirements.
   Bidders read it selectively and substitute against it openly.
5. The reflected ceiling plans, for the extent of the sprayed ceiling assemblies. This is a known
   conflict: an estimator has asked its whole bid list whether they took the garage ceiling area off the
   floor details sheet or off the reflected ceiling plan, because the two give different areas.

Not deducible from any drawing:

- Whether the cavity insulation is in the drywall package. The largest single unknown in this trade.
- Labor market, per work group rather than per bidder. Union has been quoted at about 1.36 times open
  shop for byte-identical scope, stated on one page by one bidder. Prevailing wage is a third basis
  again, and one aerosol sealer publishes it at about 1.47 times its standard rate.
- Which product family answers each assembly where the specification permits more than one. A
  specification naming both fiberglass and mineral wool manufacturers as acceptable for sound
  attenuation batts means a bidder pricing everything as fiberglass is inside the specification, not
  deviating from it, and the price difference between the two on the same walls has been seen at over
  two times.
- Which thermal or ignition barrier coating product is required over exposed foam.
- Whether the in-unit sound walls are insulated at all. The drawings frequently do not call them out and
  bidders price them three different ways.
- Whether the interstitial floor cavities get non-combustible fill, and whether that fill is trading
  against a fire protection scope. On one project a general contractor asked its insulator by email what
  it would cost per square foot to fill the interstitial spaces if the sprinklers came out of them. That
  rate exists in no proposal.
- The energy program the project is chasing, and its target. A passive-house-standard certification
  drives a per-dwelling-unit compartmentalization requirement expressed in airflow per square foot of
  unit shell area, and changing that number changes the price. Certification, rating and rebate
  processing are separate services from the sealing itself.
- Who owns the airtightness test, the air barrier inspection, the thermal imaging and the third party
  conformance testing. Currently nobody.
- Site access, staging adjacency and hose reach for the spray rigs.
- Power availability by level, in volts, phase and amperage.
- Winter temperature and who provides the heat and containment.
- Mobilization count, and the loading dock, hoist or elevator the applicator needs.
- Whether the drywall will actually cover the foam where the foam bidder assumed it would.
- Insurance program credits, bond, payment portal fees and workforce compliance policies. All arrive by
  email at rates per thousand of contract value.
- Whether current tariffs are covered and for how long the price holds.
- Whether the mark vocabulary will survive. Wall type designations change wholesale between set issues:
  one mark in an earlier round becomes three differently-named marks in the next. Scope lines keyed to
  marks do not survive a re-issue unchanged, so key them to products and assemblies and carry the marks
  as attributes.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet of assembly area, by product and by R-value. The unit the whole trade prices in privately,
  and the one it almost never publishes. The single real takeoff seen breaks it down by area type,
  framing member, spacing, depth, material, R-value and building.
- Square feet split into a material rate and a labor rate. Seen exactly once, and only because the
  general contractor asked for a breakout by email. That split is the most useful cost data in this
  trade and it has to be requested.
- Square feet of envelope, for aerosol sealing.
- Dwelling units, for aerosol compartmentalization and for per-unit firestop options.
- Gross square feet of building, as the leveling denominator. See the warning below.
- Square feet of coating area, for the barrier over exposed foam, which is a different area from the foam
  itself.
- Each, per plate or per connection point, for structural thermal breaks.
- Linear feet, for waterstop and joint accessories bought alongside rigid board.
- Tubes, for the interior-side caulking add.
- Gallons, for aerosol sealant, capped in the base with an overage rate.
- Days and man-hours, for patching, delay and out-of-sequence work. Several bidders publish an hourly
  rate by role.
- Mobilizations, each with a flat fee.
- Percent or per thousand of contract value, for bond, general liability, insurance program credits and
  payment portal fees.

### A warning about the denominator

Every project in this corpus normalized to a different quantity, and none of them is interchangeable:
gross square feet; gross square feet net of an excluded tenant area; square feet of wood framed area.
On one project the ranking denominator did not reconcile with the sheet's own bid-form breakout
quantities. A cost per square foot from this trade is meaningless without its basis, and a cross-project
comparison without matching bases is not a comparison.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Whether the cavity insulation is in or out of the drywall package. Roughly a factor of two on the
   same scope from the same bidder.
2. Product family per assembly, particularly on the sprayed ceiling over unconditioned space. Roughly a
   factor of three across the answers to one assembly on one project.
3. Mineral wool versus fiberglass across the interior and demising walls. One bidder priced mineral wool
   throughout with a standing note that unfaced batts would cost less, came back a round later having
   made the swap, and dropped about twenty-two percent while its exterior wall specification went up.
   Another offered the same walls as two mutually exclusive options differing by over two times.
4. Labor market, per work group. About thirty-six percent union over open shop for identical scope; a
   prevailing wage basis higher again.
5. Total assembly area, which follows unit count and the amount of amenity and back of house space.
6. Whether the thermal or ignition barrier coating is in, and which product. Around a seventh of a whole
   bid on the product choice alone.
7. R-value and thickness, which is where value engineering usually lands. Reducing the between-floor
   fiberglass depth and shaving an inch off the slab-deck foam were offered together as the two value
   engineering items on one project. Both reduce a specified R-value and neither was flagged as a
   deviation, which is a compliance drop wearing a saving's clothes.
8. Whether the in-unit sound walls are in.
9. Scope breadth, meaning how much of the package the bidder carries at all. This produces the widest
   apparent spreads and none of it is competitiveness.
10. Access, staging, hose reach, power and mobilization count.
11. Compressive class on rigid board, where rigid is in scope.
12. Winter work, heat and containment.
13. Bond, insurance basis, tariffs and portal fees.

### On dispersion

Within a single project the spread between the lowest and highest whole-package bid has been seen at
about 1.3 times and about 2.3 times. Rankings that mix scope breadths have shown twelve times, and that
figure is an artifact rather than a price. On directly comparable narrow scopes, spray foam at the same
walls on one project, three bidders ran about 1.8 times low to high.

The top of an insulation range is usually a bidder who priced a different product, not a bidder who is
expensive, and unlike most trades this one will often tell you so on the face of the proposal. Read the
product columns before reading the totals.

Bidder counts per project have run from four to nine, which is a much shallower list than most trades,
and a good part of any list is not comparable to the rest of it.

Finally, an estimator does not compare base bids here either. It compares base bid plus plugs, and in
this trade the plugs can exceed the bid: adding the cavity batt to a spray-only bidder at a rule-of-thumb
rate against the whole building area has been seen at two to three times that bidder's own number. Any
scope line set that names the eleven items in section 3 is doing that work in advance.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly forty insulation, air-sealing and air-barrier proposals plus four
estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all
bid by one general contractor. It includes three ground-up wood and concrete projects and one renovation
of an existing high-rise. The proposals span design development, permit, construction document and
conformed rounds, several same-bidder revision pairs, and one four-round ladder. Several bidders appear
on more than one project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in
section 4 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work in a cold northern climate, in one market,
  through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors, institutional,
  healthcare, industrial, warm-climate assemblies or public prevailing-wage work.
- Only one project involved renovation, so the existing-conditions observations rest on that project
  alone. Cavity depth variation, shaving labor and access by elevator all come from it.
- Only one proposal in the whole corpus contained a genuine measured takeoff, and only one bidder ever
  split material from labor. Every square-foot rate in section 7 rests on those two documents.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is present, so nothing here shows which exclusions survived
  negotiation, whether the cavity batt ended up in the drywall package on any of these projects, or what
  the thermal barrier coating was actually bought as.
- No change order history is present. The claim that the sequencing and access qualifications convert
  into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed in the field.
- The rigid and continuous insulation boundary is established from exclusions and from leveling-sheet
  routing rows rather than from any bid that actually carried it. What continuous exterior insulation
  costs inside a cladding package is not visible from here.
- No project in this corpus used a fluid-applied air barrier, spray cellulose in a wall cavity, vacuum
  insulated panels, or any below-grade insulated foundation system beyond flat rigid board.
- Firestopping is seen only as an optional line on insulation proposals and as a claimed row on a
  drywall scope sheet. No dedicated firestopping proposal was read, so the boundary is described from
  the insulation side only.
- Airtightness and air barrier testing is described entirely from exclusions. No test report, no
  commissioning scope and no evidence of who was eventually paid to do it appears anywhere.
- Energy program requirements appear on one project only, as a passive-house compartmentalization target
  and a certification pursuit. How those requirements shape the insulation package on a project chasing
  a different program is untested.
