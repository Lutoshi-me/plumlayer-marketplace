# Fireproofing

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the fireproofing
package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets.
This file speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. Fireproofing is a small package that behaves like a large one.
The dollars are modest and the scope is a handful of members, but almost nothing that decides the
number is drawn: which of two incompatible products applies, whether the steel arrives primed,
whether the assembly is restrained, who repairs the coating after the pipefitters knock it off. A
scope line that names only a member and a rating is unbiddable.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Section 078100, Applied Fireproofing, is the anchor and nearly every proposal names it. It is a
reliable identifier, unlike the trade token on the file.

The package holds two products that are not variants of each other:

- Sprayed fire resistive material, the cementitious or gypsum-based spray. Thick, textured, opaque,
  cheap per member, applied to concealed steel. Written as SFRM.
- Intumescent, the thin-film coating that swells under fire. Thin, paintable, expensive per member,
  applied to steel that will be seen. Written as IFRM.

They demand opposite substrates, they are sold by partly different companies, and on the same members
the intumescent price can run several times the cementitious price. A bidder that prices one and is
silent on the other has not offered a cheaper package; it has offered half a package. Expect to buy
the two halves from two companies and expect the leveling sheet to plug the missing half from a
competitor's number, because a bidder carrying both in one proposal is unusual.

Establish the kind of company before comparing anything. Five kinds bid this work:

- The dedicated fireproofing applicator, often carrying national certification for both product
  families. Reads the structural set, returns a marked-up takeoff, prices restrained assemblies.
- The coatings and linings applicator, which sells fireproofing, intumescent and sprayed thermal
  insulation off the same rig and will happily put all three in one proposal under a fireproofing
  heading.
- The commercial painting contractor with a fireproofing division. This is where intumescent
  reliably lands, and it is worth knowing, because painters bidding the paint package exclude
  intumescent about half the time and carry it never.
- The plasterer with a fireproofing line, usually a one or two person operation quoting a few
  sentences by email.
- The insulation installer with a fireproofing line, whose commercial terms are often a
  pre-printed work agreement rather than a proposal.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package from elsewhere:

- Sprayed thermal and acoustic insulation. Same spray rig, same staging demands, same access and
  sequencing conditions, same patching day rate, frequently the same crew. A proposal titled
  fireproofing can carry a spray insulation line several times larger than both fireproofing lines
  combined. Read the breakout before you read the total.
- Sprayed acoustic finish at exposed decks and soffits, for the same reason.
- Existing fireproofing on renovation work, which nobody wants and which arrives as a patching
  allowance rather than as scope.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like fireproofing:

- Firestopping and fire-safing. Excluded by every fireproofing bidder that mentions it. Section
  078400 and its successors 078413 and 078443 are a separate package with separate bidders, separate
  certification requirements and a separate normalizing denominator. Do not merge them.
- Fire blocking and draft stopping. By others in every firestopping proposal seen, and generally a
  framing carpentry item, though nobody claims it out loud.
- Fire rated expansion joints, which follow the expansion joint supplier.
- Sealers, topcoats and any decorative finish over intumescent. Excluded by every intumescent bidder.
  The coating leaves the applicator as a sprayed orange peel texture and nothing more.
- Concrete encasement of columns, which is a concrete scope that silently removes members from this
  package. Two bidders on one project independently found garage columns already encased and deleted
  them; the ones who did not find it were pricing air.

## 2. How the market bids

One number. Segmentation appears only when the proposal carries more than one product, and then it
segments by product class, not by area or member.

What a proposal reliably states, and this is unusually rich for a lump sum trade:

- The product class and a named proprietary material, nearly always. The material name is the single
  most load bearing word in the document, because it determines whether the substrate must be primed
  or bare.
- The hourly rating, always, and often more than one rating in a single sentence. Three ratings in
  one proposal is not rare on a mixed-use building: one for the floor framing, another for the roof,
  a third inside a mechanical vault.
- The member classes: primary beams, secondary beams, columns, braces, deck. These are priced
  differently because required thickness depends on the member's weight and shape, so a scope line
  that says structural steel is not a scope line.
- The structural sheets it took the scope off, by sheet number. This is the trade's actual scope
  statement and it is far more precise than anything the exclusion list contains. Some bidders list
  a dozen sheets across life safety, architectural and structural.
- Restrained or unrestrained. Usually one word, sometimes buried in a house standards page as an
  unless-otherwise-noted default. It changes required thickness materially and therefore the price.
  One bidder assumed unrestrained where every other bidder assumed restrained, and nothing on the
  leveling sheet would surface that.
- Mobilization count, with a per-mobilization rate for additional ones. Repeat bidders carry the
  same rate across projects and years.
- Labor type and sales tax treatment.

What a proposal will not state:

- Quantities. Almost never priced by the each, the linear foot or the pound.
- Thickness, usually. When it does appear it is in an attachment, not the letter.
- Any acknowledgement that the two product families exist, unless the bidder sells both.
- A go-back, punch or patching allowance in the base bid. Patching is excluded by essentially every
  bidder and offered, when offered at all, as a crew day rate.

The attachment is where the real information lives, and it is easy to miss because it is image only
and yields no extracted text. Two forms recur. The common one is a marked-up copy of the architect's
own structural framing plan with every sprayed member highlighted and its endpoints noded, one page
per framing sheet: a scope map with no numbers on it. The rarer and far more valuable one is a
schedule listing every steel shape on every sheet with its assigned test design, its hours and its
required thickness, so that a wide flange at three hours takes over an inch while a heavier shape at
the same rating takes three quarters, and the same shape at two hours takes half. That schedule is
often produced by a third party fireproofing estimating service rather than by the bidder, which
means competitors may be buying the same takeoff and their spread is then pure markup and labor.

Email-only proposals are normal here and they are competitive, not fringe. A three sentence email
with no letterhead, no exclusions and no conditions has ranked mid-field against formal proposals and
has come within a modest margin of the selected bid. Treat an email as a real proposal, and treat a
one line reply saying to retain the previous pricing as a real revision.

Exclusion lists are house boilerplate. Repeat bidders reproduce the same list verbatim across
projects and years, and competitors reproduce each other's phrasing. Level off the priced or narrated
scope, never off the exclusion list. Documents that contradict themselves are common enough to expect:
a base bid including columns and braces sitting above an exclusion list excluding columns, or a
proposal assuming the steel is primed in one paragraph and unprimed in the next.

Revision habits:

- Scope entry with a price rise dominates. A conformed-set round more than doubled a number whose
  scope sentence changed by two words, adding columns to a list that had said beams. Nothing about
  the total explains that, and diffing exclusion lists would show nothing either.
- The trigger is almost always the general contractor's own scope sheet growing. Where the sheet
  went from one row to a dozen between rounds, every bidder's narrative went from a line to a page,
  and one bidder's number rose about a sixth.
- No-change confirmations by email are accepted as the revised bid, and get filed as if they were a
  new proposal in the new round's folder. Counting files counts them twice.
- A price falling between rounds by a third, with no stated reason, also occurs.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Say up front which of two regimes applies, because they enumerate differently.

New construction is the podium regime: steel framing beneath wood or above a garage, a horizontal
separation rating driven by construction type, a well-bounded set of members on two or three
structural sheets. Scope is defined by member class and rating.

Renovation and infill is the existing-fireproofing regime: new steel, reinforced existing steel, and
existing steel at infills, all of which must match an existing material and meet the code provisions
the existing building was built to. Scope here is defined by location and by condition, and the
largest single number on the sheet is usually patching, not application.

Scope sheets have run one, five, twelve and twenty-four rows. One row cannot distinguish a bidder who
priced intumescent from one who did not. Target fifteen to twenty-five rows for a real package. The
twelve-row and twenty-four-row sheets produced the two tightest spreads and the two award decisions
that survived scrutiny; the one-row and five-row sheets produced the widest.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Product class, as separate rows: sprayed fire resistive material, intumescent, and any
  high-insulating-value or proprietary exposed-finish coating the drawings call for. Every bidder
  answers these independently and several answer no to two of the three.
- Rating, wherever it changes. Three-hour primary framing and two-hour secondary framing in the same
  bay is normal and it is two rows, because the thickness and therefore the cost differ. Ratings also
  change by level, by zone and inside mechanical or vault spaces.
- Member class: primary beam, secondary beam, column, brace, deck, each its own row. The distinction
  between primary and secondary carries a full rating step on some projects. Braces are the row most
  often left blank by bidders, and blank is not no.
- Underside of metal deck as its own row, always. It is excluded by nearly every bidder, and by name.
  A rating achieved on the beams alone is not the rating the assembly needs if the deck was assumed
  in the test design.
- Location, keyed to structural sheet number rather than to floor name. Bidders answer sheet by sheet
  and their silences become visible only at that grain. One bidder left a nine-floor block
  unanswered and was still selected, because nobody read the blank cell.
- Vertical extent for columns, separately from their presence. A column sprayed from sub-basement to
  level two and a column sprayed at one framing level are the same row on a naive sheet and are not
  the same scope.
- Material density or durability grade. Standard, medium and low density are different products at
  different prices, and a bidder may use two grades on one job, putting the tougher grade where the
  steel is exposed to abuse in a garage and the standard grade where it is concealed.
- Substrate condition, meaning whether the steel arrives primed, painted or bare. See section 5. This
  row is the one most likely to be missing and most likely to become a change order.
- Patching, split into two rows: repair of new fireproofing damaged by other trades, and repair or
  extension of existing fireproofing on renovation work. Both are excluded by essentially every
  bidder and both must carry a stated day-rate basis.
- Testing and third party inspection, its own row, naming who pays. Excluded by every bidder seen.
- Surface preparation and primer removal, its own row. Excluded by every bidder seen, and it is the
  row that pays for the fabricator's decision.
- Overspray removal from decks and adjacent surfaces, its own row. A beam-only application will put
  material on the deck, and at least one bidder excludes cleaning it while including general
  cleaning below ceiling level, which is a distinction nobody notices until the floor is scraped.
- Heat, enclosure and ventilation, its own row. Excluded universally and genuinely expensive in
  winter, because the material needs a minimum temperature held for a day or two either side of
  application.
- Mobilizations beyond the first, with the stated per-mobilization rate.
- Members already protected by other means, most often concrete-encased columns. A row that says so
  explicitly stops half the bid list pricing them.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual members. No document prices a single beam. The shape-by-thickness schedule is an
  internal calculation, not a leveling grain.
- Thickness. It is an attribute of a member class at a rating, not a row. It moves inversely to
  member weight, which is arithmetic the bidder does, not a scope decision.
- The test design number. An attribute of the assembly.
- Texture, finish and appearance, unless the steel is exposed and the architect expects something
  other than sprayed orange peel, in which case that expectation belongs on the topcoat row rather
  than the fireproofing row.
- Floors within one rating zone.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be silent
about it, and the three outcomes produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied here the test fires
hardest on the seams in section 5, on the two product classes, and on patching, which is why those
get rows even though the drawings show none of them.

Silence is the dominant failure mode in this trade because the proposals are short. A bidder that
does not mention intumescent on a project where the specification calls for it has not excluded it
and has not included it, and its number is not comparable to anyone's. Where the estimator's own cell
on a row is a question mark, that row is an RFI, not a leveling problem.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs an estimator adds to every bidder before comparing, so a scope line set naming
them is doing the plugging in advance:

1. Patching of new fireproofing damaged by other trades, at a crew day rate.
2. Patching or repair of existing fireproofing on renovation work, at a crew day rate, in quantity.
3. Third party thickness, density and adhesion testing.
4. Surface preparation, primer removal, lath or mechanical bond where the steel arrived coated.
5. The second product family, wherever the package needs both and no bidder carries both.
6. Underside of metal deck.
7. Overspray removal from decks and adjacent surfaces.
8. Heat, temporary enclosure and ventilation during cold weather application.
9. Topcoats and sealers over intumescent.
10. Additional mobilizations, which the schedule will demand and the base bid will not include.

On new construction the patching plug runs a few percent of a typical base. On renovation the two
patching rows together can exceed the lowest base bid on the sheet and approach half the highest.
That is the single most important number in this trade: on existing buildings, patching is not a
contingency, it is the package.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of both tables is boilerplate reproduced verbatim. What is project signal, because it was written
for this set:

- A named material stated as the basis, together with a rating. That pairing is a real reading.
- A cited list of structural sheet numbers, which tells you which drawings the bidder actually opened
  and, by omission, which it did not.
- The word restrained or unrestrained.
- A bidder saying it found no other fireproofing on the drawings, or that it did not see any spray
  fireproofing at all. On one project a bidder reported finding neither the structural steel nor the
  fireproofing until the general contractor named the exact detail and the exact sheet note. When
  bidders cannot find the scope, the scope is not shown, and that is a documents problem to route
  upstream rather than a bidder to discount.
- A bidder taking exception to a specific detail, disclosing that the specified material cannot meet
  a stated requirement, or noting that the specification does not match the details.
- A bidder pricing a different product than the one specified and saying so.
- A member count, a grid line range or a vertical extent, all of which mean the bidder counted.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

Each of these is a condition attached to the number, and violating it converts the lump sum into a
change order.

- Restrained assembly assumed unless otherwise noted. Unrestrained requires more material.
- One mobilization, single phase, with a stated rate for each additional one. This trade sprays a
  floor once and leaves; every return trip is priced.
- Steel must be freely accessible, with no ducts, pipes, conduits, equipment, partitions or exterior
  sheathing in the way, and floors clear of stocked material so scaffolds and lifts move freely.
  Several bidders state there is no allowance to return to a sprayed floor to reach steel that was
  blocked, at any price.
- Hangers and clips for all services must be installed before spraying, and one bidder bounds them to
  not more than a foot below the underside of structure. Anything hung afterwards means patching,
  which is excluded.
- No concealing construction until after application, inspection and testing.
- Minimum temperature, commonly forty degrees, maintained for a day or two before, during and after
  application, with the general contractor responsible for holding it. One bidder states that if heat
  and enclosures are not provided, the other trades must stop so the MEPs do not get installed first.
- Floors above poured and cured; roofs dried in, loaded and free of roof traffic for a period after
  application on metal deck.
- Specific utility service at the staging location: a dedicated water source not shared with other
  trades, and single phase power at a stated voltage and amperage within a stated distance, with
  hookup by others.
- A permanent staging location for the spray rig and a trailer, with relocation charged as time and
  material.
- For warranty validity, one bidder requires that all patching be performed by it, which means the
  patching money cannot be given to anyone else even though it is excluded from the base.
- On renovation work, a site visit to verify existing conditions before contract acceptance.

Commercial terms occasionally decide the buy before scope does. One bidder's pre-printed work
agreement refuses, unless separately paid for, to name the general contractor as additional insured,
to provide per-project aggregate coverage, to join an owner controlled insurance program, or to
indemnify beyond a narrow clause, and requires payment in full regardless of any pay-when-paid
arrangement. None of that reaches a leveling sheet, and it would disqualify the bid at subcontract.
Read the terms page.

Two firestopping instruments have no leveling column anywhere and should be captured explicitly. One
bidder's proposal is void if a particular inspection firm is appointed, which is a bidder vetoing the
owner's inspector. Another requires notification by email the day after the general contractor
submits its bid, or the price lapses. Both are award conditions, not qualifications.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### The shop primer seam, which decides more than anything else

The two products require opposite substrates, and this is the fact the drawings never carry:

- Sprayed fire resistive material needs bare steel. Primers, paint, mill scale, roll oils and
  lubricants all defeat the bond. If the steel arrives coated, the fix is lath or a mechanical bond,
  which is excluded by every bidder.
- Intumescent needs shop primed steel, with a primer compatible with the coating. Unprimed steel, or
  an incompatible primer, means additional cost.

So the decision is made in the steel fabricator's shop months before the fireproofer mobilizes, it is
made per member, and the fireproofing package cannot fix it. Any scope line set must carry a row
naming which members are shop primed and with what, and the steel package must carry the mirror.

Expect the documents themselves to be confused about this. A single proposal has been seen assuming
primed steel in its scope paragraph and unprimed steel two bullets later, because the bidder's
standard terms were written for one product and pasted into a proposal for the other. The clearest
statement in the market comes from a bidder that spells out both rules side by side. When a proposal
states only one of the two, check which product it is actually pricing.

### Patching, the recurring orphan

Nobody carries it, everybody excludes it, and it is the largest plug on a renovation sheet. The
sequence is unavoidable: the fireproofer sprays, then the MEP trades hang from the steel and knock
material off, then somebody repairs it. The market's three answers are all different. Some bidders
exclude patching outright. One states that the general contractor shall pay for repair of fireproofing
damaged by others, which is an inclusion of the labor and an exclusion of the cost. One excludes it
from the base and then requires that it be performed only by them for the warranty to hold. Price it
as crew days, not as area, because that is the only basis any bidder offers and it is the basis the
estimator uses. Rates for a small crew including equipment and material cluster closely across
bidders, so the quantity of days is the variable, not the rate.

### Testing and inspection

Excluded by every bidder and paid by the owner or general contractor. Several bidders demand advance
notice of scheduled tests, the right to receive copies of results, and the right to correct
deficiencies before demobilizing an area. That last one is a schedule dependency: an area cannot be
closed until it is tested, and the crew cannot leave until deficiencies are corrected, so a slow
inspector directly buys additional mobilizations.

### MEP hangers and clips

Every proposal requires them installed before spraying and requires ducts, pipes, conduits, equipment
and non-structural partitions installed after. This is the tightest sequencing constraint in the
package and it is a coordination row on the general contractor's schedule, not a scope row.

### Concrete encasement and other passive protection

Columns encased in concrete need no spray. Two bidders on one project found this independently and
deducted the garage columns; others did not and priced them. Wherever the structural set shows
encasement, fill or a rated enclosure, that member leaves this package, and the scope line should say
so rather than leaving each bidder to discover it.

### Roofing and the roof deck

Roofs must be dried in and loaded before spray begins beneath them, and free of roof traffic for a
period afterwards on metal deck. Separately, a change to the roof insulation can change the
fireproofing required to hold the assembly rating, which means another trade's material decision
lands in this package's thickness.

### Firestopping, the adjacent package

Firestopping is a genuinely separate scope and this is where the general contractor's own ruling
matters. On more than one project the single-source firestopping bids were collected, priced against
a distributed alternative row by row, and set aside: the work was bought with each trade instead, with
the general contractor writing into its qualifications to the owner that it includes firestopping
with the trades and does not include single source firestopping per the insurer's requirements. On
the owner-facing bid form the firestopping lines carry the words with the trades where a number
should be.

Three consequences for a reader. First, do not assume a firestopping package exists; check whether
the trade even has a leveling tab. Second, when the work is distributed, each trade's scope must
carry its own firestopping row or the work is nowhere, and the exclusions in the table above become
real gaps rather than boundary lines. Third, the specification's single-source and certification
requirements, which usually appear in a section 1.6 and which certified bidders lead with, are
knowingly deviated from. That deviation is a decision worth surfacing, not a filing error.

Note also that firestopping normalizes on gross building area while fireproofing normalizes on podium
area or on units. Two adjacent trades in the same division, levelled on incomparable bases.

### Sprayed thermal and acoustic insulation

The same applicators, the same equipment, the same staging and access and sequencing conditions, the
same patching day rate, and frequently the same proposal. Where a fireproofing proposal carries a
spray insulation line, the insulation is often the great majority of the total, and ranking the
printed total against fireproofing-only bids overstates that bidder several times over. Read the
breakout, never the total.

The confusion also runs the other way in the documents. An architectural detail requiring an
insulating value per inch from the fireproofing produced three irreconcilable readings on one bid
list: one bidder said a single named product is the only one that meets it and priced it, one took
formal exception saying nothing specified meets it, and one excluded high-insulating-value
fireproofing outright. The estimator's own cell on that row was a question mark. That is an RFI to
the architect about whether the fireproofing is being asked to do the insulation's job, and it should
never be levelled.

### Painting and finishes

Intumescent leaves the applicator as a sprayed orange peel texture, explicitly with no tamping, no
rolling and no strike lines. Every intumescent bidder excludes sealers, topcoats and decorative
finishes. Painters exclude intumescent and high performance coatings about half the time and carry
them essentially never. So on exposed architectural steel there are two gaps in a row: the coating's
appearance and the finish over it. If the architect expects a smooth painted column, that expectation
belongs to neither package as currently bid.

### Drywall and concealing construction

No enclosing or concealing construction until the material is applied, inspected and tested. On the
other side, an exclusion of a thermal barrier or a rating on the grounds that another assembly
provides it is only valid if that other package carries it.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What fireproofers actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The structural framing plans, sheet by sheet. This is the primary document and bidders name the
   sheets. Member sizes, member roles and locations come from here. A set whose beam sizes are
   incomplete produces budget pricing and says so.
2. The code or life safety sheet, for the rating requirements and the construction type. The rating
   is a code consequence, not a specification choice: a three hour horizontal separation exists
   because of the construction type and the podium, and reading the specification alone will not tell
   you that.
3. Architectural details and general sheets, for the exposed conditions that drive intumescent, for
   the assembly requirements, and occasionally for a stray requirement the fireproofing cannot meet.
4. The specification, mostly for the section number. Bidders cite it and rarely quote it, and at
   least one has priced a different product family than the specification names, disclosed it, and
   turned out to be right when an RFI later confirmed no intumescent was required.

Watch for the rating schedule conflict. Where the structural sheets and the architectural or code
sheets carry rating information separately, they can disagree, and bidders will price different
sides. That is a documents conflict to route as an RFI, never a leveling problem to absorb. On the
projects that ran RFIs on this trade, the answers became scope rows: no intumescent required, and a
deduct for steel at a named space between named column lines.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Whether the steel arrives shop primed, and with which primer.
- Restrained or unrestrained assumption.
- Material density or durability grade, which the specification may leave to the applicator.
- Whether spray can proceed in one uninterrupted mobilization, and how many mobilizations the real
  schedule will demand.
- Whether the floors will genuinely be clear and the other trades genuinely held back.
- Winter conditions and who provides heat, enclosure and ventilation.
- How much patching the job will actually generate, which no drawing predicts and which dominates the
  renovation case.
- Who pays for testing, how quickly the inspector turns around, and whether a slow inspector adds
  mobilizations.
- Existing conditions on renovation work: what the existing material is, whether it contains anything
  requiring abatement, and how far the damage extends beyond the drawn work. Bidders handle this by
  requiring a site visit before contract acceptance and by disclaiming responsibility for existing
  fireproofing entirely.
- Labor type. Union and open shop bidders compete here, and the union bidder is not reliably the
  highest, which makes this trade unlike the finishing trades.
- Whether an owner controlled insurance program applies. The credits are a fraction of one percent on
  a package this small, so this is a rounding question here, not a driver.
- Warranty term. One bidder stated flatly that one year and two years cost the same, which is worth
  knowing before anybody prices a warranty alternate.

Set-stage behavior. At design development the drawings are commonly too thin for this trade and
bidders say so in the proposal, calling the price an estimate drawn from incomplete drawings, or
budget pricing due to incomplete beam sizes. Treat a design development fireproofing number as an
order of magnitude. By construction documents and conformed sets the same bidders switch to naming
sheets, grid line ranges, vertical extents and specific unresolved details. Note also that a
proposal's cited basis date and its folder's set stage disagree often enough to matter; order rounds
by the basis document the proposal cites, never by the date on the letterhead.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- The steel member, classified by shape, role and rating. This is the real unit and it is almost
  never priced individually.
- Thickness in inches and fractions, assigned per shape at a given rating, and moving inversely to
  member weight: a light wide flange at three hours can need over an inch while a heavy one at the
  same rating needs three quarters, and the same shape at two hours needs about half. This
  relationship is the trade's core arithmetic and it appears on no drawing.
- Square feet of podium or of protected framing area, which is the sensible denominator on new
  construction because the fireproofing exists only where the steel does. Gross building area is the
  wrong denominator for this trade and produces meaningless rates.
- Residential units, used as the denominator on projects where every other package is normalized that
  way, even though unit count has no relationship at all to how much steel needs spraying. Know which
  denominator a rate was computed on before comparing across projects.
- Crew days, for patching and for repair of existing, always including equipment and material.
- Each, for mobilizations.
- For firestopping specifically: the residential unit, with a stated number of mobilizations per unit
  covering in-wall penetrations, pre-rock, mechanical closets and punch; the outlet putty pad, priced
  per each beyond an included count of a few per unit; and gross building area as the leveling
  denominator. Maximum annular space and maximum joint width are the real pricing basis and appear on
  no leveling sheet anywhere.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Which product family applies. On the same members, intumescent has run several times the
   cementitious price, and a bidder substituting one for the other has produced a number an order of
   magnitude apart from its competitors. Nothing else in this trade moves money like this.
2. Rating, and how many rating zones the building has. A rating step changes thickness, and thickness
   is material and labor together.
3. Restrained versus unrestrained.
4. On renovation, the extent of existing fireproofing to patch, repair and match.
5. Member count and member weight distribution, which is the actual quantity and which only a takeoff
   reveals.
6. Whether the steel arrives primed.
7. Sequencing and access. Every bidder prices one clean uninterrupted pass and charges time and
   material for anything else, so a schedule that fragments the work rewrites the number.
8. Winter conditions and who holds the temperature.
9. Density or durability grade.
10. Mobilization count.
11. Labor type, which matters less here than in the finishing trades.
12. Testing turnaround, indirectly, through mobilizations.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within a single project the spread across
genuine fireproofing bidders has run from about one and a half times low to high on a well-specified
package, to about two and a fifth, to about three times. The tight end sits on the most detailed
scope sheet and the wide end on the thinnest, which is the pattern worth remembering: scope definition
compresses spread more than competition does. Bidder counts per project run from three to six, which
is a short list by any standard, and one project drew no fireproofing package at all.

Two warnings about reading a ranking in this trade. First, a printed total may not be a fireproofing
base bid: a proposal on a fireproofing letterhead has carried spray insulation as the great majority
of its total, and levelling that total would have overstated the bidder about sevenfold. Second, a
ranking's own rows lie: firestopping proposals misfiled under the fireproofing token have sat at the
top of a fireproofing ranking at several times the highest real bid, a Division 10 supplier has sat
in the same ranking, and the same bidder's two rounds have appeared as two ranking rows. Count
companies from the documents, not from ranking rows.

One more convention. The award in this trade does not reliably follow the low number. On the projects
where the scope sheet was thin, the selected bidder was the highest of its round, chosen because it
matched the specified product and answered the commercial questions; on the project where the sheet
had grown to a dozen rows, the low bidder won, because the sheet finally proved the low number
covered the same work. The lowest number on a thin sheet is usually a bidder who priced a different
rating, a different product, or half the members.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly two dozen fireproofing and firestopping proposals plus one
adjacent sprayed insulation quote, and on five estimator leveling tabs across four projects in one
regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The projects span design development, permit,
construction documents and conformed sets, and three of the four are new mid-rise residential or
mixed-use construction over a steel podium; the fourth is a renovation of an existing office building
to residential, which is the sole basis for everything said here about existing fireproofing. Several
bidders appear on three of the four projects, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal
distinction in section 4 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from mid-rise residential and mixed-use work in one market through one
  general contractor. Nothing is tested against high-rise steel frame construction, where fireproofing
  is a major package rather than a minor one, nor against industrial, institutional, healthcare or
  public prevailing-wage work.
- The packages seen are small: a lobby, a stair, a podium, a set of infills. Nothing here is
  calibrated on a project where fireproofing runs the full height of a steel building, and the
  sequencing, mobilization and testing economics of that case are likely to differ substantially.
- Only one proposal carried a shape-by-thickness schedule, so every thickness convention in section 7
  rests on that single document plus one partial corroboration.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is present, so nothing shows which exclusions survived
  negotiation. In particular, whether the patching plugs were ever actually spent, and on whom,
  remains untested.
- No change order history is present. The claim that the sequencing and access qualifications convert
  into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than observed in the field.
- No inspection or testing report is present, so nothing shows how often applied thickness fails and
  what remediation costs.
- The firestopping picture is one-sided. Single-source bids were collected and the work was
  distributed to the trades instead, so nothing shows what the distributed firestopping actually cost
  once carried inside each trade's number, or whether the deviation from the insurer's single-source
  requirement caused any downstream problem.
- Intumescent appears on only one project, and there as a small lobby and stair scope. Nothing here
  is calibrated on a project where exposed architectural steel is a significant intumescent package,
  and the topcoat and appearance gap identified in section 5 is therefore untested at scale.
- No project in this set required a high-insulating-value fireproofing that any bidder agreed was
  achievable, so the three-way disagreement about that requirement remains unresolved rather than
  resolved.
- Nothing shows how this package is bought when the structure is entirely concrete or entirely wood,
  where the trade may not exist at all. One of the four projects came close to that case and drew no
  package.
