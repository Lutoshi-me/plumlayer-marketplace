# Countertops

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the countertop
package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets.
This file speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real fabricators can bid it and a real estimator can
level it.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 123600 (Countertops) is the anchor, and it is the section the estimator's leveling tab
carries. Where a spec breaks it further, 123640 for stone countertops shows up as a sub-reference on
the scope sheet rather than as a separate package. Proposals themselves rarely cite a section number
at all, so the trade token and the division are the strong identifiers and the section number is
weak.

Countertops are bid as their own package. That is the single most important packaging fact and it is
worth stating plainly, because the drawings show countertops sitting on cabinets and invite the
assumption that one contractor buys both. The market does not work that way. The estimator opens a
countertop tab, invites a countertop bid list, and ranks countertop numbers on a cost per unit. Most
of the bid list is stone fabricators who do not sell cabinets at all.

But a substantial minority of every bid list quotes cabinets and countertops together in one
document, and some of those also quote doors, flooring, tile, shower surrounds, glass enclosures and
appliances in the same file. When that happens the general contractor cannot level the number until
the bidder splits it, and the estimator has to ask. On one project the scope sheet's second row is
the estimator writing that a bidder's number covers casework and countertops together, that a
breakout was requested, and that the reply is still awaited. A bundled bidder that never splits its
number will either be dropped or ranked against a different scope than everyone else, which is how a
bundled bid ends up eight times the cost per unit of the low bidder on the same list.

The bidder pool comes in four commercial shapes, and which one a bidder belongs to changes almost
every other answer in this file:

- The domestic stone fabricator. Templates the installed cabinets in the field, fabricates locally,
  installs. The most common shape and the one the drawings implicitly assume. Lead times measured in
  weeks.
- The offshore supplier or importer. Fabricates overseas from approved shop drawings, does no field
  measurement at all, and oversizes tops at wall to wall conditions to absorb field variation.
  Freight, tariff and duty are separate priced lines and can be a large fraction of the delivered
  cost. Lead times measured in months from shop drawing approval, and an overseas factory holiday can
  extend them. Often the low bidder.
- The material manufacturer running a managed program. The company that makes the specified quartz
  bids the installed package directly, managing a fabricator behind it, with the material warranty
  and the installation warranty stated separately and back to back.
- The cabinet or whole-interiors distributor. Countertops are one section of a package. Usually
  supply only with installation as a separately priced add-on, which is the trap described above.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits in
another division or another spec section:

- Sink cutouts and faucet holes, universally. Cutting the opening is the fabricator's work in every
  proposal seen, whoever supplies the sink.
- Sinks themselves, sometimes. See section five; this is the single most contested seam in the trade.
- Sink clips, mounting hardware and dishwasher brackets or metal strips. Carried by some bidders as
  named lines, excluded by others, and a recurring estimator scope row.
- Countertop protection film. A named priced line on some proposals and an estimator scope row on
  most projects.
- Shop and coordination drawings. Priced as their own lump by some bidders, folded into the rate by
  others, and named as a scope row on half the estimator sheets.
- Stone panels applied to the face or the side of a cabinet, and stone cladding on a floating shelf.
  When the design wraps a millwork piece in the countertop material, the fabricator prices the stone
  and someone else builds what it is stuck to.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like countertops:

- Tile backsplashes at kitchens. Nearly always tile by others, and the countertop bidders exclude it
  in the same breath as they include the vanity splash. See section five.
- Thresholds and saddles at unit baths and unit entries, even where they carry stone marks off the
  same finish schedule. An estimator keeps them on the countertop scope sheet as a row and routes
  them to the tile contractor.
- Cast and cultured marble bath products, shower bases, tub and shower surrounds and glass
  enclosures. These are a different trade, but the vendor class straddles because the same
  manufacturers make vanity tops. One such quote sat in a countertop folder, carried a countertop
  trade token, and was ranked on the countertop tab at a cost per unit while containing no
  countertops at all. Check what is actually in the document before you trust the label.
- Structural support, brackets and corbels at overhangs. Excluded by nearly every bidder and
  routinely unassigned.
- Plywood subtops, blocking and substrate. Excluded by nearly every bidder and routinely unassigned.

## 2. How the market bids

Countertops are not a degenerate proposal trade. This is the opposite of painting: a countertop
proposal usually carries measured quantities, and often carries them at a finer grain than the
general contractor asked for. Expect numbers.

Two proposal structures dominate.

- One priced block per unit plan type, each block carrying material by area, template, fabrication,
  edge by length, sink cutout by count, and installation by area. Some bidders draw a dimensioned
  sketch of every counter run above the block, with each edge tagged as unfinished, appliance or
  finished. This is the most common shape among the domestic fabricators and it is the finest grain
  in the trade.
- Two to six numbers split by scope family, typically kitchens, vanities, amenity and sometimes unit
  laundry, each with a total square footage behind it. Common among the suppliers and the manufacturer
  programs.

Single lump sum with no internal breakout is rare, and even the terse one-page quotes usually state
the kitchen and vanity square footages, the material, the edge, the splash and the cutout scope in
prose. The genuinely degenerate countertop proposal is more specific than the typical painting
proposal.

Segmentation by area is nearly universal, and segmentation by element is common: the trade thinks in
tops, edges, cutouts and splashes as separately measurable things. Unit pricing is present in most
proposals in some form, even if only as a square foot rate.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The material by finish schedule mark, with manufacturer, color name, thickness and finish. Nearly
  always. Marks are the trade's vocabulary and bidders quote them back.
- Thickness, in centimetres or millimetres, per mark. Almost always, because thickness drives both
  price and whether a substrate is needed.
- The edge profile, and where it changes. Almost always, and often as its own measured length.
- The splash convention, stated as an inclusion and an exclusion in the same sentence: no splash at
  kitchens, a four inch back and side splash at vanities. Nearly always.
- Sink cutout scope and faucet hole drilling, and whether the openings are undermount. Nearly always.
- The template, fabricate and install triple, named as three things. Almost always, because each one
  can be bought separately.
- The basis document, a named drawing set with a date and often the architect's name. Nearly always.
- Unit counts and vanity counts, and the square footage behind each. Commonly. Some bidders name the
  drawing sheet and the table the counts came from.
- Labor type, union or open shop, stated by about half, and recorded by the estimator for every
  bidder regardless.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Seam locations. The fabricator reserves discretion over layout and seam placement in almost every
  set of terms, and no proposal shows where seams land.
- Slab counts or yield. Never seen. Pricing is stated as based on best material yield, and vein or
  pattern matching across a seam is explicitly not included and costs more.
- The hoisting and access assumption, unless it is being excluded.
- What the substrate under the top is, or who supplies it.
- Whether the price survives a change in cabinet shop drawings, unless the bidder says so, and about
  half of them do.
- Bond, insurance basis and payment portal fees, unless asked. These come back by email.

Two behaviors are distinctive to this trade and worth expecting.

First, options culture. Bidders routinely quote the same scope two, three or four times over with
different materials, as parallel priced columns or as separately totalled options. A proposal with
three totals is not three scopes, it is one scope at three material grades, and the estimator may
select an option rather than a base bid. Value-engineering material substitutions are offered
unprompted and are large.

Second, the bidder as technical reviewer. Countertop bidders read the finish schedule critically and
push back on it in writing. Specified colors turn out to be discontinued. A specified manufacturer
turns out to have no domestic presence. A specified material turns out not to exist in the specified
thickness. A specified stone turns out to be special order only and unavailable in small quantities.
A bidder may write that it does not recommend the specified thin porcelain for countertops at all and
that special sub tops would be needed. Every one of these produces a substitution, the bidder says so
plainly, and the bids stop being comparable on material. Treat a substitution note as a document
finding, not as bidder noise.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs:

- The most common revision changes no price and no scope at all. It re-presents the same numbers
  split into a cabinet column and a countertop column, because the general contractor asked. That is
  a leveling artifact, and it is the clearest evidence that the bundling problem is real.
- The next most common is a value-engineering round: same geometry, same quantities, same edge
  treatments, different material. The move is usually downward by roughly a tenth, and material
  substitutions offered as deducts can run a quarter to a third of the whole number.
- A revision occasionally moves because a measured quantity moved, usually after the bidder answers a
  question about whether islands were included. Then the price tracks the quantity, a few percent.
- A revision may add a second manufacturer's material as an extra option and change nothing else, in
  which case the new information is one price at one alternative brand.
- Revisions arrive inside forwarded email threads about subcontractor interviews as often as they
  arrive as reissued documents. A general contractor's own questions about warranty term, insurance
  program and bond are answered in email and those answers are part of the proposal.

Two warnings about revisions here. A file named as a later revision may quote an entirely different
job: one such document sat in a project folder, labelled as the third round, addressed to a different
jobsite with a different unit count. Treating it as a revision would corrupt the pair. And a bidder's
supply-side document and its installer's document may be two files from two companies, dated weeks
apart, neither complete alone.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Countertop scope sheets have run 42, 46, 49 and 69 rows. Target roughly forty-five to seventy scope
lines for a full multifamily countertop package. The thin end works only when the amenity content is
small; every row past about forty on the larger sheets is an amenity room or a commercial term, and
those are exactly the rows the plugs come from.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Unit plan type, or better, the group of plan types that share identical countertop geometry.
  Bidders price by plan type in every quantified proposal, and the sharper ones group several
  differently-named plans into one block because the counters are the same. Grouping by geometry is
  the correct grain; grouping by plan name is what the estimator's sheet does and it produces more
  rows than the work has.
- Vanity by width. Vanity tops are enumerated in inches of width, not by plan type, because the top
  is a function of the vanity and the same vanity appears in many plans. Every quantified proposal
  does this.
- Kitchen, bath, laundry, bedroom and desk as separate room families on the unit side. Do not assume
  the unit side is only kitchens and baths. One set carried unit laundry tops as a third priced
  category and unit bedroom and desk tops that the bidder named and excluded.
- Every amenity room, individually, keyed to its finish mark. Amenity tops are per room, never per
  type, because each room carries its own material, thickness, edge geometry and splash. Every
  estimator sheet enumerates them by room name and every quantified proposal answers room by room.
  The named rooms recur: lobby or leasing desk, mail room, work room, club lounge, fitness, dog or
  pet spa, conference, private dining, gaming and social lounges, and the common restrooms.
- Material by mark, with thickness. Both matter and both change the rate. Thin porcelain and
  twelve-millimetre material need a substrate that two-centimetre and three-centimetre stone do not.
- Edge profile, wherever it changes. Eased and straight polished is the unit default. Mitered aprons
  at one and a half inches, two inches, three inches, six inches and seven inches all appear, and a
  mitered apron is priced by length as its own line by several bidders. Appliance edges are counted
  separately from finished edges by the finer bidders. A single edge line will not survive.
- Waterfall legs and double miter conditions with face and return dimensions, at amenity. These are
  named and priced individually and they are expensive relative to their area.
- Backsplash, separately for kitchen and for vanity, and separately again by height where the design
  calls for a full-height or a tall splash at an amenity counter. On a three-centimetre vanity top the
  four inch splash may be a separate special-order two-centimetre slab, so it is a distinct material
  purchase and not a trimming.
- Sink cutout, by type and by opening size. Undermount kitchen and undermount vanity are different
  lines, a drop-in is a different line again, and the finer bidders qualify the cutout by opening
  size. A vanity with two sinks is a different line from one with one.
- Cooktop cutout, separately. It appears only on the barrier-free plan types on most projects,
  because that is where a drop-in cooktop replaces a freestanding range. If the range is freestanding
  there is also no splash behind it, which is a related assumption bidders state.
- Sink supply and sink installation as two separate rows. The estimator sheets do this and the
  bidders split exactly along that seam. See section five.
- Substrate: plywood subtops, blocking under counters and at miters, and honeycomb backing under thin
  porcelain. Named as three different things and excluded by most bidders.
- Countertop support brackets and corbels at overhangs, keyed to the detail sheet that shows them.
- Template and field measure, as its own line, because a whole class of bidder does not do it.
- Hoisting, lifts and jobsite distribution, and offload.
- Delivery terms: mobilization minimums, units required ready per trip, full truck minimums, and
  on-site storage requirements measured in containers.
- Countertop protection film.
- Shop and coordination drawings.
- Tariff, duty and freight exposure, as its own row. On this trade it is not a footnote.
- The commercial block: tax, payment portal fee, owner-controlled insurance program participation,
  general liability if no program applies, performance and payment bond, warranty term.
- Cleanup to dumpster.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual counter runs within one plan type. A bidder describes a plan type as one sink run
  counter at a stated size plus one stove side counter at a stated size, and prices the pair as one
  block. Do not create a row per run.
- Faucet holes. They are part of the sink cutout in every proposal seen.
- Seam count and slab count. These are the fabricator's yield problem, explicitly reserved to its
  discretion, and no document prices them.
- Coat of polish, finish name and color, within one mark. These are attributes of a mark, and the
  mark is already an enumeration axis.
- Room-by-room enumeration on the unit side. Unit tops collapse to plan type or geometry group, never
  to individual apartments.
- Levels. Countertop scope does not vary by floor. No document seen segments by level.
- Individual items within one amenity room, unless the mark changes. A mail room with an island and a
  sort counter in the same material is two lines only because the geometry differs; two counters in
  one room in one material with one edge treatment are one line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied to
countertops, the test fires hardest on the sink question, on the substrate and support question, on
installation itself, and on every amenity room, which is why those get lines even where the drawings
show them as one mark on a schedule.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. If a proposal does not say whether it includes
installation, that silence is a scope gap of roughly forty percent of the supply price, not an
inclusion to assume. If it does not say who supplies the sinks, that is a gap with two different
right answers depending on the bidder.

### The structural gap list

These are the recurring plugs: scope no bidder carries that the general contractor must add to every
number before comparing. A scope line set that names all of them is doing the estimator's plugging in
advance.

1. Plywood, blocking and substrate under counters and at miters. Excluded by nearly everyone and
   plugged at a per-unit rate on at least one project.
2. Support brackets and corbels at overhangs. Excluded by nearly everyone.
3. Hoisting, lifts and vertical distribution. Excluded or made conditional on the general contractor
   providing a hoist by nearly everyone. One standing set of terms excludes lifting stone above the
   second floor outright.
4. Sinks, as supply and as installation, held as two separate plugs.
5. Sink clips, mounting hardware and dishwasher brackets or strips.
6. Countertop protection film.
7. Kitchen backsplash, in whatever material actually gets built, if the tile package does not carry
   it.
8. Thresholds and saddles carrying stone marks.
9. Caulking beyond the silicone joint at the counter to splash and at the sink.
10. Scribing to walls, which one standing set of terms excludes unless stated.
11. Tariff, duty and freight escalation beyond the rates quoted.
12. Mobilization and remobilization beyond the bidder's stated batch minimum, and on-site storage
    space.
13. Shop and coordination drawings, where a bidder prices them separately.
14. The commercial adders: bond, general liability if no owner-controlled program applies, payment
    portal fee, and the delta from a one-year to a two-year warranty.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the trade that must pick the work up, and
if no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| Excluded by the countertop bidder | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Sinks and faucets, supply (most bidders) | Plumbing package, or the owner, or bought back from the fabricator as an add |
| Plumbing and electrical connections, drains, disposals (nearly all) | Plumbing and electrical |
| Structural support, brackets and corbels at overhangs (nearly all) | Frequently unassigned, and a recurring real gap |
| Sub tops, substrates, plywood, adhesives (nearly all) | Frequently unassigned; carpentry where anyone carries it |
| Hoisting, cranes, lifts, lift gate (nearly all) | General contractor, usually as free use of the building hoist |
| Kitchen backsplash, stated as tile by others (nearly all) | Tile package |
| Pattern, vein and bookmatch matching across seams (about half) | Nobody; it is an added cost if wanted |
| Caulking other than silicone at counter to splash and at the sink (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned |
| Scribing to walls (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned |
| Removal and haul-off of existing tops (some bidders, on renovation) | Demolition |
| Off hours, overtime, out of sequence work (about half) | General contractor, or time and material |
| Receiving, signing off and distributing product at site (the supply-only bidders) | General contractor |
| Field measurement and template (the offshore bidders, and one distributor) | Nobody; the shop drawings replace it |
| Installation itself (the supply-only bidders) | A separate installer, priced separately |
| Moving or securing appliances, damage to unprotected surfaces (some bidders) | General contractor |
| Support elements for cast iron or farmhouse sinks (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned |

| Floating shelf structure under stone cladding (occasionally) | Millwork |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

The standing terms and conditions block of a repeat fabricator is reproduced verbatim across every
project it bids, and repeat bidders make up a good share of any countertop bid list. Read boilerplate
for the trade map, not for the project.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else in that bidder's
other proposals:

- A named finish mark that is excluded or substituted. When a bidder writes out a specific mark and
  says the material is discontinued, unavailable in the specified thickness, special order only, or
  made by a manufacturer with no domestic presence, it read the finish schedule and found a real
  problem. Several bidders do this on every project, and they rarely agree on which marks are the
  problem, which is itself informative.
- A technical objection to a specified material. A bidder writing that it does not recommend
  twelve-millimetre material for countertops without special sub tops is reviewing the design.
- An exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a plumbing shroud panel, a concrete panel at
  a lobby desk, a floating shelf, a grill station, an exterior counter.
- A count taken from a named table on a named sheet. When a bidder cites the drawing sheet its unit
  counts came from, it is telling you which document the set makes authoritative, and it is telling
  you that if that table is wrong every bid is wrong.
- A statement that the price is subject to change based on cabinet shop drawings. This is common
  enough to be near-boilerplate, but it is worth reading as project signal whenever the casework
  package is not yet bought.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number, and violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order. Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- The template happens after the cabinets are installed. This is the trade's defining schedule
  dependency and it is stated explicitly by the bidders who template: templating of completed
  cabinetry and substrate, with installation following the template by roughly seven to ten days.
  Add the material lead time in front of that and the countertop is gated by the cabinet set date.
- Final price is based on actual square footage measured at template, and the fabricator reserves the
  right to adjust up or down against the customer-provided quantities. Every domestic quote says
  this. A countertop number is provisional in a way a lump sum is not.
- Where there is no template, dimensions come from approved shop drawings and nothing else. The
  offshore bidders oversize tops by two to three inches at wall to wall conditions to absorb field
  variation, and items not on the approved shop drawings are simply not in the contract.
- Lead time is stated and it is long. Roughly eight weeks after shop drawing approval domestically,
  and ninety to one hundred twenty days for slab release or for overseas fabrication. Lead time
  starts at deposit and approved shop drawings, not at award.
- A material deposit is required before fabrication, commonly half the material value, more for
  non-stock selections, and one importer's balance falls due within days of arrival at port.
- Delivery is batched. Bidders state a minimum number of units per mobilization, a full truck minimum,
  or a requirement that multiple units per floor be ready per trip, and charge per extra trip
  otherwise. One base bid is conditioned on the site providing storage for a stated range of units at
  a time, roughly two containers.
- Tariffs and duties are quoted at rates in effect on a stated date and any increase passes through.
  Several bidders itemize by tariff program with effective dates. One advances domestic manufacture
  as a competitive claim precisely because it removes the exposure.
- Union or open shop, and prevailing wage. Open shop non-prevailing-wage is the stated default in
  most proposals and union pricing appears as its own column where a bidder offers both.
- All work during regular business hours; premium and overtime excluded.
- Warranty is commonly one year on installation, with the material warranty running from the
  manufacturer and sometimes stated as lifetime on natural stone or ten years on a builder line.
  Honed materials, exterior seams and seams at sinks are commonly carved out of the warranty, and
  installing a garbage disposal can void the sink attachment warranty.
- Tolerance is stated. One standing set of terms allows a quarter inch plus or minus between the wall
  or finished panel and the edge of the stone.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

### Casework: the seam that defines the trade

Countertops and casework are separate packages that cannot be separately scheduled. Everything below
follows from that.

- The template waits for the cabinets. A domestic fabricator cannot measure until the cabinets are
  set and level, and it says so. Any schedule that treats the countertop as an independent lead item
  is wrong, and any countertop delay is usually a cabinet delay.
- Where there is no template, the countertop is fabricated from the approved cabinet shop drawings,
  and several bidders write that their price is subject to change based on those shop drawings. So
  the countertop price is a derivative of the casework submittal either way.
- Countertop support must be installed by others before the stone arrives, and fabricators state
  flatly that they will not install supports. This is a real sequencing gap: the item is excluded by
  the countertop bidder, it is usually not in the casework bid either, and it has to be on site before
  the stone.
- Finished end panels behind peninsulas and islands are the cabinet supplier's, and at least one
  excludes them on the assumption of a finished wall. If the countertop is a waterfall, the stone
  wraps and the panel question changes.
- At amenity, the seam runs the other way. Stone is applied to cabinet faces and cabinet sides, and
  stone clads floating shelves whose structure is by others. The fabricator prices the stone panel
  and someone else has to build and hang what it attaches to. Beware of the same assembly being
  half-claimed by two packages.
- The bundled bid. A cabinet distributor bidding both makes the countertop number invisible until it
  is split, and its cabinet half and countertop half often carry opposite labor terms, with cabinets
  supply-only and counters furnish-and-install in the same document.

### Plumbing: the sink question

The sink is where this trade's furnish-and-install seam actually lives, and the drawings will not
resolve it. Four configurations all appear:

1. The fabricator cuts the opening and mounts the sink, and someone else supplies it. The most common
   shape.
2. The fabricator supplies and installs the sinks, priced per sink model as its own line. Common
   enough to be a real option, and several bidders would clearly rather sell you their sink.
3. The fabricator supplies nothing and installs nothing, and the sink is provided and installed by
   others while the fabricator only cuts and polishes the opening.
4. The supplier ships the tops uncut and the sink cutout is done by the installer on site. Unusual,
   and it moves a shop operation into the field.

The estimator sheets carry furnish sinks and install sinks as two separate rows, which is exactly
right, because bidders split along that seam in both directions.

Two consequences worth carrying onto the scope line. First, using a third-party sink is not free even
where the fabricator agrees: one bidder charges a stated coordination fee per sink type, and requires
either an electronic cutout template or a physical sample sink shipped to the overseas factory.
Second, the sink and the cabinet have to fit each other. A thirty-three inch sink base takes a thirty
to thirty-one inch kitchen sink, and at that clearance the mounting method may have to change from
metal clips to wood blocking. That is a three-way constraint across plumbing, casework and stone that
no single drawing shows.

Faucets, drains, disposals and all plumbing connections are excluded by essentially everyone, and a
disposal installed later can void the fabricator's sink attachment warranty.

### Tile

The kitchen backsplash is tile by others on every project seen, stated as an inclusion boundary by
the countertop bidders and settled on the estimator's own sheet. The vanity gets a four inch stone
back and side splash and the kitchen gets none. Where a general contractor wants stone at the kitchen
instead, it appears as a priced add alternate, separately for each material family, which tells you
the swap is real and priced.

Thresholds and saddles are the other tile seam. A finish schedule can carry stone threshold marks for
unit baths, unit entries and amenity, and an estimator will keep them on the countertop scope sheet as
rows while routing them to the tile contractor. Check that the tile package actually took them.

### Appliances and electrical

Cooktop cutouts appear only where a drop-in cooktop replaces a freestanding range, which in this
corpus means the barrier-free units, so the cooktop cutout count is a proxy for the accessible unit
count. Freestanding ranges also mean no splash behind the range, which bidders state as an
assumption. Outlet, disposal button, soap dispenser and sprayer cutouts are extra and priced
individually where a bidder offers them at all, and one standing set of terms disclaims any
electrical work needed for powered countertop equipment.

### The general contractor

Hoisting, lifts, offload, floor readiness, protection of the finished top, and space to stage two
containers of stone all land back on the general contractor. Most of these are excluded rather than
priced, and free use of the building hoist is assumed rather than requested. Damage to unprotected
walls, floors and fixtures during installation is disclaimed.

### Millwork

Amenity countertops sit on millwork, and an estimator's scope sheet notes the amenity rows as
requiring coordination with the millwork package. Outdoor amenity counters get a note routing them to
the sitework division. Neither is a countertop bidder's problem and both are on the countertop scope
sheet, which is the right place for them.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What countertop bidders actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The finish schedule, or the interior finish description sheet. This is the primary document. Marks
   come from here with manufacturer, color, thickness and finish, and bidders quote the marks back.
2. The unit plan types and their counts. Every quantified proposal is organized by plan type, and
   every estimator sheet normalizes every bid to a cost per unit and ranks on it. Unit count is the
   leveling currency of this trade. One bidder names the unit makeup table on the cover sheet as the
   source of its counts, which is exactly the dependency to worry about: if that table is ambiguous,
   every bid is incomparable.
3. The casework details and unit details. Edge profiles, apron dimensions, miter faces and returns,
   waterfall conditions and splash heights are read off details, not off plans.
4. The interior elevations, to settle whether a backsplash exists and in what material. One estimator
   justified a not-in-contract kitchen splash row by citing the elevations and the tile mark scheduled
   there.
5. The amenity or room finish schedule, for the room-by-room amenity marks.
6. The specification, cited rarely and quoted almost never.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff of the whole building. The normal method is plan type
times count, with each plan type's counters dimensioned once. Where a bidder does not model each run,
it builds square feet per unit type times unit count in a separate attached table.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Whether the bidder fabricates domestically and templates in the field, or fabricates overseas from
  shop drawings. This single fact changes lead time by months, changes whether a template exists,
  changes freight and tariff exposure, and correlates with being the low bidder.
- The tariff and duty regime in force at order date, and whether the material is domestic. Tariff
  can run a tenth or more of the pre-tariff price on imported goods, and it is quoted as of a stated
  date with escalation passed through.
- Whether installation is in the number at all. A supply-only number and an installed number for the
  same scope differ by something like forty percent of the supply price, and if both sit in one
  ranking the ranking is meaningless.
- Union versus open shop, and prevailing wage applicability. Union installation runs materially above
  open shop where a bidder prices both, and the union bidders sit at the top of the range.
- Whether the specified materials are actually available, in the specified thickness, in the required
  quantity, from a manufacturer with domestic distribution. Assume some are not.
- The cabinet set date and the cabinet submittal approval date, which together gate the template, the
  fabrication release and the installation.
- Site storage. Whether the site can hold two containers of stone at a time is a base-bid condition
  for at least one bidder and an extra monthly cost otherwise.
- Hoist availability and the floor release sequence, which drive mobilization count against the
  bidder's stated minimum units per trip.
- Who supplies the sinks, and who sets them, and whether the sink fits the sink base.
- Whether a substrate is required, which follows from the material thickness rather than from any
  note on a plan.
- Deposit and payment terms, which on the import side tie cash to arrival at port rather than to
  installation.
- Warranty term required. One year is the market default; a specification demanding two years is
  invisible on the drawings and the delta is priced back as an alternate when asked.
- Insurance basis and bond. Both are quoted as a percentage of contract value or a rate per thousand,
  rarely, and usually only after an estimator asks by email.
- Payment portal fees.
- Whether a mockup unit is expected.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders price budgetary ranges per square foot, carry
colors as to be determined, and price against a material group rather than a named product. By permit
and construction documents the same bidders switch to plan-type takeoffs with measured square feet,
counted cutouts and named marks. A proposal's stated basis and its folder's set stage occasionally
disagree: a bid may name a design development basis and then note that it was revised to the permit
submission, or carry two different set dates in one document.

The material substitution problem is worth stating on its own because it is the single most
consistent source of non-comparability in this trade. On every project, several bidders substituted
at least one specified material and said so, and they did not substitute the same ones. A leveling
list where three bidders priced three different materials is not a price comparison. Any countertop
scope line set should therefore carry the material mark explicitly on every row and should carry a
value-engineering material row as a first-class line, because the market will supply one whether or
not it is asked for.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet, by material and by thickness, for tops. The primary priced unit in nearly every
  quantified proposal, and the basis for the general contractor's plugs.
- Square feet, separately, for backsplash material where the splash is a different thickness from the
  top.
- Linear feet for edge, banded by profile. Eased and polished, pencil, appliance edge, and mitered
  aprons at their stated face and return dimensions are all measured separately by the finer bidders.
  Some bidders enumerate edge length and then price it at zero, which means the edge is inside the
  material rate while still being carried as a measured quantity; do not read a zero as no scope.
- Each for sink cutouts, by type and opening size, and for cooktop cutouts.
- Each for miscellaneous cutouts, which are priced individually and cheaply: disposal button, soap
  dispenser, electrical, sprayer, additional vanity faucet holes.
- Each, or per unit, for whole tops. The supply-side importers price a fabricated top per plan type
  as a single each.
- Units, as the normalizing denominator for the whole package. Every estimator sheet, without
  exception.
- Containers, for storage and freight logistics.
- Working days, for the installation duration, and weeks or days for lead time.
- Percent of contract value for bond, and a rate per thousand of contract for owner-controlled
  insurance enrollment credit.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Material family and grade. The largest single driver by a wide margin. A value-engineering
   substitution of a builder-line white for a specified premium white can be worth a third of the
   whole number, and a same-scope substitution of a similar color runs about a quarter of the unit
   side. Amenity porcelain and quartzite run three to four times the unit quartz rate per square foot.
2. Whether installation is in the number, and whether the bidder templates. These are commercial
   shape questions before they are price questions, and mixing shapes in one ranking is the most
   common way a countertop leveling goes wrong.
3. Fabrication origin, with the tariff, duty and freight that follow. Freight and tariff together can
   approach a quarter of a delivered import number.
4. Union versus open shop.
5. Total square footage, driven by unit count and by how much island and peninsula the plans carry.
   Note that vanity count exceeds unit count on every project seen, because two-bedroom plans carry
   two baths; a scope line assuming one vanity per unit will be wrong.
6. Thickness and edge buildup. Three-centimetre against two-centimetre, and mitered aprons against
   eased edges, both move the material and the fabrication.
7. Amenity content. Amenity is a small fraction of the area and a large fraction of the complexity,
   with waterfall legs, double miters, thin porcelain needing honeycomb backing, and one-off
   materials in slab quantities too small to buy efficiently.
8. The sink decision, in all its forms.
9. Substrate, support brackets and protection, all of which are excluded by the market and therefore
   land as plugs rather than as price variation.
10. Access, hoisting, mobilization count and on-site storage.
11. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here: within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest cost per unit runs about 2.4 times, 2.9 times, 3.6 times and 4.6 times. Where a
bundled cabinet-and-countertop bid sits unsplit in the list the spread reads as about eight times,
which is a measurement artifact and not a market fact. Bidder counts per project run from nine to
eighteen. The top of the range is usually a bidder who priced a different scope, most often one that
includes cabinets or excludes nothing.

One more pricing convention worth knowing. An estimator does not compare base bids, it compares base
bid plus adjustments, and on this trade the adjustments split into two kinds. There are the plugs
listed in section three, which cover what the market systematically will not carry. And there is the
material normalization, which has no equivalent in most trades: before the numbers mean anything, the
estimator has to know which bidders priced the specified material, which priced a substitution, and
what each substitution is worth. A scope line set that names the mark on every row and carries an
explicit material-substitution row is doing both halves of that work in advance.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on about three dozen countertop proposals covering roughly forty documents,
plus four estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional
market, all bid by one general contractor. The proposals span design development, permit,
construction documents and conformed sets, and the bidder pool includes many companies that appear on
three or four of the projects, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction
in section four testable. All four commercial shapes described in section one are represented, and
several bidders appear in more than one shape across projects.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back of house space, in
  one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors,
  institutional, healthcare, laboratory or food service work, where the countertop package looks
  quite different.
- Every project here is quartz-dominant on the unit side, with porcelain slab, quartzite, terrazzo
  and natural marble appearing only at amenity. Granite appears as a base material on one project
  only. Solid surface appears once, and laminate not at all, so the file cannot yet say how the
  package behaves when the specified material family is laminate or solid surface throughout.
- No renovation project in the corpus carried countertops, so removal and haul-off of existing tops
  appears only as a standing exclusion and never as priced scope.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, or whether the plugs the estimator added were ever actually bought.
- No change order history and no template-stage requantification is in the corpus. The claim that
  final quantities move at template is read out of the bidders' own terms rather than observed, so the
  size of that movement is unknown.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the general contractor issued a formal enumerated
  countertop scope sheet to bidders and got it back answered row by row. The evidence for what a
  scope sheet should contain is the estimator's internal grid plus the emailed question-and-answer
  threads, not a returned checklist.
- The corpus has not shown seam layout drawings, shop drawing submittals, or any document where the
  fabricator's own quantities were reconciled against the general contractor's takeoff, so section
  three's grain recommendations rest on bid-stage documents only.
- Two recurring bidders submit only scanned image proposals with no extractable text, so any
  automated read of this trade must handle scans or it will silently drop part of the bid list.
