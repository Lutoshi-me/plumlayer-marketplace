# Demolition

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the demolition package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. Demolition is the first package bought and the one that pays
for every unasked question later, because almost everything it touches belongs to somebody else at
some point in the operation, and the drawings do not say when.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 024100 is the anchor for demolition, and 028000 for abatement. Both live in Division 02
and are commonly bid by the same company, which is the single most misleading fact about this trade.

The first thing to establish on any project is which of two different trades you are scoping. They
share a division, a bidder pool and a vocabulary, and they have almost nothing else in common.

Structural demolition means taking a building down. The whole scope collapses to one number, the
proposal runs one to two pages, the enumeration lives entirely in the exclusion list, and the
discriminating questions are about foundations, party walls, salvage and the street. A scope sheet
for it runs sixty to seventy rows and most of those rows are general conditions and monitoring.

Selective or interior demolition means gutting a building that stays standing. The scope decomposes
into more than a hundred rows, the bids answer a general contractor's numbered scope sheet row by
row, and the discriminating questions are about access, sequence, layers, and which of five trades
touches an item before it reaches the dumpster. Bidders on this work price by vertical zone, by
operation, or by scope sheet row number, and their numbers spread by a factor of eight.

A bidder that does one does not necessarily do the other. A structural demolition proposal was seen
explicitly excluding selective demolition for bracing and utility relocations, on the same building it
was demolishing. Treat them as separate packages that happen to share a bidder list.

Abatement is the licensed sibling, not a subset. It is separately certificated work, priced from a
survey rather than from drawings, notified to a state agency on a statutory clock, and subject to a
third party clearance the abatement contractor is forbidden to perform on its own work. Section 5
covers the seam in full. The packaging rule is simple: most demolition contractors in this market
carry an abatement license and will price both, but they price them as two numbers on one document,
and the general contractor decides whether to buy them together or apart. A general contractor was
seen asking a bidder directly whether it wanted a combined number or a separate proposal, and
answering its own question with a request for both, stand-alone and combinable. That is the packaging
decision made explicitly, and it is a general contractor decision, not a market given.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope that the market pulls into this package even though it sits
elsewhere:

- Abatement, as above, by way of the same bidder rather than the same scope.
- Universal waste, meaning fluorescent bulbs, ballasts, thermostats, aerosols, paint and chemicals.
  Some bidders name it as an inclusion and take it, a similar number exclude it by name, and where it
  is excluded it is frequently unassigned.
- Refrigerant recovery and rooftop unit recovery. Genuinely contested, and the split does not track
  anything visible: some demolition bidders carry rooftop unit recovery, others exclude reclaiming of
  gas or liquid refrigerants in the same sentence they exclude asbestos. The mechanical trade prices
  it too, in hours.
- Removal of loose contents, furniture and equipment. Commonly excluded, sometimes carried, and where
  the building is being emptied by an owner it belongs to nobody on the bid list.
- Site demolition, meaning pavement, curb, walks, site furnishings, light poles and fencing. Almost
  every bidder draws its line at the building footprint. One bidder priced pavement removal as an add
  alternate, another enumerated the abutting sidewalk, sign base and bollards as inclusions, and most
  simply exclude anything outside the footprint.
- Dumpsters and containers. Usually the demolition contractor's own containers for its own debris,
  sometimes carried outright, sometimes excluded, and dumpster permits are excluded by nearly
  everyone.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like demolition:

- Utility cut, cap and make safe at the street. Excluded by nearly every demolition bidder, and it is
  a separate trade: it comes back priced by an excavation and utility contractor, enumerated by
  service, with police detail days and municipal permit allowances. Section 5 covers it.
- Make safe of mechanical, electrical, plumbing and fire protection inside the building. A different
  exclusion from the one above, and the two need separate lines. The mechanical trade sells this as
  its own small package.
- Shoring, bracing, underpinning and support of excavation. Excluded by nearly every bidder. It comes
  back either from a specialist shoring contractor or, occasionally, as a priced line from a
  demolition bidder that has retained an engineer.
- Third party air monitoring, dust and vibration monitoring, and the final visual inspection and air
  clearance after abatement. Excluded by every bidder seen, including every abatement bidder, because
  regulation requires the owner to hire an independent monitor. It arrives as its own bought package,
  often from the same firm that wrote the survey.
- Hazardous materials survey. Excluded by every bidder seen, and several state on the face of the
  proposal that a survey is required for all demolitions. The owner or general contractor buys it.
- Licensed site professional services, contaminated soil, storage tanks and soil export. Excluded by
  everyone.
- Elevator decommissioning. Excluded by most; removal of the decommissioned cab, cables, guides,
  motors and switch gear is then carried by demolition, sometimes as its own priced line.

## 2. How the market bids

Structural demolition is nearly a degenerate proposal trade. Interior demolition is not. Expect
completely different documents.

For structural demolition, the default output is one number, a short inclusion list, and a long
exclusion list. Proposals run one to two pages. Segmentation, when it happens, is by scope family:
demolition, then separately priced bracing, cut and cap, separation, underpinning or abatement.
Segmentation by building appears where there is more than one structure, and it is worth asking for,
because it is the only grain that survives a partial award or a phasing change. Nobody segments a
single structure by element.

For interior demolition, the proposal answers whatever scope sheet the general contractor issued.
Three shapes recur. A bidder may reproduce the issued scope rows verbatim by row number for the rows
it takes, which is the clearest form and also the most dangerous, because the row numbers it omits are
its exclusions and they are invisible unless the answer is diffed against the issued sheet. A bidder
may segment by vertical zone: basement and sub-basement, floors, roof, plus named operations. Or a
bidder may reproduce the operation list as flowing prose with no extent at all, which reads as
complete and prices as a fraction.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The labor basis, union or open shop, nearly always, because it is the largest single price
  variable in this trade. A third basis, prevailing wage, appears on some bids and the leveling sheet
  usually has no column for it.
- One mobilization, one phase, first shift, standard eight hour weekday shifts. Nearly universal, and
  it is a pricing basis rather than a courtesy.
- Who takes the salvage. Nearly always the bidder, stated as a right rather than a price.
- A stated basis for existing dimensions it cannot see: slab thickness, foundation depth, footing
  size, number of finish layers. Commonly present and load-bearing, see section 6.
- An assertion that debris is disposed or recycled at approved facilities, and sometimes that
  disposal documentation will be provided at completion.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Quantities of any kind. A stated building area appears sometimes, and where several bidders state
  one for the same building they routinely disagree. Four different areas were seen stated for one
  single-storey building, the largest being roughly forty percent above the smallest, and the
  leveling sheet had to pick one to normalize on.
- Any unit price. Rare, and where present it is almost always for an alternate rather than the base:
  a per window assembly rate, a per square foot floor grinding rate, a per month bracing rental.
- Duration or crew size, unless the scope sheet asks. Even then, most leave it blank.
- Off-hour rates, unless asked. One interior bidder published second and third shift hourly adders.
- Time and materials rates. Asked for on an interior scope sheet, answered by nobody.
- Waste diversion or recycling documentation. Named in exactly one proposal, to exclude it, so
  construction waste diversion reporting is effectively an unowned deliverable.

Exclusion behavior is where this trade differs sharply from the finish trades. Nearly every demolition
proposal carries a long exclusion list, twelve to twenty-two lines, and most of it is a standing
template. The same list appears verbatim from the same bidder on unrelated projects and building
types, and some of it travels between competitors: an identical qualification about standard slab,
foundation and footing dimensions was seen word for word in proposals from two unrelated companies on
two different projects. Read the boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

The priced breakout beats the printed exclusion list, and in this trade the two contradict each other
routinely. Proposals were seen pricing abatement on one page while the boilerplate on the next page
excluded asbestos testing and removal, the abatement work plan and air monitoring. Another included
removal of finish flooring clear of glue and mastics and excluded removal of mastic floor adhesives in
the same document. Another listed the demolition permit as included in the quote body and excluded in
the notes below it. Never level off an exclusion list alone.

Revision habits split by cause, and the causes are worth telling apart:

- Negotiation ladders are common and the movement is very large. On one interior package three
  bidders dropped between a fifth and two fifths of their number between rounds with no stated scope
  change. On structural work drops of about a fifth were seen with the scope text byte-identical.
- Exclusion to priced line migration is the most informative revision in this trade. A bidder issued a
  demolition-only number one day and, the next day, returned with the two seams it had excluded now
  named, referenced to a specific civil drawing and a utility authority sign-off list, and separately
  priced. The base demolition line also rose by more than forty percent in the same move, so there is
  no clean delta between the rounds: the earlier base was not the same scope.
- Survey-triggered abatement pricing is the defining revision of this trade. See section 5.
- Commercial basis restatement moves no physical scope. A bidder discovered an owner-controlled
  insurance program might apply, re-based its number on that assumption, and priced conventional
  coverage as a small add alternate worth low single-digit percent of the base.
- Genuine document-change revisions are rare but real, and this trade produces the cleanest examples.
  One interior bidder reviewed a conformed set against the set it had priced, found saw cutting and
  slab penetrations across every floor that the earlier set had not shown, and priced the increment at
  about five percent of its demolition line, itemised to specific scope sheet row numbers.
- Reconfirmation revisions are the trap. On that same conformed set, a competitor issued a revision
  that changed nothing but its own date, while still citing drawings three months older than the set.
  A re-dated bid is not evidence that anybody re-read the documents. Where one bidder finds real added
  scope in a new set and another finds none, assume the second one did not look.
- Access-duration negotiation appears where sequence is the constraint. A bidder offered a combined
  lump sum about eleven percent below its own priced total, conditioned entirely on being allowed to
  defer the roof demolition and abatement until the material hoist was operational. The concession
  bought was schedule, not scope.

Two document hazards worth naming. First, the printed total is frequently not the base bid. Bidders
roll add alternates and abatement into a single printed subtotal, and a leveling sheet was seen
carrying one such printed total as that bidder's base bid, ranking it last on the sheet when its
demolition line alone would have placed it near the front. Second, the revision may not be a document
at all: it may be an email sentence, a negotiated figure in a thread, or a proposal attached to a
message whose filename date is the general contractor's filing date rather than the document's. Files
labelled as an earlier round were seen carrying documents dated later than the round above them.
Order rounds by the basis document each one cites, never by the filename or the letterhead date.

## 3. Scope grain rules

The two demolition trades have different answers here, so decide which one you are scoping first.

Structural demolition scope sheets have run about sixty to seventy rows. Target roughly sixty rows for
a whole-building teardown, and expect most of them to be general conditions, monitoring and
commercial questions rather than physical demolition. The physical work genuinely is close to one
line. The value of the sheet is entirely in the seams and the plugs.

Interior demolition scope sheets have run about a hundred and forty rows on a mid-rise gut, and a
thinner sheet fails visibly. A nine-row sheet on a partial demolition left the foundations question
unasked, and the two bidders who answered disagreed fundamentally about them: one removed foundations
to a foot below proposed grade and the other left them entirely in place. Neither was wrong on the
drawings, and their numbers were never comparable.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

For any demolition:

- Each seam in section 5, without exception. Utility cut and cap at the street, make safe of building
  systems, refrigerant recovery, universal waste, elevator decommissioning versus removal, salvage,
  temporary protection, and the hazmat survey itself. These are where the money moves and where the
  gaps live.
- Foundations, split three ways: slab on grade, below grade foundation walls and footings away from
  property lines, and foundation walls and footings at sidewalks, roads or abutting structures. The
  third one is treated differently by every bidder. Most leave it in place, some remove it only if
  support of excavation is installed by others first, one removes to a stated depth below proposed
  grade. A single foundations line will always be ambiguous.
- Deep foundation elements, meaning piles and caissons, as their own line. Excluded by every bidder
  seen, always as a conditional.
- The disposition of the demolition debris. This is not a housekeeping detail. Bidders were seen
  planning to keep crushed masonry and concrete on site to form a rubble buttress or a sloped bank
  supporting the basement walls that remain. That decision changes the haul quantity, the export cost
  and the temporary earth retention question at once, and two bidders on the same building can differ
  on it.
- Separation from an attached neighbouring structure, as its own line, and the temporary support and
  neighbour protection that come with it as a second. Where a project turns on this, the bidders that
  price it separately can be compared and the bidder that is silent cannot.
- Salvage, split into two lines that are commonly confused. Taking title to scrap metal is a credit
  the bidder claims. Removing, stacking, packaging, storing, transporting and reinstalling material
  the owner wants back is a cost, and it is excluded by nearly everyone who claims the first.
- Permits, split by kind: demolition permit, the fees for it, hot work permits, street opening and
  sidewalk permits, dumpster permits. Bidders take these in different combinations, and several
  include the permit while excluding its fees.
- Regulatory notification and filings, separate from permits.
- Dust control, and separately, dust and noise barriers and temporary partitions. Dust control is
  commonly included and barriers are commonly excluded, and a single line covering both will be read
  by every bidder in its own favor.
- Water source, water meter and water permit, since dust control is usually priced assuming somebody
  else supplies water.
- Monitoring, split by kind: air, dust, vibration, noise, settlement. All commonly excluded.
- Pre-demolition condition survey of adjacent properties, photographic documentation, and
  post-demolition survey.
- Rough grading and site condition at handover, since bidders describe the finish state differently:
  a sloped void, a rough level graded site, holes sloped to meet surrounding grades.

For interior demolition, additionally:

- Each vertical zone: sub-basement, basement, each contiguous band of floors, mezzanine, penthouse,
  roof. This is the primary organizing axis for anyone who wants a comparable or divisible number.
- Each removal operation as its own row, in the way an issued scope sheet does it: partitions
  including sill plate, top plate, blocking and anchors; masonry wall assemblies; retrofit infill at
  former openings; concrete slab; areas of floor assembly; door systems; doors where the frame stays;
  stairs, ramps, landings and railings; casework and its blocking; finish flooring; dropped ceiling
  systems down to structure including fixtures; interior glazing and frames; plumbing fixtures, toilet
  accessories and mirrors; column wraps; furring walls; exterior cladding down to substrate; chain
  link, plywood, wall coverings and abandoned conduit. Bidders answer these row by row, so a row that
  does not exist has no answer.
- Layers, explicitly. One layer of finish flooring is not the same scope as all layers, and the same
  is true of walls and ceilings. Bidders price a single layer and exclude additional layers as an
  unforeseen condition, and a building old enough to need demolishing usually has more than one.
- Floor preparation, split into removal of the finish, removal of adhesive and mastic, removal of
  levelling compound, grinding, and shot blasting. Nearly every bidder stops at the finish, and each
  further step is excluded by name. This is a real gap between demolition and the flooring trade.
- Saw cutting and coring, split by purpose: new openings in masonry walls, slab openings for new
  building systems, new openings for stairs and pits, basement slab removal for new footings,
  sidewalk openings at area ways. Enumerate with counts where the drawings allow, and note that at
  least one bidder excludes coring for building systems outright while the scope sheet carries it as
  a demolition row.
- Slab scanning and x-ray, separately from the cutting. Bidders exclude it and expect the general
  contractor to do it.
- Salvage, protect and maintain, as its own block keyed to the demolition keynotes. On a building
  with any historic fabric this block alone ran sixteen rows: protect existing flooring and plaster
  to remain, salvage window trim, frame, sill and apron, salvage door trim and frames for reuse,
  maintain door frames where the door is removed, salvage doors for reuse elsewhere, protect railings,
  protect the sill where casework is removed, protect historic marble wainscot and base, protect
  ornamental elevator doors and frames, protect interior frames and transoms, protect a mail chute,
  salvage an ornamental directory frame. Every one of these is a different verb applied to a different
  object and each is answered differently. A bidder was seen making historic items and conservation
  the general contractor's responsibility in a single blanket line while these sixteen rows sat on the
  scope sheet unanswered.
- Chipping and exposing structure for the engineer, as its own line, with locations counted. Exposing
  columns, chipping plaster or concrete off beam flanges at connections, and whether it is at point
  connections or entire lengths. One sheet asked whether the exposed steel might carry lead paint,
  which is the right question and turns a demolition row into an abatement row.
- Elevators, split: decommissioning by the elevator trade, staging the shaft, removal of cab, cables,
  guides, motors and switch gear, and disposal. Count the cars and note which are full height.
- Roof scope, split: membrane down to deck, ballast, roof anchors, parapet details, skylights, and
  whether existing roofing is demolition's or the roofer's. This last question was written on a scope
  sheet with a question mark and never answered.
- Facade and exterior scope, split: cladding, masonry removals, flashing removal, and courses of
  brick removed for new flashing.
- Access and logistics as priced rows, not as assumptions. How debris leaves the building, the
  basement and the roof; hoisting and cranes; whether dumpsters may sit on the street or must be live
  loaded; how many containers may be on site at once; which elevators the contractor gets and for how
  long; whether the sidewalk lay-down is secured. Bidders state these as conditions of price, so they
  belong on the sheet as rows.
- Off-hour work and shift premiums, with a stated hourly basis.
- Time and materials rates, with the labor tiers named.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Room by room enumeration. No document seen prices a single room. Collapse to floor, zone or
  building.
- Individual partition, door, ceiling or fixture counts within one zone. The operation row is the
  unit. Even the bidders that count something count only what is unusual, such as beams to be chipped
  or columns to be exposed.
- Coat, layer or material detail below the operation. What the partition is made of matters only where
  it changes the operation, meaning masonry versus stud, or plaster adhered to brick versus plaster on
  lath.
- Means and methods. Whether the wall comes down by machine or by hand is the bidder's business, and
  the two proposals in the corpus that described their methods were describing temporary works, which
  is a different thing and does earn a line.
- The waste stream split by destination facility. One bidder described segregating waste streams by
  the facility each goes to. That is internal to disposal and does not need a row unless a diversion
  target is being tracked, in which case it becomes one row for the reporting, not many for the
  streams.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In demolition
the test fires hardest on the seams in section 5, because in this trade the seams are not edge cases,
they are the majority of the contested value.

Silence is a finding here more than in most trades. A structural demolition proposal on a project
whose defining feature was separating from an attached neighbour carried no separation line and no
bracing line at all. Two competitors priced both. The silent bid was not cheaper for the same work, it
was a different bid, and only the sheet's own row made that visible.

There is a second test worth applying, specific to this trade: for every item, ask who touches it and
in what order. Most demolition disputes are not about whether the work happens but about which of
three or four parties does which verb to the same object, and in what sequence. A window is removed by
one trade, dropped by another and disposed of by a third. A pipe is capped by one and hauled by
another. An elevator is decommissioned by one, staged by a second and removed by a third. If a scope
row names an object without naming a verb and an actor, it will be answered differently by everyone.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

These are near-boilerplate. Their value is that they name the party that must pick the work up, and if
no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

| Excluded by the demolition contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Cutting and capping of utilities at the street mains (excluded by nearly every bidder seen) | Excavation and utility contractor, or the site package |
| Make safe, disconnection and decommissioning of building systems (nearly every bidder) | The mechanical, electrical, plumbing and fire protection trades, before demolition mobilizes |
| Hazardous materials survey (every bidder seen, several noting it is required for all demolitions) | Owner or general contractor, via an environmental consultant |
| Third party air monitoring and final visual inspection and clearance (every bidder seen, including abatement bidders) | Owner or general contractor, via an independent monitoring firm, because regulation forbids self-clearance |
| Shoring, bracing, underpinning and support of excavation (nearly every bidder) | Specialist shoring contractor, or occasionally a demolition bidder that has retained an engineer |
| Engineering, structural design, professional engineer survey and layout (nearly every bidder) | General contractor or engineer of record |
| Temporary protection, poly sheeting, barricades, dust and noise barriers, temporary partitions (most bidders) | General contractor, and frequently unassigned |
| Demolition permit fees, and often the permit itself (most bidders, though a minority include one or both) | General contractor or owner |
| Dumpster permits, sidewalk permits, street opening permits (commonly) | General contractor |
| Police and fire details, flagmen, traffic and pedestrian management (every bidder seen) | General contractor |
| Backfilling, import of material, compaction, dewatering, rock excavation (every bidder seen) | Site package |
| Deep foundation elements such as piles and caissons (nearly every bidder, always conditionally) | Site or foundation package, frequently unassigned |
| Erosion and sediment control, temporary fencing, tree protection (nearly every bidder) | Site package |
| Rodent and pest control (commonly) | General contractor, frequently unassigned |
| Removal and disposal of loose furniture, equipment and contents (commonly) | Owner, before mobilization |
| Salvage of items for owner or general contractor reuse, and their storage and reinstallation (most bidders, while claiming scrap title in the same document) | General contractor, or a salvage specialist, frequently unassigned |
| Elevator decommissioning (most bidders) | Elevator contractor |
| Refrigerant recovery and reclaim (about half) | Mechanical contractor, priced in hours |
| Universal waste, bulbs, ballasts, mercury switches (about half, while about half include it) | Abatement contractor, or unassigned |
| Patching, repairing, matching, infilling and preparation of surfaces for new finishes (nearly every bidder) | The following trade, and this is a recurring real gap |
| Removal of floor adhesive, mastic, levelling compound, grinding and shot blasting (nearly every interior bidder) | Flooring package, or unassigned |
| Removal of additional layers of wall, ceiling or flooring beyond one (most interior bidders) | Unassigned, and it becomes a change order |
| Saw cutting and coring penetrations for building systems (some interior bidders, while scope sheets carry it as a demolition row) | Contested between demolition and the building systems trades |
| Winter conditions and snow removal (commonly) | General contractor |
| Overtime, premium time, second shift, multiple mobilizations (every bidder seen) | General contractor, and priced as a change |
| Site restoration, paving, sidewalks, fencing, landscaping (every bidder seen) | Site package |
| Power, water, temporary lighting, water meter and water permit (nearly every bidder) | General contractor |
| Bonds (nearly every bidder, several quoting an adder as a percentage of contract value) | General contractor, priced as an alternate |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate. Repeat bidders reproduce the same list verbatim across
projects and building types, and some of the language circulates between competitors. Two unrelated
companies were seen using an identical qualification about standard slab, foundation and footing
dimensions on two different projects. A demolition exclusion list is a map of the trade, not a
statement about the set.

What is project signal, because it was written for this building and appears nowhere else in that
bidder's other proposals:

- An assumption about a condition the bidder cannot see and cannot verify. A bidder assumed that the
  wall shared with the neighbouring building has a bond breaker separating the two structures. Another
  assumed the building had no basement and was slab on grade, on a building that later rounds describe
  as having a basement, basement walls and a fire pump room. These assumptions are the price.
- An exclusion or inclusion tied to a named drawing, a grid line range, or a utility authority
  sign-off list. When a bidder bounds its foundation scope to what falls outside specific structural
  grid lines, it has read the set.
- A statement of what the site will look like at handover. Bidders describe the finish condition
  differently and each description implies a different quantity of work.
- Any statement about the neighbour: protection of the adjacent roof, taking care with an adjacent
  residential building, temporary support during separation. These are read from the site, not from a
  template.
- A disagreement with the documents. One bidder told the general contractor that if a
  pre-demolition regulatory survey had been purchased, its materials should have been quantified as
  regulation requires, and they were not. When a bidder and the general contractor disagree about
  whether the documents define the scope, that is a documents problem and it belongs upstream as a
  request for information, not downstream as a leveling adjustment.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number. Violating them converts the lump sum into a change order.

- One mobilization, one phase, first shift, standard eight hour weekday shifts. Nearly universal.
  Phasing an interior demolition around an occupied tenant breaks this assumption on every bid
  simultaneously.
- Continuous and unrestricted access and egress for the equipment required. Nearly universal on
  structural work, and it is the assumption that fails first on a constrained urban site.
- Named access resources on interior work, which are priced conditions and not requests: dedicated
  use of a specified number of elevators for the duration, a stated number of containers permitted on
  site at once, sidewalk lay-down secured on both sides, specific exterior windows removed by others
  to get material out, work areas free of other personnel.
- Assumed existing dimensions. Slab thickness stated as up to a figure, foundation depth and footing
  size stated as standard, number of finish layers stated as one. Anything beyond the stated dimension
  leaves the scope. This is the most consequential qualification in the trade and it is entirely
  invisible on the drawings.
- Layout, dig safe notification, slab scanning and x-ray performed by the general contractor. Common
  on interior work, and it means the demolition contractor is cutting where somebody else marked.
- All salvageable material, ferrous and non-ferrous, becomes the property of the bidder. Nearly
  universal, and it is a consideration flowing to the bidder that offsets the price. Where an owner
  wants salvage back, that offset disappears and three new scope lines appear.
- Site conditions priced from a visual observation or from documents supplied, with an explicit
  disclaimer of the accuracy or completeness of those documents. Stated by some bidders outright.
- Escalation and disposal cost adjustment. Some bidders name escalation for labor, materials,
  disposal and equipment as an included item; one reserves an equitable adjustment for increases in
  disposal fees, taxes and fuel surcharges, with fuel indexed to a published national price index.
- Commercial terms attached to the number, which change what it is worth. Bidders were seen refusing
  flow-down obligations, indemnification, liquidated damages, retainage provisions and heightened
  insurance requirements unless separately negotiated; capping retainage at a stated percentage;
  requiring payment in full at completion with no retainage; charging interest on unpaid balances and
  recovering legal fees; requiring written notice and an opportunity to cure before any backcharge;
  and reserving a lien. One environmental firm's terms capped its total liability at its own fee and
  ran a hazardous-materials indemnity back to the client. Two proposals reached the general contractor
  with five pages of terms and conditions ahead of the first price. Capture these, because they
  materially change the value of a low number.
- Insurance basis. Whether an owner-controlled program applies is a real variable here. One bidder
  based its number on the assumption that one was in place and priced conventional coverage as an add
  alternate worth low single-digit percent of the base; another priced its whole bid twice, with and
  without.
- Bond adder, quoted as a percentage of contract value by several bidders. The same bidder quoted
  double the rate on larger projects as on a small one, so it is not a fixed company number.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section that matters most in this trade, and on a gut renovation it should lead your
reading. Demolition has almost no furnish component and an enormous custody component. The recurring
question is not what gets removed but who performs which verb on it, in what order.

### The demolition and abatement seam

This is the defining seam of the trade and it is worth understanding as a sequence rather than a
boundary.

The survey is the hinge. Before a hazardous materials survey exists, every demolition bidder handles
it the same way: it excludes hazardous materials entirely, states that it assumed none are present,
and offers to price once a report is provided. Proposals say this in almost identical words across
companies and across projects. One bidder alone was seen doing something different, carrying an
explicitly labelled abatement budget based on standard assumptions, which made its total look high
against a field carrying nothing at all for the same risk. That is the honest bid, and a leveling
sheet with no row for it punishes it.

When the survey lands, the same bidder re-issues, and the shape changes completely. The abatement
appears as a second priced number on the same document, and it is the only line in the entire trade
that is quantified. It is quantified because the survey supplies the quantities: square feet of
flooring and mastic, counts of pipe fittings, square feet of duct insulation, linear feet of caulk,
counts of lights, ballasts and mercury switches. Four bidders on one project priced against that same
quantity set, which makes it the only genuinely comparable line in the package. On another project the
enumerated survey scope ran fourteen material rows split by building zone, from pipe insulation and
floor tile throughout, to specific ceiling tile glue on one floor, to seven separate roof sealant and
flashing conditions.

Everything that goes wrong with this seam goes wrong inside the survey itself:

- Surveys carry a quantified table and an unquantified one. The second lists materials that tested
  negative but must be treated as asbestos anyway, typically because a contaminated layer above them
  cannot be separated. Bidders priced different readings of whether those materials were already
  inside the quantified figure, and said so. The general contractor was still asking the question
  weeks later.
- Surveys quantify some hazards and merely identify others. A lead paint section was seen naming the
  components affected, structural steel, columns, safety poles, doors, frames and an overhang ceiling,
  with no square or linear footage for any of them. A bidder instructed to price whatever the report
  shows, no more and no less, answered that the report does not show enough to price. That scope was
  still unpriced at the end of the round.
- Surveys get revised, and the revision adds assumed materials that arrive as a separate add.
- Bidders restate the survey's counts in ways that do not reconcile with the survey or with each
  other, which matters because the count is the only comparable thing in the line.
- A survey may assume material behind walls and ceilings that nobody has opened, and state the assumed
  count separately from the observed one. Whether a bidder carried both is invisible in a lump sum.

Three further things always leave the abatement package:

- Third party air monitoring and the final visual inspection and clearance. Regulation requires the
  owner or general contractor to hire an independent monitoring firm, so no abatement contractor can
  carry it. One bidder helpfully told the general contractor roughly what it costs. It arrives as its
  own bought package, often from the firm that wrote the survey.
- The regulatory notification clock. A statutory working-day notice runs before abatement can begin,
  commonly around two weeks. It is invisible on the drawings and it sits in front of everything.
- The selective demolition needed to reach the hazardous material. One bidder holding both the
  demolition and the abatement scope still excluded demolition required to access asbestos on
  surfaces noted to remain. Since it held both packages, that work had no owner anywhere in the bid
  list.

Note also that a demolition contractor may handle lead as a worker-protection matter under the
occupational standard rather than as licensed abatement, and price it inside the demolition number
while excluding lead remediation by name. Those are two different treatments of the same material and
the leveling sheet needs to know which one it bought.

Finally, the filing convention. Where a bidder prices demolition and abatement on one document, a
general contractor commonly files the identical file under both trade tokens. On one project more
than a dozen byte-identical file pairs sat under both labels. A bidder count taken off a folder, or
off a leveling sheet's ranking rows, will overstate the real bid list substantially; one interior
ranking carried nineteen rows for about thirteen distinct companies, because superseded rounds and
current rounds each got a row.

### The utility seam, in three parts

This seam is consistently excluded and consistently misread as one thing. It is three.

1. Cut and cap at the street main. Excluded by nearly every demolition bidder. It comes back priced by
   an excavation and utility contractor, and that proposal looks nothing like a demolition proposal:
   it enumerates by service, counts them, prices crew days and police detail days, carries named
   allowances for municipal excavation permits and utility authority inspection fees with overages
   billed separately, includes restoration of the excavated area in kind with backfill and paving, and
   shows overhead and profit as a visible separate line. It also flags where a fire service line may
   need separate fire department permitting to be coordinated by the general contractor or the
   sprinkler contractor.
2. Make safe, disconnection and decommissioning of systems inside the building. A different scope,
   excluded separately, and it belongs to the building systems trades. From the mechanical side it is
   sold as its own small package: cut, cap and make safe only, with an explicit statement that all
   dropping to the floor and removal is by others, plus a bank of refrigerant reclaim and drain-down
   hours with a per-day overrun rate beyond it.
3. Drop and dispose. Once the systems trades have capped and made safe, somebody has to bring the
   material down and take it away. On interior work this is demolition's, and a scope sheet was seen
   asking the question explicitly, whether dropping systems to the floor is by demolition and not by
   the systems trades. One bidder described the arrangement precisely: the trades make safe, cut, cap
   and drop to floor in managed pieces, and the demolition contractor removes, disposes, and retains
   the non-salvage items the trades leave behind.

Two consequences. First, a scope line saying utilities without naming which of the three is
unanswerable. Second, the general contractor usually ends up carrying the make-safe line itself. On
one project it sat as a general contractor budget line at an identical figure for both a union and an
open shop buyout, outside both the demolition and the abatement packages, which is the clearest
possible statement that nobody in this trade prices it.

Watch also for the risk transfer. One bidder excluded damage to any mechanical system left live during
demolition, which puts the entire consequence of an incomplete make-safe back on the general
contractor.

### Other seams

- Salvage, which is two opposite things in one word. Nearly every bidder claims title to all
  salvageable material, ferrous and non-ferrous, as a right, and it is consideration flowing to the
  bidder that reduces its number. In the same breath most exclude any salvage for owner or general
  contractor reuse, and separately exclude removing, stacking, packaging, storing, transporting or
  reinstalling anything designated for reuse. One bidder split it cleanly: the owner packages and
  stores what it wants, and the salvage value stays with the bidder. Where an owner wants material
  back, expect the credit to vanish and handling lines to appear. On buildings with historic fabric
  the protect-and-maintain block is large and is answered inconsistently, and one bidder disposed of
  the entire block by making historic items and conservation the general contractor's responsibility.
- Product take-back programs. One scope sheet carried a carpet reclamation block: the carpet
  manufacturer takes its own product back, the demolition contractor stockpiles, palletises and
  shrinkwraps it by named floor, and a disposal credit flows back per the manufacturer's terms. This
  turns a disposal line into a handling line plus a credit, and neither the drawings nor the
  specification will tell you it exists.
- Temporary protection, which is contested in both directions. Dust control is commonly included.
  Poly sheeting, barricades, fencing, dust and noise barriers, temporary partitions and weather
  protection are commonly excluded, sometimes with the demand that protection in the work area and on
  the path to the building be complete before mobilization. One bidder made protection and its
  maintenance the general contractor's in a single line. Where no other package carries it, this is a
  real gap and it is one of the largest recurring plugs.
- Temporary works and the structure seam. Shoring, bracing, needling and underpinning sit at the
  boundary with the structural package, and nearly everybody excludes them, so they land either with a
  specialist or unpriced. Where a demolition bidder does carry them, it is worth reading closely: one
  priced foundation wall bracing including engineering, shop drawings, channels, kickers, bent plates
  and gussets as its own number; another priced underpinning with a retained structural engineer and
  described a checkerboard sequence; a third priced wall bracing as an allowance with a counted number
  of braces, one month of rental inside the base, and a published monthly rate after that. That last
  is the instrument to ask for, because temporary works cost is duration, and a schedule slip on
  bracing has a known unit cost only if somebody published one. The general contractor otherwise plugs
  it, and one leveling sheet carried its own linear foot rate for wall bracing because no bidder
  supplied one.
- The floor, at the flooring trade's boundary. Interior demolition stops at one layer of finish and
  excludes adhesive, mastic, levelling compound, grinding and shot blasting, each by name. The
  flooring package generally expects a prepared substrate. Somebody must own the gap, and where it is
  priced it appears as a per square foot grinding alternate.
- The wall and ceiling, at the following trade's boundary. Patching, repairing, matching, sanding,
  infilling and preparation of surfaces for new work are excluded by nearly every demolition bidder.
  A scope sheet was seen asking how a sub-ceiling adhered to terra cotta would be removed and whether
  patching would then be needed by others, which is the right question and is exactly the shape of the
  gap.
- The roof, three ways contested. Whether the existing roof membrane comes off in the demolition
  package or the roofing package was written on a scope sheet as an open question. One interior bidder
  priced membrane removal down to deck as its own line, qualified to a rough scrape of high spots
  with no grinding of asphaltic material. Roof sealants and flashings are commonly on the abatement
  survey, so a single roof may be split three ways between demolition, abatement and roofing.
- The elevator, three parties. The elevator contractor decommissions; the general contractor may stage
  the shaft; demolition removes and disposes of cab, cables, guides, motors, equipment and switch
  gear. Most demolition bidders exclude decommissioning explicitly and one scope sheet asked outright
  what would be required of the elevator contractor. Meanwhile several interior bidders require
  dedicated use of an elevator for the duration of their work, and one excluded the costs of using
  the elevator, so the same machine is simultaneously an access resource and a demolition object.
- The neighbour. Separating an attached building is its own scope: saw cutting the shared wall,
  separating roof and floor, temporary support during the operation, and protection of the adjacent
  roof. Bidders price it separately, include it silently, or leave it out. Adjacent-property condition
  surveys, vibration and settlement monitoring, and mitigation of cosmetic damage are excluded by
  everyone.
- Site work, at the footprint line. Nearly every bidder draws its boundary at the building footprint,
  and several state a distance, such as ten feet outside it. Pavement, curb, walks, site furnishings,
  light poles, underground utilities and site structures then belong to the site package, and where no
  site package exists yet they belong to nobody.
- The haul, disposal and export. Demolition normally carries disposal of its own debris and its own
  containers, and excludes dumpster permits and the disposal of water runoff from dust control and
  wheel washing. Excavated material is a different thing: bidders cast it adjacent, rough grade it
  into the void, or load it directly into trucks supplied by others, and exclude double handling,
  trucking and disposal of it. Where soil actually leaves the site, that is a separate export contract,
  and it exists in the corpus as its own bought package. Note that a bidder planning to keep crushed
  concrete on site as a buttress is planning a much smaller haul than one disposing of everything.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

This is the section the drawings cannot cover, and in demolition it is unusually large, because the
subject of the work is a building nobody has measured and the documents describing it are the thinnest
in the set.

What demolition contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. A site visit. Cited constantly, and on interior work several bidders price from plans plus a visit
   and say so. One conditioned its agreement on a walk-through to confirm existing conditions before
   contract. Another noted that if it priced the job unseen, a visit would occur before mobilization.
   Where a scope sheet asks whether a bidder has walked the site, some answer that they have not, and
   their numbers should be read accordingly.
2. The hazardous materials survey, for the abatement number, and it is the only quantified source in
   the package. See section 5.
3. The demolition drawings, on interior work. These can be substantial, and one bid cited twenty-two
   of them. They carry the keynotes that drive the salvage and protect block and the operation rows.
4. The general contractor's issued scope sheet. On interior work this is frequently the real scope
   definition, more so than the drawings, and bidders answer it row by row and cite its row numbers
   back. It also grows between rounds, and every added row is a gap an earlier answer exposed.
5. The civil and utility drawings, for the cut and cap scope, plus the utility authority's own
   inspection sign-off list, which enumerates the services and is a better scope statement than the
   drawings.
6. The specification section for demolition, cited by some bidders, rarely quoted from.
7. Existing conditions and survey drawings, where they exist.

They do not price from a measured takeoff of the existing building. Stated areas are approximate,
inconsistent between bidders, and frequently absent.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of these moves the number:

- Union versus open shop labor. The largest single driver, and the split is stark. On a small
  residential teardown the non-union bid and the prevailing-wage bids differed by about half again,
  and that gap was essentially the whole spread. On a large interior package the low bidders were all
  open shop and the union bidders were at the top of the ranking. A union bidder was seen approaching
  its benefit fund for market recovery money to close the gap, and being told none was available
  because the project address was not grandfathered and would require fully union labor. Market
  recovery subsidy is a real instrument in this trade, it is decided address by address, and it can
  fail.
- Prevailing wage applicability, which is a third basis and not a variant of the first two.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies.
- What is actually behind the finishes. Number of layers of flooring, wall and ceiling; slab
  thickness; foundation depth and footing size; whether a shared wall has a bond breaker; whether the
  building has a basement at all. Bidders state assumptions for all of these and the assumption is the
  price. A bidder assuming no basement on a building that has one is not a cheap bidder.
- Whether the hazardous materials survey exists yet, and if so whether it is complete enough to price
  from. This is the single largest determinant of whether the bids on the table are comparable.
- Who supplies power and water, and whether a water meter and permit are available. Dust control is
  usually priced assuming somebody else provides the water.
- Access, in detail, and on interior work this is priced. Which elevators and for how long, whether
  the sidewalk can be used for lay-down, whether containers can sit on the street or must be live
  loaded, how many at a time, how debris leaves the basement and the roof, whether a hoist exists and
  when. A bidder offered about eleven percent off its number purely for permission to sequence the
  roof work after the hoist was operational.
- Whether the building is occupied. One project ran the entire gut around a live commercial tenant,
  and the leveling sheet's normalizing denominator explicitly subtracted that tenant's area. Occupancy
  drives phasing, shifts, protection, noise and access all at once, and none of it is on the drawings.
- Union interference and jurisdictional friction, which one bidder made an explicit general contractor
  responsibility.
- Whether the project carries a participation goal for certified minority, woman-owned or
  disadvantaged businesses. Several demolition bidders lead with their certifications on the
  letterhead, one offered full certified participation as a named inclusion, and a leveling sheet
  carried a bidder's certification in its own ranking row. Demolition is an early, self-contained,
  labor-heavy package, which makes it a common vehicle for meeting a participation goal, and that can
  outweigh a price difference. Nothing on the drawings says so.
- Whether an owner wants anything back. Salvage rights are worth real money to the bidder, and a
  reversal removes a credit and adds handling scope.
- Whether the crushed material stays on site as fill or buttress, or is exported.
- Escalation and disposal cost movement, which some bidders price and some reserve the right to
  adjust for.
- Schedule pressure, shift, and the number of mobilizations. Every bid assumes one mobilization and
  first shift.
- The regulatory notification clock before abatement can start.
- Whether the general contractor will do the layout, dig safe notification and slab scanning, which
  most interior bidders assume it will.

Set-stage behavior is pronounced in this trade because of the survey. Before the survey exists, every
number on the table excludes hazardous materials and the bids are comparable to each other but not to
the eventual cost. After the survey lands, the abatement number appears and it is the one quantified,
comparable line in the package. Treat any pre-survey demolition bid as a demolition-only number with a
known unpriced risk sitting beside it, and expect the abatement to be a meaningful fraction of the
demolition line, in the corpus running from roughly a third to two thirds of it and once approaching
parity. Note also that a demolition number can sit unchanged for months across rounds while everything
around it moves: one bidder's demolition line was byte-identical nine months later, including a line
promising escalation for the earlier year. And a bid on the same building from a pursuit two years
apart came in slightly higher in the earlier year, so escalation is not visible in this trade's
pricing the way it is in others.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Lump sum. The dominant unit by a wide margin, for the whole package.
- Square feet of building, as a stated approximate area and as the leveling denominator. Where several
  bidders state an area for the same building they disagree, so the denominator is the estimator's
  choice, not a fact.
- Gross square feet of the building, on interior work, and the denominator may exclude an occupied
  tenant area, which must be named or the rate is meaningless.
- Each, per structure, where there is more than one building. The only grain that survives a partial
  award.
- Each, per element, for the few counted things: windows as an alternate unit price, beams to be
  chipped, columns to be exposed, elevator cars, utility services to be cut and capped.
- Square feet and linear feet of surveyed hazardous material, which is the abatement unit and comes
  from the survey rather than from the drawings.
- Linear feet for wall bracing and underpinning.
- Square feet for floor grinding, offered as an alternate rate.
- Crew days and police detail days, for utility cut and cap.
- Days, for a support laborer carried across a stated duration, and for standby when the contractor
  is idled by others.
- Months, for temporary bracing rental beyond an included period.
- Hours, for shift premiums, refrigerant reclaim and time and materials work.
- Percent of contract value, for bond and for conventional general liability where no owner program
  applies.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Labor basis, union versus open shop versus prevailing wage. The largest driver by a wide margin,
   and on small work it is nearly the entire spread.
2. Scope breadth, which in this trade outranks price as a cause of ranking movement. See below.
3. Whether abatement is inside the number, and whether a survey exists to price it from.
4. The seams: cut and cap, make safe, bracing and shoring, protection. Where a bidder carries them
   they can add half again to a base, and where it does not the general contractor plugs them anyway.
5. Access, sequence and phasing on interior work. Priced in real money, and a sequencing concession
   was worth about eleven percent of a large interior number.
6. Foundation treatment. Whether foundations come out to a stated depth, come out except at property
   lines, or stay entirely in place. Two bidders on one set disagreed completely and neither was wrong
   on the drawings.
7. Salvage rights, which flow value to the bidder and reverse if an owner wants material back.
8. Layers and concealed conditions, which are priced as an assumption and change-ordered when the
   assumption fails.
9. Whether debris stays on site as fill or buttress, or is hauled and disposed.
10. Shift, mobilization count and duration.
11. Commercial terms: bond, insurance basis, retainage, escalation and flow-down.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. Structural demolition of a single building ranges about
two to three times from the lowest to the highest cost per square foot within one project, with bid
lists of six to nine bidders. Interior demolition is far wider: one project ranked more than a dozen
distinct companies across a range of about eight times per gross square foot. That range is not a
price range. The bottom of it was a proposal that reproduced the operation list faithfully and stated
no extent at all, and the top of it included superseded rounds of bidders who later dropped by a fifth
to two fifths. On small residential teardowns the honest spread is about one and a half times and it
tracks the labor basis almost exactly, except where a bidder priced a different number of buildings.

Which leads to the pricing convention that matters most in this trade. In demolition, an apparent
outlier is a scope difference until proven otherwise, in both directions. The high outlier on one
small project was pricing three buildings and the utility cut and cap where the field priced two
buildings without it, and it read as roughly five times the low bid. The low outlier on an interior
project was answering the scope rows without the vertical extent, and it read as a quarter of the next
bid. Neither number was about price. Before ranking anything in this trade, confirm the building
count, the vertical extent, whether abatement is in, and whether the printed total is a base bid.

That last point is worth its own warning. Bidders roll add alternates and abatement into one printed
subtotal, and a leveling sheet was seen carrying such a total as a base bid, ranking that bidder last
when its demolition line alone would have ranked near the front. For every other bidder on that same
sheet, the estimator correctly took the demolition line only. Read the breakout, never the total.

Finally, an estimator does not compare base bids in this trade either. It compares base bid plus
plugs, and the plugs are unusually large because so much of the contested value sits in seams nobody
carries. The plugs to expect are utility cut and cap; make safe of building systems, which was seen
carried as its own general contractor budget line outside both packages; temporary protection and dust
barriers; shoring, bracing and its engineering, with removal; underpinning; demolition permit and
fees; police and fire details; third party air, dust and vibration monitoring; the hazardous materials
survey; the final abatement clearance; pre-construction condition survey; temporary water meter and
power; floor preparation beyond the finish layer; patching after demolition; and hoisting. A scope
line set that names all of them is doing the estimator's plugging for it, and on this trade that is
most of the leveling work.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly three dozen demolition proposals plus a handful of boundary
records from the licensed abatement, utility cut-and-cap, mechanical make-safe and third-party
monitoring packages, together with four estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four projects in one
regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The projects cover four genuinely different
demolition problems: a whole-building teardown of a single-storey commercial structure, a full gut of
an eleven-storey downtown building with a live commercial tenant, a partial demolition requiring
separation from an attached neighbour, and a small residential teardown of two houses. Proposals span
design development, permit, construction document and conformed rounds, and the bidder pool includes
several companies appearing on more than one project, which is what makes the boilerplate versus
project signal distinction in section 4 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- The corpus is one market, one general contractor and four projects. Nothing here is tested against
  public bidding, institutional or healthcare work, industrial decommissioning, or any market where
  demolition is bid to an owner directly rather than to a construction manager.
- Only one project was a large interior gut, so the entire interior demolition grain in section 3,
  including the salvage and protect block and the hundred-and-forty-row scope sheet, rests on that
  project alone. The second interior data point is a much smaller scope sheet.
- Only one project involved separation from an attached structure, so the separation, bracing and
  neighbour protection material rests on it.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus. Nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation and which were bought out. The one buyout-side artifact is a general contractor
  contingency worksheet, which is where the make-safe plug was found.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that assumed slab thicknesses, assumed layer
  counts and access qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own
  language rather than observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- The corpus does not show what happens when the assumed conditions fail. Every bidder states a
  dimension it cannot verify, and no document in the corpus records what was actually found.
- The corpus has not shown demolition on a structure requiring engineered dismantlement, high reach or
  explosive methods, nor any building over about eleven storeys.
- The corpus has not shown a project where an owner-directed salvage or deconstruction program ran,
  so the reversal of the salvage credit is described from exclusions rather than from a priced example.
- The corpus has not shown a waste diversion target being enforced. Diversion reporting appears once,
  as an exclusion, so who owns it is unknown.
- The abatement material here is deliberately a boundary read, not the trade. Abatement means and
  methods, containment design, worker protection levels, disposal manifesting and the licensing regime
  are outside what these records support, and abatement deserves its own file.
- No project in the corpus had contaminated soil, storage tanks or a licensed site professional
  actually engaged, so those exclusions are recorded without a counterparty ever being observed.

This file carries no structural gap list. The first corpus round did not draw one out for this
trade; a later round writes it where the corpus supports one.
