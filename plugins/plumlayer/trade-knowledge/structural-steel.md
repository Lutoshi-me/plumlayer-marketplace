# Structural Steel

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the structural steel
package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets.
This file speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. Structural steel looks like the most determinate package on
the job, because the frame is drawn, sized and scheduled. It is not. What the drawings fix is the
frame; what the market disagrees about is everything attached to the frame, everything above it,
everything done to it in the shop, and what the number is still worth a week after it was written.
The scope sheet is what closes those questions, and this file is mostly about which questions it has
to ask.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Section 051200 (Structural Steel Framing) is the anchor, with 053100 (Metal Deck) riding inside the
same package and 051000 (Structural Metal Framing) used as the cost code on most estimator workbooks.
Bidders cite sections unevenly. Some list four or five sections across the structural, deck and
fabrication range in a header line; most cite none and quote sheet and detail numbers instead. Sheet
references are the reliable identifier in this trade, and the section number is a weak one: the same
scope appears under several numbers, and two workbooks in one office can disagree about which.

Do not assume a specification exists. On one construction-document round all four bidders
independently recorded that no structural steel specification had been issued, in four different
wordings, and the estimator's own scope row said the same. Elsewhere a bidder records the project
manual as none provided, or cites the division only as a code reference. Where the specification is
absent, the drawings are the whole contract, and several bidders say so in their own terms.

### The two kinds of bidder, which decide the packaging before any price question

The single most useful thing to establish about a structural steel bid list is which of two kinds of
company each bidder is, because the kind decides what the number covers.

- The tonnage fabricator. Typically out of market, often cross border, usually certified, selling
  frames by the ton. It bids the frame, the deck and the erection, and it excludes miscellaneous
  metals by name, with a routed counterparty, on every job. Where it is precise it defines the
  boundary by citing the clause of the industry code of standard practice that separates structural
  steel from everything else, which is the market's own written definition of where this trade ends
  and is worth reading before drawing the line by hand.
- The local fabricator and erector with its own miscellaneous metals department. It carries both
  halves of the division in one letter, and it prices them as two named sums.

Both kinds bid the same jobs. On one project the same solicitation drew three of each, and on
another the split ran three to three again. The kinds sort cleanly and they hold: of the companies
appearing on more than one project, most state the same position everywhere they appear, and the
position tracks the shop rather than the building.

So the packaging question has a specific answer, and it is neither of the two the market's own
paperwork seems to offer. On new construction with a real steel frame, whether miscellaneous metals
arrives inside the structural steel proposal is a property of the bidder, not of the project. Both
answers occur in the same round on the same drawings. Which answers you get is a property of the bid
list, so read the list before reading the numbers.

Reading the list starts before the documents are open, because the filing already answers this
question for each bidder. A proposal logged under both a structural steel trade and a miscellaneous
metals trade is a bid that spans both packages, recorded as such by the buyer at the time. Section 2
sets out how far that holds and what else it tells you. Note the direction it settles the boundary
in: a document deliberately logged under both trades is a combined package by the buyer's own
reckoning at the moment of receipt, whatever the proposal's own printed house language later says
about excluding miscellaneous metals.

Reading the list also means not over-counting it. Bidder identity is softer than a ranking makes it
look: the same operation appears under several company names, sometimes at one address, sometimes
across two legal entities in two countries, and one shop in this trade accounts for at least three
ranking identities across four projects. A count of company names is an upper bound on the number of
independent bidders, never a count of them.

Five consequences follow, and each of them bites.

How the packages are bought and how they are bid are different questions with different answers.
Every estimator workbook seen buys them apart, on a structural steel tab and a metal fabrications
tab ranked separately, sometimes selecting a different winner on each. Roughly half the proposals on
a combined-capable bid list arrive with both halves inside one document. The split is real at the
buying end and unreliable at the bidding end, and the gap between the two is where the leveling
errors live.

A combined proposal has to be split, and the splitting is done per bidder by hand. In one workbook
one bidder's combined proposal was correctly split across two cost codes while another's was left
whole on the structural tab with a note in the adjacent cell reading that it includes misc metal, so
that tab ranks a steel-only number against a steel-plus-misc number. Two related failures: a
combined bidder whose miscellaneous half sits inside its structural number gets no column at all on
the metal fabrications tab, so a company competing for that scope is invisible in the ranking for
it; and a combined printed total entered whole into a miscellaneous metals ranking lands last, where
its real miscellaneous sum would have ranked third.

The combined document rarely produces one number. Nearly every combined proposal keeps two named
sums with a boundary drawn in prose between them, and several print a third figure that is the two
added together. Only on the smallest packages does the merge become genuine, with lintels, columns,
wall ties, handrails, a ladder and fencing in one flat list under a single price a bidder calls a
package price. Treat the printed total as suspect by default: a combined total can be a third larger
than the structural sum it contains, and an accounting-system estimate spanning four separately
bought packages ran nearly six times its own structural row.

The priced breakout beats the printed exclusion list, always. Several combined proposals exclude
miscellaneous metals in their standing house language on one page and price a named list of
miscellaneous items on another. The house sentence is a pasted template that travels between
projects unchanged; the priced list was written for this set. Where the two disagree the priced list
is the offer, and where there is no priced breakout the question has to be asked rather than
inferred from the boilerplate.

Silence is not carriage. A frame-only bid may never mention miscellaneous metals at all, and its
inclusion list is then the whole statement: where that list reads leveling plates, anchor bolts,
columns, beams, bracing and deck, nothing else is in the number. Do not read an absent exclusion as
an inclusion.

### What moves a combined shop off the miscellaneous half

A shop that sells both halves does not always bid both. Where a company changes position between
projects, the variable seen is the character of the miscellaneous scope rather than the vintage of
the building. Where the miscellaneous work is ordinary building metal, meaning pan stairs with pipe
rails, pit ladders, sump grates, sill angles, loose lintels and bollards, a combined shop carries it.
Where the miscellaneous work is ornamental or architectural, meaning a feature stair with stainless
offset handrails and cable guardrail, or a restoration's ornamental iron package, the same shop bids
the frame alone and routes the ornamental work to a specialist by name. On one restoration the
separately bid ornamental iron package was about two and a half times the structural steel package
beside it, and every structural bidder stayed out of it.

Job size moves the mix of kinds on the list. The largest new-construction frames draw tonnage
fabricators and the split holds cleanly, with the frame bidders excluding miscellaneous metals by an
enumerated list and specialists bidding the other tab. The mid-range is where the combined document
with two named sums dominates. The smallest packages draw one local shop and one undivided number,
and the workbook may have no structural steel tab at all, leveling the whole thing under a metal
stairs or metal fabrications cost code.

Where the structural work is reinforcement and infill at an existing building rather than a frame,
the trade can disappear entirely. On one such project there is no structural steel tab, no structural
steel bid folder and no structural steel bidders, and the structural content is nine rows inside the
metal fabrications sheet: new members at a penthouse roof, infill beams over existing dunnage,
vibration isolation pads, grouting for continuous bearing, scanning for reinforcement before
drilling, priced per member and per day rather than per ton.

### The boundary rule that explains the sheet-range exclusions

On wood framing over a steel and concrete podium, which is the dominant building type here, every
fabricator writes some form of exclusion covering the upper floors. Four bidders in one round
excluded the same set of upper-floor sheets in four different wordings, and a fifth drew the same
line by naming grid lines at two levels instead. Positions seen include everything above a named
level furnish only, everything above a named level excluded outright, no steel above the third floor
included, and one bidder pricing a level furnish and install except three named beams on one grid
line, which stay furnish only because they are not attached to a steel column.

That last bidder explained the rule the others state coarsely, and one wrote it as a principle
rather than item by item: anything not attached to the steel frame is furnish only. Attachment
defines custody. The elevation is a proxy and the connection is the real variable.

The mature estimator scope sheet reaches the same rule from the other side, routing sixteen named
items between the two packages on the basis that the same item belongs to structural steel at the
steel-framed levels and to miscellaneous metals at the wood-framed levels. Relieving angles and
hangers at steel-framed levels are structural steel; the identical items at wood-framed levels are
miscellaneous metals. Balcony framing and decking follow the same rule.

So the package boundary follows the framing type, not the item type, and the drawings define it
without anyone writing it down. That single rule accounts for every sheet-range exclusion in the
trade, and a scope sheet that does not state the boundary explicitly will collect four incompatible
answers to it.

### Collapse attractors

Scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another division or section:

- Metal deck. Universal, inside every fabricate-and-erect bid seen, and installed by the erector.
  It leaves at the edges only: deck bearing on light gauge framing or on glue-laminated timber is
  commonly supplied and not installed, stated by several bidders in three separate parts.
- Open web steel joists. A live question rather than an absent one. Joists appear as a standing
  tickable row on a fabricator's own face sheet, as an included line paired with deck, as a named
  exclusion, as a stated tonnage with a count, as a shop-primed item whose finish the joist supplier
  sets, and as the cheapest of three priced structural system options at concept stage. Expect the
  answer to be somewhere in the document, and do not read silence as absence.
- Shear studs. Included by most and rarely quantified separately. Vertical studs are supplied and
  installed by the erector; horizontal studs must be shop installed by the fabricator, stated
  independently by two erectors.
- Precast plank, on projects that use it. One bidder supplies and installs it, another installs with
  supply by others, and a third offers a menu of two base prices for two plank widths plus two plank
  supply alternates. Plank width is the organizing question, and it also chooses the crane.
- The tower crane, sometimes. On one project the crane has its own division 05 leveling tab with four
  ranked suppliers, and one steel bidder carries the crane inside its lump sum while fixing its
  location by grid line in the proposal text, and a second carries it for its own erection only. The
  same equipment sits in two packages at once and the two tabs never reference each other.
- Metal stairs, on the small and mid-range jobs, and on renovation. On the large frames stairs go
  with miscellaneous metals.
- Masonry and fire wall anchors, in a reverse seam: supplied by another trade, delivered to the
  fabricator's own shop at no cost, and shop installed.
- A proprietary composite beam system's licence fee. On one project two of four conventional
  fabricators state the licence fee for the same system inside their base bids, and the leveling
  sheet's cost-per-square-foot denominator row is named after the system. Where the market has
  standardized on a system, its fee is an ordinary line rather than an exotic alternative.

### Specialty carve-outs

Scope that leaves this package even when it looks structural:

- Miscellaneous metals as an enumerated block, from the tonnage fabricators: stairs, handrails,
  guardrails, grating and grating stair treads, loose lintels, ladders, pipe bollards, elevator
  framing and angles, railings, stainless steel, trench and sump pit frames.
- Everything aluminum, including balcony rails, and one fabricator excludes even aluminum items that
  are structural in function, naming aluminum rails supporting precast panels and aluminum clips at
  precast walls.
- Light gauge metal framing, in three parts that must be read separately: installing the framing,
  connecting steel to it, and installing deck on it. A package that carries deck does not necessarily
  carry deck on light gauge, and the third part is the one that gets missed.
- Reinforcing steel and reinforcing accessories, routed to concrete by every bidder seen.
- Architecturally exposed structural steel. Excluded by name wherever it appears, carried as an
  estimator allowance on one sheet, answered yes by exactly one bidder anywhere. Nobody in this
  has been seen pricing it.
- Fireproofing, and the removal and replacement of it around erection interferences.
- Roof anchors and fall protection hardware, routed to a specialties division where anchor
  manufacturers bid it directly. One bidder draws the finest line seen, including the attachment
  plate for the fall arrest anchors and excluding the fall arrest anchor plate.
- Prefabricated balconies, and the embed plates at their connection to the slab.
- Expansion joint covers, corner guards, edgings and anything associated with millwork.

### Fabrication and erection

The market usually sells the two together. Most documents naming their operations name fabricate,
deliver and erect as one product, and no document splits them on many projects. But the split market
is real, not exceptional: standalone erection-only and install-only bids appear on several projects
on several projects, and integrated bidders asked to break out their internal erection carry disclose
it readily.

Erection runs roughly a quarter to three tenths of a fabricate-and-erect number. Standalone erection
bids land in that band; fabricate-and-erect bidders asked on a scope row to disclose their internal
erection carry give figures in the same band; and the disclosed internal carries bracket a standalone
erection bid on the same drawings within single-digit percentages. Independently, an estimator's own
internal budget splits the package at two per-ton rates whose erection share is about three tenths.
The split market and the integrated market price erection at the same level, and the fabricator is
not hiding a margin in it. Note the asymmetry: the market usually does not split, and the estimator
always splits, internally.

The grain of the two halves is different and no single leveling basis holds both. The fabricator
thinks in tons and in pounds per square foot. The erector thinks in pieces, picks and crane days: one
erection proposal's final page counts columns, lateral columns, beams, tube beams, bracings, frame
openings, hangers, posts, outriggers, column splices, linear feet of deck support angle, shear studs,
moment connections, miscellaneous picks and crane days, sixteen categories against one lump sum. A
ranking that divides by tons cannot express an erection bid, and rankings have tried: an erection-only
bid ranked first above every fabricate-and-erect bid in the same column, with a per-ton rate computed
and printed beside theirs, distinguished only by a note in the telephone cell. That ranking selected
a subcontractor.

Establish the package mode before comparing anything, and do not trust the document's own label. An
erection-only quote may be filed as steel erection while a fabricate-and-erect bid arrives on an
erector's letterhead with an acceptance block written for a fabricator-to-erector contract.

One further hazard belongs to the split market. An erector may be quoting several of the fabricators
on the same bid list at once, and one said so unprompted, naming the rival general contractors and
the fabricators it was also pricing. If the same erection number sits inside several fabricators'
bids, ranking those fabricators is not ranking independent prices.

### The transfer the split package leaves unpriced

Every erection-only proposal seen carries a block of obligations on the fabricator, each item ending
at no cost to the erector. Three different companies across two projects produce heavily overlapping
lists:

- punched tie-off holes in beam flanges a stated distance in from every beam end
- attachment points for perimeter cable on all exterior columns, and on interior columns at stair and
  shaft openings
- stanchions with pre-punched holes for perimeter cable around interior floor openings
- angle safety rail posts, and safety posts generally
- all field bolts and fasteners, supplied free
- erection bracing and shoring, engineered and supplied free
- all bent plate, base plates, stiffeners, gussets, clips and outriggers shop attached and welded
- all diagonal, tube and gravity-column connections designed as bolted only
- brick shelf shipped as shop-assembled units
- material delivered in erection sequence, to the hook

This is the central finding about buying the halves separately. That list is paid for by nobody when
fabrication and erection come from two documents, and it appears on no leveling sheet because it is
neither a scope row nor a dollar. If the package is being split, the list becomes scope rows on the
fabrication side or it becomes a change order.

### Labor basis

Union, open shop and prevailing wage all appear, and the basis does not predict rank. On one mixed
pool the two open-shop columns rank first and third and the three union columns rank second, fourth
and fifth; on two other projects the union column is third of four. Record the basis and do not
assume its direction.

The basis is a property of the package rather than of the job, and sometimes of the work group rather
than the package: one project ran structural steel union in every round while the miscellaneous
metals package on the same job in the same year ran open shop, and one combined bidder ran both bases
inside one proposal, union for steel erection and open shop for miscellaneous erection, priced by two
different estimators in the same shop. Another states open shop non-prevailing wage on page one and
prevailing wage installation on page two.

Two cautions on the estimator side. A labor basis is often recorded for a column whose proposal
states none, and columns get labelled union when the bidder's own face sheet says open shop, so the
workbook field is sometimes an attribution rather than a reading. And where a bidder prices a union
erection add on its own proposal, worth about a twentieth of its base, that number is the only
quantification of the labor question anywhere and it usually never reaches the sheet.

## 2. How the market bids

There is no default proposal shape. The same package on the same drawings draws documents with
nothing structurally in common, and the shape tells you the kind of company before the price does.
Fourteen distinct enumeration grains appeared across one set of twenty-two documents, and on a single
project's ten documents no two bidders answered in the same shape.

### What the filing tells you, before the document is open

How a bid is logged carries information about what it covers, independent of anything the bid says
about itself, because logging one bid under several trades is how an estimator records that it spans
several packages. The same document appears in two places on purpose.

The signal is strong enough to act on. Of the documents logged under both a structural steel trade
and a miscellaneous metals or metal fabrications trade, every one that is a bid on this package
carries both halves: most as two named sums in one letter, one as a single undivided package price
on a small job. Not one frame-only proposal is logged that way, and the frame-only bidders are the
larger half of the market. The only cross-logged documents that do not carry both halves are two
material supply quotes from suppliers belonging to another trade entirely, a joist and deck
manufacturer and a reinforcing steel supplier, and neither is a bid on this package at all. So the
rule reads in both directions: a bid logged under both trades covers both, and a bid logged under
one covers one.

The same practice marks the other seam in this trade. One erection-only quote is logged under a
structural steel trade and a structural steel erection trade at once, which is the same record being
made about the fabrication and erection boundary rather than the miscellaneous metals one. Wherever
a bid crosses a package line, expect to find it filed on both sides of the line.

Two cautions on reading filings, both of which look like the signal and are not:

- Ordinary variation in how a trade is written is not a package span. One bid logged under two
  spellings of the same steel trade carries no information at all. Read what the two trades are, not
  that there are two.
- Company identity is softer than the filing suggests. The same bid gets logged more than once, and
  bidders are logged under different names: one operation in this trade appears under at least three
  company names across four projects, one of them at an identical address under a different name,
  and another appears under two legal names and two countries' addresses. A bid invitation may name
  one company three ways in a single field, at an address that does not match the signature block on
  the quote it returned. Which legal entity is bidding is sometimes not resolvable from the file.

The consequence for leveling is direct and it runs against the reassuring direction. A package that
looks well covered may have fewer independent bidders behind it than the ranking rows suggest,
because one operation can occupy several rows under several names, and because one document logged
twice can be counted twice. Establish how many operations are bidding, not how many rows exist,
before reading a spread as evidence of competition.

The shapes actually seen:

- The bidder-minted mark takeoff. Every scope item gets its own mark, a quantity, a unit, a finish,
  structural and architectural sheet references, member sizes written out, separate supply and
  install columns and per-mark notes on what is by others. The deepest ran to roughly forty marks
  with landing areas and per-line assumptions beneath each. This is a takeoff presented as a
  proposal, and it is the single most valuable document a general contractor receives on this trade.
- The element count list with a finish column. Numbered groups split into furnish-and-install and
  furnish-only blocks, every line carrying a count, a finish and a drawing reference. Members counted
  and attributed to a floor, columns and beams by section size, moment connections counted, joists
  counted by series and depth, bridging by row and linear foot, deck by square foot and gauge per
  location, clips by room name and grid mark.
- The supply-versus-install matrix. A printed grid whose columns are included, supply only, supply
  and install, install only, and notes, answered per work family. Quantities rarely appear; sheet
  references do. This is a standard instrument rather than a quirk, and it is the market's own
  paperwork.
- The mode-coded list. Every work group carries a three-part code stating custody and shop finish
  together: fabricate and prime and install, fabricate and galvanize and install, fabricate and
  color-galvanize and install, fabricate and prime and supply. The legend is printed in the
  qualifications. Four finishes and two custody endings, per work group, in four characters. Where
  this notation appears there is no ambiguity left.
- The bidder-authored scope questionnaire. A reusable form carrying its own form number, with roughly
  thirty-five decision rows answered yes, no or not applicable and a comments column naming the
  counterparty for each. Its rows are, in order, testing agency, steel on architectural drawings,
  miscellaneous metals, grating, pergola posts, railing anchorage, wood-to-masonry anchor rods,
  mockups, brace axial capacity, hold downs, architecturally exposed steel, relieving angles, edge of
  slab, pour stop, masonry ties, seismic clips, opening frames split into structural and
  architectural with a count, reinforced openings, web doublers, beam penetrations by six named types
  each with a count, primer system, finish coats, three kinds of touch-up, anchor bolt survey,
  as-built survey, plumbness survey, acoustic deck insulation, safety cables split four ways, grout,
  caulking, site safety, six foot tie-off, mobilization count and schedule start. That is this
  trade's structural gap list written by the market rather than by an estimator, and the same form
  filled in on two different projects gives opposite answers on the primer row.
- The detail-referenced exclusion list. A short inclusion block and then an exclusion block of
  fourteen to twenty-nine sub-bullets, each carrying a sheet or detail reference. The enumerated axis
  is the drawing reference, and the deepest of these is a sheet-by-sheet reading of the structural
  set naming roughly thirty details, schedules and sheets it refuses.
- Clarifications and omissions. Thirty or more numbered clarifications asserting how the bidder read
  the drawings, then twenty or more omissions, and nothing priced by line at all.
- The lump sum with numbered clauses. Three or four scope lines all answered included, then thirty or
  more basis clauses and sixty or more exclusions carrying all the weight.
- The counted piece takeoff, from the erectors: pieces, picks and crane days.
- The email. Six lines, one number, a crane recommendation and a duration.

Two cross-cutting facts about grain. First, grain rises as package size falls: the most completely
enumerated documents are also the smallest numbers, because on a small package
the finish is the only thing distinguishing otherwise identical pieces. Second, counts are the
primary comparable in this trade and almost nobody states them. Where counts exist they are the whole
content; where they do not, the tonnage is a derivation from an assumed pounds per square foot that
varies by structural system from about eight to about fourteen.

What a proposal typically will state:

- A basis document, meaning a named drawing set with a date, and usually which addenda are carried.
  This is stated more reliably than in most trades and it is still not reliable enough to order
  rounds by, for the reasons below.
- A tonnage, from about half the fabricate-and-erect bidders. Where it is absent it is usually absent
  for good, and asking for it after the fact mostly fails: on one project one bidder of six answered
  a direct request for tonnage.
- A shop finish position, from nearly every bidder, in one sentence.
- An exclusion list, usually long, mixing a standing house block with project-specific rows carrying
  detail numbers.
- A document hierarchy. This is the one place the trade is genuinely unanimous: where structural and
  architectural drawings conflict the structural governs, where drawings and specifications conflict
  the drawings govern, and an item in the specification that does not appear on the drawings is
  excluded. One bidder goes further and excludes deck shown only on architectural drawings by name.
  The consequence is that anything an architect draws and an engineer does not is unbought by default.
- A validity window, and a commercial block covering escalation, deposits and retainage.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Counts of anything, unless the grain is one of the enumerated shapes.
- A beam penetration count, unless asked. See section 3.
- A mobilization count. One mobilization is the market default and appears on most documents, but one
  bidder assumed seven on one job and one on the same job later, another assumed six, and another
  assumed one per floor. Nothing in any set fixes it and nothing on any leveling sheet asks.
- A fabrication lead time. One bidder puts durations directly under its price, at six to
  eight weeks for shop drawings and six to eight for fabrication. Nobody quotes a mill delivery
  duration. The one hard dated lead time anywhere belongs to a purchased proprietary product, at ten
  to twelve weeks measured from order placement and receipt of approved shop drawings and verified
  field dimensions, which is a miscellaneous metals item inside a steel package setting the whole
  buyout date.
- A crew size or an erection duration, unless a scope sheet asks, in which case most answer.

### Revision habits

A revision in this trade is usually a documents artifact or a commercial one, and rarely a
straightforward reprice. The mechanisms actually seen, each of which needs a different diff to catch:

- Silent reprice. One bidder issued its written proposal twice under one proposal number, one date,
  one tonnage and word-identical scope text, at two numbers more than forty percent apart. Another
  issued a revision twenty-four days after its base round whose two documents differ in exactly five
  places: the date, the revision suffix, the three money figures and one added line naming a
  request-for-information log. Nothing but the numbers distinguishes the rounds.
- Scope entry as a price rise. A blanket exclusion of ten named upper-floor sheets simply stops
  appearing, at a price up a fifth. An exclusion of moment connections by detail number stops
  appearing, at a price up a sixth.
- Scope departure as a price decline. The cleanest instance: two issues of one proposal four days
  apart, the base down two percent while the stated tonnage went up. The whole movement is five tons
  of canopy leaving the base, visible only because one sentence lost a clause. A competitor priced
  that same canopy as an add in the same band as the drop.
- Comeback absorption. A base rises while the cited drawings, the addendum number, the tonnage and
  the wage basis all stay identical on page one, and everything that moved is disclosed on page
  three: furnish-only beams added, machine room steel adjusted, deck added, and a mill increase
  received that morning.
- Specification correction upward. Two days apart, the wall thickness of every one of a hundred and
  nine columns changed by one gauge step inside otherwise identical description lines, and the base
  rose about a percent and a half. A diff of totals shows a rise with no reason and a diff of counts
  shows nothing at all. Only a diff of the description strings finds it, and a rising number reads as
  escalation, which makes this harder to see than the falling form.
- Measurement basis mutation. A row keeps its quantity and changes its unit, one item running a
  stated length in linear feet in early rounds and a count in each in the last. A diff of extensions
  sees nothing; the unit column is where the change is.
- Breakout restructure. Six rail breakouts appeared and every figure stayed identical. No money moved
  and no sentence changed, and the breakouts are the information: they show what two competitors'
  cheaper rail substitution was actually worth.
- Uniform escalation re-issue. One proposal issued twice eight months apart on the same building,
  price up more than forty percent while the stated tonnage fell nearly a tenth, which is close to
  sixty percent per ton. Every qualification, every finish statement and a four-defect list of
  drawings not completed are word for word identical. The only substantive changes are the cited
  basis, a specification section appearing where the earlier issue recorded none, and the validity
  window tightening from ten days to seven. The earlier issue is addressed on its face to a different
  general contractor: the building was being bid by someone else in that round.
- Documents gap closure, which is visible only as assumptions disappearing. One bidder wrote eight
  project-specific qualifications at design development and five at construction documents, and the
  three that vanished are exactly the three member sizes it had invented because the earlier drawings
  did not size them. Nothing else in the document records the closure.
- The appearing disclaimer, and the disappearing one. Two bidders disclosed a wood-seam carve-out
  only in a revision, so a reader leveling the first round had no way to know the wood-attached steel
  was outside the erection scope. Run the exclusion diff forward as well as backward: a newly
  appearing exclusion is evidence about the earlier round.
- Basis restatement by the estimator rather than the bidder. A previous round's number from a bidder
  that did not rebid gets carried into the next round's ranking unchanged and ranked against fresh
  bids. On one project two of five ranking rows were stale, and on another a seven-month-old design
  development bid finished last against construction-document bids and widened the apparent spread.

### Ordering the rounds

Order by the cited basis document where you can, and know that it fails often enough to check. Two
rounds from one bidder carry identical basis lines while the price and the scope both move. Two
rounds from another are more than a year apart on their letterheads on a project whose bid period was
weeks, and neither cites any basis at all. One document's two pages carry dates a month apart with
different bid numbers and different estimators. Two independent bidders cite the same structural
drawing date three months in the future, so the error is in the issued document list and propagates
identically into every bid. Where the basis line is unreliable, the document's own revision marking
and its project-notes block are what separate the rounds.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Structural steel scope sheets run from one answered row to a hundred and twenty-one, and the row
count is the single best predictor of whether the trade closes. The thin sheets, meaning one to nine
answered rows, level five bids that answer seven different scope questions seven different ways on a
single row headed with the package name. The mature sheet, at a hundred and twenty-one numbered rows
in eleven blocks, drove four bidders to within one percent of each other. Target sixty to a hundred
and twenty answered rows for a full package on a real frame, and expect to reach that number over
more than one round.

The mechanism is measurable. Across one project's three rounds the trade tab grew from two hundred
and eighty-three populated lines to two hundred and ninety-two to three hundred and twenty-two, the
bid spread fell from about ten percent to under seven to about one, and the tonnage spread fell from
about ten percent to about seven to about two. Each round's new rows are the previous round's
unanswered questions, line by line. The scope sheet is the instrument that closes this trade.

The controlled case is six foot fall protection. Same item, same bidder pool, two projects. On the
project whose sheet demanded it in a numbered row and chased it round over round, two of four bidders
left the row blank in the first round and all four answered yes by the second. On the project where
nobody chased it, three of four answered no or not in contract and no plug was computed. One bidder
excludes it by name on one project and includes it on the other. Six foot tie-off is not a market
convention in either direction; it is whatever the scope sheet makes it. Shop finish behaves the same
way, and one row settles it: a single row reading that all steel is to be unprimed ready to receive
spray fireproofing was answered yes by all four bidders, on a trade where every round with no such
row produced four different finish statements.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- The package boundary at the podium, stated as a level or a grid line and as a mode. Not one line
  but three: what is furnished and installed below the boundary, what is furnished only above it, and
  what is excluded entirely. Nine distinct positions on this question were recorded across three
  projects and not one of them is stated the same way twice.
- Steel attached to wood, as its own row separate from the elevation row, because attachment rather
  than elevation is the real variable. Include connections to and hardware for connections to wood,
  flitch plates, steel sitting between two wood members, and every wood-to-wood, wood-to-steel and
  wood-to-concrete bolt. The one leveling sheet seen to ask this, in two rows, got no
  mark from any of six bidders.
- Beam and beam-web penetrations, with three attributes on the row: the count, the reinforcement type,
  and whether the work is shop or field. Those three together move the each-price by close to ten
  times inside one bidder's own rate card.
- Framed openings, roof opening frames and slab openings, separately from penetrations, and with the
  note that where none are shown none have been carried.
- Deck, by location and gauge, and separately deck on light gauge framing or on timber, which is a
  different custody answer.
- Pour stop and slab edge, by detail, because there are at least four readings of one perimeter
  condition: all slab edge as pour stop with no bend plate carried, light gauge pour stop everywhere
  with shoring at the perimeter bent plate excluded, light gauge at the exterior perimeter with steel
  bent plate around the stairwell and elevator shaft, and light gauge assumed unless a priced line
  says bent plate. None of the four is presented as a question; three are stated as assumptions in
  qualification blocks where a leveling read looks last.
- Elevator metals, split finer than one line: divider beams, hoist beams, safety beams, sill angles,
  pit ladders, sump pit frames and grates, guide rail supports. Divider and hoist beams may split by
  wall type, furnished and installed at cast-in-place shear walls inside the podium and furnished only
  at masonry shear walls above it. On one set the same scope drew four answers: furnish and install,
  furnish only, exclude installation, and silence.
- Anchor bolts, leveling plates, bearing plates and embeds, as a furnish row and a separate setting
  row, because they are furnished here by every bidder seen and set by none.
- Grout and dry pack under columns and bearing plates, its own row.
- Relieving angles, shelf angles, masonry ties, seismic clips and loose lintels, each its own row with
  its framing type stated, because the routing follows the framing type. Expect two bidders in one
  pool to answer the loose lintel row oppositely.
- Moment connections, and web doubler plates, and the increased member sizes that follow from the
  bidder's own connection design. One proposal excludes the doublers and the upsizing both, which
  means the consequence of its own engineering is unpriced.
- Connection engineering and the professional stamp, as two rows rather than one. One bidder includes
  the engineering in its scope list and excludes the stamp for the same connections in the same
  document.
- Shop finish, per work group rather than per package, with the surface preparation grade named. See
  section 5.
- Safety cable, split four ways: install at floors, maintain, remove, and roof perimeter. Every bidder
  installs and maintains while on the floor; every bidder excludes removal and later maintenance; and
  roof perimeter cable is excluded by every bidder seen while floor cable is included by every bidder
  seen.
- Six foot tie-off, its own numbered row, chased.
- The crane, with type, duration, hourly rate and required pad size, and whether it is inside the base
  or an alternate, and who provides the foundation.
- Mobilization count.
- Fire watch and police details, its own row, because the market splits on it.
- Certification requirements on the fabricator, the erector and the detailer, each as its own row.
- Escalation, tariff and validity as three separate commercial rows, each answered as a mechanism and
  not as a yes.
- Sales tax, as its own row. A pool in which one bidder assumes no sales tax cannot be ranked on
  stated totals, and the workbooks that get this right carry tax as a scope row answered by everyone.
- On renovation, one row each for exposing existing encased steel, removing existing coatings and
  slag, grinding and preparing existing surfaces for new attachment, lead testing and abatement,
  removing and reapplying existing fireproofing, reinstating concrete encasement, and scanning
  existing concrete before drilling. On renovation these are unanimous exclusions and they must
  happen before the steel bidder can start.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual members within one framing condition. Columns, beams, bracing and their connections at
  one level collapse to one line. Even the deepest takeoff seen prices them as counted rows
  inside one work group, not as separate scope decisions.
- Bolts, plates, stiffeners, gussets, clips and outriggers. They are attributes of the connection
  design, not scope lines, except where an erector is naming what the fabricator must supply free.
- Grades and section sizes. They are the engineer's, and where the drawings do not give them the
  right response is a question upstream rather than a scope row.
- Levels within one framing type. A level range collapses to one line unless the package boundary or
  the framing type actually changes, which is precisely where it must not collapse.
- Detailing and shop drawings. One line, not one per submittal.
- Deck accessories such as closure, pour stop trim and side lap treatment collapse into the deck line,
  with the exception of the side lap method itself where the specification is silent, which is worth
  a row of its own.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In this trade
the test fires hardest on custody rather than on existence. Almost nobody disagrees that a beam
exists; they disagree about who installs it, what finish it ships with, and whether it is above the
line.

So apply the test with a second question attached: for every item, does the document answer furnish
or install or both, and does it answer the shop finish. A scope row that cannot carry both custody and
finish loses half of what this trade actually decides. The market's own three-part code proves the
point by carrying both in four characters.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. A bidder that says nothing about steel above the
podium has not carried it, and a bidder that says nothing about beam penetrations has not counted
them. Where an item is one that some bidders carry and others exclude, silence is a gap to surface,
not an inclusion to assume.

### The structural gap list

Scope that no bidder carries, and that has to be added to every number before comparing. This is the
plug list, and a scope line set that names these explicitly is doing the estimator's plugging in
advance.

- Beam and beam-web penetrations and openings for other trades. The largest gap in the trade, on
  every project family seen. Treated at least six ways: excluded outright with no reason;
  excluded with the reason stated, meaning none shown or none denoted or no location or missing
  information; excluded from the base with a rate card by type and a stated minimum quantity;
  excluded with a ranged rate because the driving dimensions are fixed nowhere; included with a
  count by type; and routed by name to miscellaneous metals. See below for how it closes.
- Installation of furnish-only items. Egress beams, elevator divider and hoist beams, steel in
  wood framing, anchor bolts, leveling plates, embeds, balcony cap plates and columns, exterior
  lintels. Furnished by one package and installed in neither. On one project four items arrive from
  the miscellaneous metals side furnish only with bolts by others, having been excluded by every
  structural bidder, so the routing worked and the installation still landed nowhere.
- Setting and grouting of anchor bolts, leveling plates, leveling nuts and bearing plates.
  Furnished by steel in every proposal seen and set by steel in none. Excluded by every bidder seen,
  fabricator and erector alike. Two bidders make correct setting a gate on starting erection with a
  stated tolerance, so it is a schedule risk as well as a cost.
- Anchor bolt, as-built and plumbness surveys. Excluded universally, routed to concrete
  sometimes, priced once as an alternate. On one sheet the same item is routed away, deducted from
  two bids and priced as an add, all at once.
- Field touch-up of shop primer, and all finish painting. Excluded by every fabricator seen, and
  the painting trade records the same exclusion from its own side.
- Fireproofing, and its removal and replacement around erection interferences. Excluded by every
  bidder seen. On renovation, removal and reapplication of existing fireproofing is a separate and
  equally universal exclusion.
- Cutting, coring and protection of deck openings, including roof deck openings. Excluded by
  fabricators, by erectors and by miscellaneous metals alike, and refused by one bidder outright on
  stated safety grounds, which is a refusal rather than a price.
- Safety cable maintenance and removal, and roof perimeter safety cable. Installed by everyone,
  maintained and removed by nobody.
- Six foot fall protection. Universally excluded unless a numbered scope row demands it and is
  chased, and never priced by anyone when excluded. Where one bidder did price it as an add it was
  worth about two and a half percent of its base, which is a large number to leave off a sheet.
- Uniformed fire watch and police details. A split market and therefore a required row. Five
  treatments seen: excluded outright; excluded but with the erector's own crew doing welding fire
  watch and expressly not a dedicated watcher; included silently in the base; included as a named
  line inside the base worth about a thirteenth of it; and offered as an allowance outside the base
  worth about a sixteenth of it, billed at cost plus a percentage. The same bidder includes it on
  one job and excludes it on two others. On urban and occupied sites this is a first-order plug.
- Thermal breaks. Excluded by name and carried by nobody. On one sheet the thermal break row
  carries a question mark in every column and the same item appears again on the same sheet as
  thermal breaks at canopies.
- Web doubler plates and the member upsizing that follows the bidder's own connection design.
- Connection engineering and the professional stamp, where not affirmatively included. Priced
  separately once at under two percent of a base.
- Certification of the fabricator, the erector and the detailer. Excluded by name by several
  bidders, asserted affirmatively by others, answered by nobody on the sheets that ask. It
  eliminates or selects bidders rather than repricing them, and it is invisible in every total. On
  one project the addendum requiring a certified fabrication shop is transcribed onto the sheet, no
  column answers it, and the one bidder in the folder that excludes the certification is also the
  one bidder never ranked.
- Local hire and workforce compliance goals. Excluded or answered best effort.
- Final plumbing of free-standing columns that support wood. Excluded by name in one proposal,
  unmentioned by everyone else, and physically necessary.
- Winter productivity, where the schedule crosses the December to March window.
- Sales tax, wherever any bidder in the pool assumes it away.
- The erector's list of fabricator obligations, wherever the package is split. See section 1.
- On renovation only: verification of existing conditions and field measurement, shoring and
  protection of the existing structure, lead testing and abatement, and preparation of existing
  steel for new attachment.

### How the beam penetration gap closes

The gap exists because the quantity lives in sheet notes rather than in a schedule and not as a
symbol, and because where penetrations are drawn they are drawn without classification. One bidder
said so in one sentence: the ones shown on the plans are not included, because it does not know which
type they are. That bidder then supplied a rate card by detail type spanning close to ten times from
an unreinforced hole to the heaviest reinforced type, all shop made under a stated maximum reinforcing
plate thickness. The type is what prices it, and the drawings do not give the type. Field work runs
roughly two to nearly three times shop work for the same type.

The market can count them when asked. Two independent bidders on one set arrived at the same count of
seven and a third said six. Two bidders on another set independently counted the same twelve
unreinforced penetrations from the same drawing note and carried them. One bidder produced sixty
openings across three types two days after issuing a round with none, against an unchanged set: the
count came from the question, not the drawings, and a newer set later halved it. The rule underneath
is that where the structural drawings show and count the penetrations, bidders agree exactly, and
where they do not, nobody carries them.

The closing sequence, documented across one project's three rounds:

1. Early round: the estimator invented both a count and a rate and plugged it flat against every
   column, splitting shop from field. No bidder was asked.
2. Middle round: one bidder supplied a per-type rate card in an email. The estimator then counted off
   the drawings by detail type, thirty-eight of one type and a hundred and twenty of another, and
   plugged at that bidder's rates.
3. Late round: two bidders priced the counted quantities directly, at per-each rates within about a
   quarter of each other and of the original card.

So: obtain one rate card by detail type, then count. The count is cheap once the types are named, and
it is the estimator's to make. The failure modes to avoid are both real. One is carrying the whole
question as a tonnage of miscellaneous supports and penetrations at a premium per-ton rate, a unit no
bidder uses for this item, and never asking anyone. The other is the sheet that has a row headed
number of beam penetrations, a per-penetration rate, an empty quantity cell and a question mark
against every one of six bidders, and then folds the item into a floor framing lump sum. The estimator
knows the item, knows its each-cost, has no count, gets no count, and buries it.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

The house condition blocks travel verbatim between rounds, between projects and sometimes between
competitors, and they differentiate nothing. A pandemic clause is still printed word for word in
unrelated recent proposals. One standing exclusion sheet excludes precast plank grouting on two
projects that have no precast plank. One bidder's alphabetized exclusion sheet is attached unchanged
to its new-construction and its renovation bids. Leveling off the printed exclusion lists yields
nothing.

What is project signal, because it was written for this set:

- An exclusion carrying a sheet or detail number. The mature proposals do this row by row, with a
  counterparty in a comment column reading by miscellaneous metals, by others, by the concrete
  subcontractor, or the engineer of record to provide loads. The rows that say by others are the ones
  nobody ever picks up.
- An exclusion carrying its reason. This is the trade's strongest signal and it is dense. Reasons
  stated on the page include none shown on plans, none denoted on the framing plans, not indicated on
  plans, no location, missing information, no details provided, no sizes provided, pending
  calculations, offset distance cannot be determined, locations not found, not shown on the column
  schedule, sheet does not exist, detail does not exist, no specification reviewed, and there is no
  steel there. The exclusion list with reasons is the project's request-for-information list,
  pre-written by the market and already cited to sheets. Route it upstream rather than absorbing it.
  Two of these converted cleanly into money on an estimator's own sheet: a no-detail answer became a
  plug at a stated rate per square foot, and a no-detail alternate became a uniform plug added to
  every column.
- An exclusion list that grows monotonically round over round, from ten rows to thirteen to fifteen
  on one form. What grows is not boilerplate. Exclusion lists accumulate the arguments the bidder has
  lost.
- A qualification naming a member size the bidder invented. On one set two bidders assumed different
  tube sizes for every column, and across two hundred columns that alone moves the tonnage by more
  than a quarter while being invisible in any total.
- A stated tariff, escalation or validity instrument that differs from the pool's.
- Any exclusion naming a condition unique to the project: a penthouse, a second building, an existing
  structure to remain, a named machine room, a canopy.

Two hazards inside this section. First, self-contradicting proposals occur, and the priced breakout
wins. One document says raw in the priced scope line, primed in the printed qualifications, and
excluded in the printed exclusions, and only one of the three is priced. Another carries two
contradictory penetration lines on one page, one absolute and one conditional. Second, the
disappearing disclaimer: an exclusion vanishes from the next round's page at an unchanged price,
which means it left the paper and stayed in the number. Diff exclusion lists round over round, in
both directions, and diff the scope sentences too. A two-word change from beams to beams and columns
more than doubled a number, and neither a total-diff nor an exclusion-diff sees it.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number. Violating them converts the lump sum into a change
order, so treat each as a constraint the scope must honor or price around.

- Erection is gated on foundations complete, anchor bolts set within a stated tolerance and surveyed,
  and masonry walls complete and cured. One fabricator demands the survey results be forwarded so
  fabrication can be adjusted. These are gates, not preferences.
- Relieving angles are installed off the mason's staging. Three bidders across two rounds and both
  halves of the package state the same condition, and adjustment from the floor is a charged extra.
  Nobody prices doing it any other way. This is a hard cross-package sequencing dependency that
  appears in no schedule.
- The price assumes a stated mobilization count, usually one, and additional mobilizations are extra.
- Delivery is in erection sequence to the hook, and material stored because the site is not ready is
  charged monthly per trailer, sometimes with a property rental on top and a cancellation ladder
  rising to a large fraction of the contract fee by week band.
- A material deposit, or raw material billed in the first requisition, whether or not any steel has
  been erected. Nearly every bidder.
- Escalation, in at least five distinct instruments: a firm-price period with rolling absorption
  after which mill increases are added; a threshold escalation triggered by a material increase
  exceeding a stated percentage between proposal date and release to purchase; an open-ended
  execution escalation against mill documentation; a demand that the subcontract itself contain an
  escalation clause; and a procurement-time review with mill increases communicated before signing.
  Two bidders go further and ask the owner to buy the steel, which is a fabricator proposing to hand
  the commodity risk back.
- Tariff, in at least four incompatible instruments: quantified and excluded as the bidder's own
  separate figure; included at bid and floating at execution; included with a one-way ratchet, where
  the price includes all tariffs known at submission, any later tariff is excluded, and no adjustment
  is made if a tariff is reduced or removed; and reserved twice over in one document, quantified
  separately and also excluded as a change order for any new duty. Domestic bidders say nothing about
  tariff at all, which is the leveling hazard: adding the one disclosed figure to the one column that
  disclosed it and leaving the rest alone is not normalizing. Ask every bidder for a tariff basis, and
  ask specifically whether a later reduction is credited.
- A validity window, running from five days to thirty, with seven the most common. Two documents print
  two contradictory windows inside themselves, one saying ten days in the clarifications and thirty in
  the terms, another saying fifteen in a boxed note and ten above the signature block, with no
  statement of which governs. Several pair the window with a statement that the price is not
  guaranteed even inside it until award, and one is automatically void after thirty days. Read the
  whole document for the validity clause; do not stop at the first one. A structural steel number more
  than about a week old is not a price.
- A mill substitution clause: steel that cannot be purchased because of mill sizing or roll schedules
  will be substituted with a comparable size. The material delivered may not be the material
  specified, because the mill is not rolling it.
- Seasonal uplift. A flat thirty percent on the installation price for work falling between December
  and the end of March, from one bidder; a ten to twenty-five percent band for the same window from
  another; and a one-month grace period on schedule postponement after which an increase or an
  extension may be claimed, naming the move from summer to winter as the reason. One bidder also
  conditions delivery on a non-thaw period, so spring load restrictions sit outside the price.
- Schedule gates that are not weather. A bidder releases itself from the agreed schedule on less than
  fifteen working days' notice of a start-date change. Another requires the subcontract within ten
  business days of a letter of intent or the agreement may be cancelled. A third makes price and
  schedule subject to shop availability at the time of award.
- Award conditions that invalidate per-package ranking. Prices valid only if the whole scope is
  awarded together; prices conditional on winning both buildings; a requested per-building breakout
  labelled for accounting only; the words when awarded with steel printed beside a miscellaneous
  metals sum on every round. Four such statements appeared across three projects, and every leveling
  sheet ranked those numbers on separate tabs anyway, once selecting a different winner on each tab.
  This is a structural conflict between how the market prices and how the estimator buys, and it is
  not rare. It also collides directly with composite award: a ranked column assembled row by row from
  several bidders' rows plus plugs requires each row to be independently awardable, which is exactly
  what these bidders refuse.
- Terms asserted as governing. Two bidders state that their proposal's terms govern over any contract
  agreement, and one asks the general contractor to countersign the proposal as an exhibit to the
  subcontract. One conditions its price on its own credit department approving the owner's financing.
- Commercial terms most sheets have no column for: retainage caps from a twentieth to a tenth with
  different release triggers, interest on arrears, claims barred a stated number of days after the
  work ends, liability capped at the contract value, an online billing platform included by one bidder
  and its fees excluded by another, and a waiver of subrogation priced as an add.

### Shop finish, which is the sharpest unpriced difference in the trade

Every bidder calls its own shop finish standard, and no two of them mean the same thing. The regimes
seen, all on comparable buildings:

- Unprimed, all steel, no surface preparation and no touch-up of any kind.
- No paint on steel, shop primer explicitly excluded, galvanizing only where the drawings note it.
- Bare, on the bidder's own assumption that all the steel is fireproofed.
- Primer everywhere over a named preparation standard, including steel another trade will fireproof.
- Primer everywhere except steel scheduled for spray fireproofing, which ships bare but is still
  prepared to the same named standard. That is finer than bare: the fireproofer receives a prepared
  bare substrate.
- Unprimed except architecturally exposed steel, which receives a primer chosen to be compatible with
  the intumescent coating a different trade applies on site.
- One coat of a named proprietary primer at a stated dry film thickness over a named cleaning
  standard, disclaimed as a temporary coating that protects the steel only briefly.
- Hot-dip galvanized, color galvanized, powder coated, blackened, black oxide, galvannealed, and
  stainless at a named satin number, appearing as per-line finish values in the enumerated shapes.
- No shop finish stated at all, only that touch-up is excluded.

Four things follow, and they are the useful part.

The finish follows the fireproofing product, not the shop. One product wants a bare substrate and
another wants a primed one, and the decision is made in another trade's shop months before anyone
paints anything. One bidder ships bare on two jobs and primed on two others, and the variable is the
fireproofing scope. Where a project has both spray fireproofing and intumescent coating, both regimes
appear in one proposal.

It is a per-project read, not a house standard. Two bidders wrote materially different finish
positions on two projects, and one company answered all structural steel unprimed on one project and
all steel primed to a named preparation grade on another, using the same standing form. A bidder's
finish statement can be word for word identical across two rounds eight months apart on one building
and different on the other. The bidder does not carry a standard; it reads whatever the documents say and, where
they say nothing, invents. Never carry a bidder's finish forward from a previous job.

Finish varies by work group inside one proposal. One bidder primes the frame, galvanizes the
areaway and the overhead-door frames, color-galvanizes the exterior rails, primes the balcony rails
with finish paint by others, and supplies the bollards primed. Six work groups, four finishes, one
number. Another states the structural half bare and the miscellaneous half primed as two house rules
in the same paragraph.

A sub-supplier can set it. One bidder states that joists are shop primed with one coat of the
joist supplier's standard primer, which is a finish nobody in the contract chain specified.

The finish question is cheap to settle and expensive to leave open. One bidder priced it directly,
offering a deduct for primer at roughly a hundredth of its base, which is the only number this trade
puts on the primer decision anywhere and explains why most bidders answer it in a footnote rather than
a line. One scope row asking for the finish gets four consistent answers. No row gets four different
ones.

Deck finish is separate and much more stable: a galvanized substrate is near-universal, and one bidder
discloses that decking may arrive galvanized without paint on availability grounds.

### Adjacent-trade overlaps worth naming

- Concrete. The largest single seam by volume of unpriced work: setting and grouting of everything the
  steel package furnishes, plus the anchor bolt survey, plus the tolerance that governs whether
  erection can start.
- Masonry. Relieving angles installed off the mason's staging, masonry ties and seismic clips routed
  variously, and masonry anchors delivered to the fabricator's shop. On one project the whole masonry
  lateral support family is excluded by structural steel, by both erectors and by miscellaneous
  metals alike.
- Carpentry and light gauge framing. Everything at and above the wood transfer level, and every
  connection to it. On one project the frame is bought predominantly on a tab whose own title joins
  light gauge framing and structural steel, and the structural steel number is only meaningful once
  the light gauge package's scope is fixed.
- Fireproofing. Sets the shop finish, and its removal and replacement at interferences is excluded by
  everyone.
- Painting. Field touch-up and finish paint, excluded by every fabricator and recorded as an exclusion
  from the painting side too.
- Elevator supplier. Divider, hoist and safety beams, sill angles, guide rail supports and pit metals,
  routed at least four ways on one set.
- Miscellaneous metals. The whole of section 1.
- Site and logistics. The crane, its foundation, its position, and whether moving it outside the
  footprint is priced. One erector states that the tower crane shown in the estimator's own site
  logistics rendering will not do the picks, prices a different machine class instead, and assigns
  the crane foundation to the general contractor.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What steel bidders actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The structural drawings, which every bidder treats as governing. Framing plans, column schedules,
   the details, and the sheet notes. Sheet notes matter more here than in most trades because the
   beam penetration quantity lives in them.
2. The addenda, cited individually. Basis divergence is routine: on one project six bidders
   acknowledged six different document bases, from none to five addenda, and nothing in the leveling
   normalized that.
3. The architectural drawings, as a supplement only, and several bidders disclaim responsibility for
   discrepancies later found in them. Steel drawn on architectural sheets and not on structural ones
   is excluded by name by more than one bidder.
4. The specification, where one exists, mostly for the finish, the certification requirements and the
   connection design delegation. It may not exist at all.
5. Their own assumptions, disclosed or silent. This is the category to watch.

The drawings will not tell you, and each of these moves the number:

- Whether miscellaneous metals is inside the proposal. See section 1.
- Where the package boundary falls, and whether the steel above it is furnished, installed or
  excluded.
- The beam penetration count and type.
- The shop finish, and the surface preparation grade under it.
- The labor basis, and whether prevailing wage applies.
- The mobilization count.
- Whether the crane is inside the package, whose it is, and where it stands.
- The tariff basis, and whether a later reduction is credited.
- The escalation mechanism and the validity window.
- Whether sales tax is inside the number.
- Whether the schedule pushes erection into winter.
- Whether a certification is required, and whether the bidder holds it.
- The deck gauge and depth where the drawings do not fix them. One bidder assumed three inches on
  span grounds against four competitors' two-inch composite deck, disclosed as an assumption and
  never called a deviation. An identical deck-gauge substitution was priced by different bidders
  across a fifty percent band.
- Shop drawing duration, which two bidders on one job stated as two to six weeks and as fifteen.
- On renovation: the weldability of the existing steel, which one bidder carries as carbon steel
  subject to weldability; the presence of lead and toxic substances; whether the site was ever
  visited, which two documents state it was not; and what temporary power the general contractor must
  supply on which floors.

### The bidder's invented fill

Where the documents are silent, bidders invent, and the disclosure is uneven. On one set two bidders
read the same unlabelled tube posts as two different sections, one two sizes heavier than the other,
each disclosing its own assumption on the face of its bid and neither asking. Elsewhere: an assumed
hoist beam size, an assumed plate gauge, hold downs taken off as the alternate detail, a machine room
roof framed against the bidder's own assumed loading in pounds per square foot rather than a drawn
frame, and an allowance of thirty or forty-two penetrations where the drawings show none.

The sharpest version of this is a defect report that reads as a qualification. One bidder writes a
numbered qualification headed that the drawings are not completed, naming four defects: edge of slab
not provided, bottom of base plate elevation not provided, brace framed elevations without the
bracing type, and no section details called out on plan views. Two competitors on the identical
drawings filled the same four gaps silently, as their own assumptions. One bidder's silent assumption
is another's documents-defect report, on the same drawings, in the same round. That same four-defect
list then appears word for word in the same bidder's next round eight months later, submitted to a
different general contractor: the gaps were declared in writing twice, across a set reissue and a
change of general contractor, and were still open.

And the hardest single instance: one fabricator states that no dimension was available on the drawings
and every framing plan was scaled at one eighth. A fabricator taking off a building by scaling belongs
upstream as a question, and instead it sits in a qualification list where nothing will route it.

### Documents in conflict, and readings that cannot both be right

Conflicts the market records for you: two competing details for deck reinforcing at columns, where one
bidder priced one and excluded the other and said so across two rounds; deck shown on architectural
drawings differing from deck on structural drawings; eight detail-versus-detail conflicts named in one
renovation proposal with the side it priced stated for each, including a schedule calling for two
different grating depths in one place; an addendum deleting a detail reference while a later addendum
reinstated the requirement through a drawing note; the same machine room floor drawn more than once
and priced twice by the bidder who noticed; and a member priced at six inches where the drawing says
six feet, with the assumption left in the bid.

Readings that cannot both be right, which is the highest-value class:

- Whether the drawings show beam penetrations at all. One bidder: none shown. Two bidders: an
  allowance, one of them counted. Same set, same week.
- What a boxed-out or crossed opening on a plan view means. One erector refuses to price them on the
  ground that no specific framing is shown, and excludes the typical floor opening detail outright.
- Whether a drawn section and detail along one grid line depicts anything. One bidder excludes it
  stating there is no steel there.
- Whether moment connections are in the base. One bidder assumes welded moment connections are
  included; a competitor on the identical drawings excludes them by detail number. The two land within
  a third of a percent of each other at the total.
- How many locations of a detail exist. One bidder finds two and refuses to unit price the rest
  without a count; a competitor carries three and excludes any other use.
- Deck side lap method, excluded as welded by one bidder, cited to a standard by another, and unstated
  by four.

All three classes route upstream as questions. None of them is a leveling problem to absorb.

### What the estimator's own documents will get wrong

These are systematic rather than occasional.

- The ranking denominator often matches no bidder, and it changes between rounds and between
  projects. One estimating team normalized this trade five ways inside one year: on tons, on gross
  square feet, on gross square feet under the name of a proprietary system, and on dwelling units,
  which has no physical relationship to a steel frame. Where the denominator changes between two
  rounds of one project, the two rankings cannot be compared without restatement, and the change is
  invisible unless the header is read. Inside one workbook structural steel may normalize on tons
  and metal fabrications on square feet, so the two halves of one metals package cannot be compared
  to each other on either sheet's basis.
- Scope rows and plug quantities get lifted from the selected bidder's own proposal. On one sheet
  every element row is transcribed from one bidder's enumerated takeoff, only that bidder's column
  answers them, and that bidder is the selected sub, so its takeoff silently became the project's
  scope definition.
- Priced offers go unranked, and it is consequential. Two complete proposals in one folder that
  reach no column and would have placed third and fourth of seven. A union erection-only bid that
  never becomes a column, on a tab whose basis is cost per ton, which is exactly the basis on which
  it would have been informative. On one project the selected column's structural document is not in
  the structural steel bid folder at all. Check a wide pool for unranked offers deliberately.
- Columns get synthesized and mis-entered. A ranked column assembled from one company's structural
  number and another's miscellaneous number, ranked as a bidder that does not exist. A tonnage
  transcribed into a competitor's column, with every per-ton figure computed from it wrong. A
  tonnage recorded for a bidder whose proposal states none. A column frozen on a superseded round.
- Adjustments get applied inconsistently, which manufactures a tight spread out of a wide one. And
  alternate and unit pricing blocks are printed and left empty round after round, on projects where
  bidders offered priced alternates and rate cards. The instrument exists and is not used.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Tons, for the fabricated frame. The primary unit for fabricate-and-erect bids and the estimator's
  own budget, and stated by roughly half of bidders.
- Pounds per square foot, as the density assumption that derives a tonnage where nobody has taken one
  off. Seen from about eight to about fourteen depending on the structural system, with one
  estimator's standing model running ten to thirteen depending on condition, and another writing
  fifteen at basement and parking, thirteen and twelve elsewhere.
- Squares or square feet, for deck, by gauge and location. Note that a per-square-foot rate in this
  trade may divide by the deck area rather than by gross floor area, and only the arithmetic reveals
  which. Bidder and estimator can mean two different square feet on one project.
- Each, for the counted things: columns, beams, braces, kickers, hangers, moment connections, column
  splices, divider and hoist beams, penetrations by type, bollards, seismic clips.
- Linear feet, for perimeter angle, deck support angle, relieving angles, safety cable and rails.
- Pieces, picks and crane days, which is the erector's unit of production and does not convert to
  tons.
- Hours, for shop, detailing, engineering and crane rates, and for the estimator's own install plugs.
- Days, for erection duration, hoisting, grouting and scanning, particularly on renovation.
- Months, for stored material on trailers and for a tower crane's duration.
- Percent of contract value, for bonds, for a union erection add, and for connection engineering
  where it is priced.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Tonnage, and therefore the member-size assumptions that produce it where the drawings do not size
   the members. This is the largest single driver and it is invisible in any total.
2. Scope definition: where the package boundary falls, and whether miscellaneous metals is in. On a
   combined package the divergence lives in the miscellaneous half, not in the frame. On one project
   the structural halves span about one and three quarters to one while the miscellaneous halves on
   the same project span nearly seventeen to one, and one bidder's miscellaneous half is nearly half
   its total bid while two competitors put theirs at about a sixth and about a third.
3. Steel price and the commercial instruments around it: escalation, tariff and validity. One
   fabricator's stated per-ton band moved with tariffs across three documents and thirteen months, from
   a normal band, to about a fifth above its top with the tariff named as the reason in writing, to a
   stated ceiling a quarter above that, at which point the shop declined to bid. And the sharpest
   isolation available: one proposal re-issued eight months later at close to sixty percent more per
   ton with every scope word identical.
4. Labour basis, worth about a twentieth of a base as a priced union erection add, and worth nothing
   at all as a predictor of rank.
5. The package mode. An erection-only number is roughly a quarter to three tenths of a
   fabricate-and-erect one, so mixing the two in a ranking produces a spread that is not a spread.
6. Erection access and the crane. Moving the crane outside the footprint was priced by four bidders
   across nearly three to one, and the machine class chosen by the plank width moved a crane package
   by about a quarter.
7. Connection type and complexity, including moment connections, and the web doublers and member
   upsizing that follow from the bidder's own connection design.
8. Shop finish, worth about a hundredth of a base as a priced primer deduct, and worth far more than
   that as an unpriced difference between bidders who mean different things by standard.
9. Beam penetrations, at close to ten times between the cheapest and dearest type inside one rate
   card, at roughly two to three times between field and shop for the same type, and at up to about
   three and a third times between two bidders on the same cell of the same job. On the estimator side
   the whole family carries a standing plug rate about a quarter above the base framing rate per ton.
10. Season. Winter is a priced event at ten to twenty-five percent, and at thirty percent, of the
    installation price.
11. Mobilization count, which varies two to one between competitors on one job and is fixed by nothing.
12. Fire watch and police details, worth about a thirteenth of a base as a named line inside one, and
    about a sixteenth as an allowance outside another.
13. Six foot tie-off, worth about two and a half percent of a base where anyone priced it.
14. Bonds, priced twice anywhere, at one percent and at one and a half percent.

### What the spread does not prove

This trade produces the tightest spreads in the division and the most misleading ones, and the two
look identical from a distance.

Once a pool is filtered to documents offering the same object, structural steel bids on one set
converge to somewhere between one and an eighth and one and three fifths to one. Everything wider is
a scope-definition problem wearing a price ranking's clothes. Four bidders landed within one percent
of each other on a mature set against a hundred-and-twenty-one-row issued scope sheet, at a tonnage
spread of two percent, while the miscellaneous metals tab in the same workbook on the same round spans
more than five to one.

But a tight spread is not evidence of scope agreement, and this is the trap. At design development on
that same project four bidders landed within ten percent while one excluded moment connections by
detail, one excluded any galvanizing at all, one stated no shop finish, two excluded all steel above
the podium by sheet range, and a third carried egress beams as furnish only. Ten percent apart and
four incompatible scope definitions. On another project four fabricators land within a fifth of each
other on scopes whose wood-level cut lines are at three different levels and whose treatment of the
wood-level steel differs in all four documents. The tightest pools seen are the ones where the sheet asked the fewest questions.

Counts and prices are independent axes, demonstrated in both directions on the same trade. Three
independent bidders enumerated one frame and landed within about one and a half percent of each other
on columns, within one percent on beams and exactly equal on braces, with shear stud counts within
half a percent and deck area within five percent across four bidders, while their prices on those same
counts spanned more than one and a half to one. And the reverse: two bidders' stated tonnages on
identical drawings differed by nearly two to one while their prices landed within a fraction of a
percent of each other, so a per-ton rate lifted from the lower-tonnage document is roughly double the
market's. Elsewhere, two bidders on an early set agreed within three percent per ton at a seventeen
percent tonnage spread, and two bidders who both clearly carry the same roof deck stated quantities
twenty-one percent apart for it.

Tonnage converges as the set matures, and it converges from below, because early-round members are not
sized and every bidder assumes light. Across one project's rounds the tonnage level rose about a tenth
from the first round to the second and then settled, while the spread fell from about ten percent to
about seven to about two. That is a property of the pool against a maturing set, not a rule any single
bidder obeys: one bidder's own tonnage fell nearly a tenth between rounds while its price rose more
than forty percent.

A wide pool is not necessarily a deep one. Ranking rows count filings and company names, and this
trade supplies more of both than it supplies independent bidders: one operation can occupy several
rows under several names, one document logged under two trades can be counted twice, and one
company's two offices can each file legitimately. Count operations before treating a row count as
evidence that the package was competitively covered, and treat any bidder count as an upper bound.

A ratio above about three to one on a division 05 leveling row is not a price finding. Check first
whether an install-only or erection-only number, a non-offer, a narrow specialist, a stale prior-round
carry or a combined printed total is sitting in the column. Removing one such column has repeatedly
turned an apparent three-to-one or fifteen-to-one spread into a real one of a fifth or an eighth. An
order-of-magnitude row spread means several scopes wearing one name, and it belongs in scope
definition, never in a comparison.

Two further cautions on the estimator's own numbers. A tight adjusted spread can be manufactured by
adjustments that were not applied consistently: on one project the adjusted columns fall within about
twenty percent while the raw bids spanned more than two to one, because some excluded a scope another
carried. And the owner-side budget column is unreliable in both directions: on one project it is
roughly a quarter of two bidder columns that sit within two percent of each other, and on another it
is nearly three times two bidder columns that sit within four percent of each other. Where several
independent bidders agree with each other and disagree with the budget, the bidders are usually right.

### The total is the last thing to read

A total can hide everything, and one combined ladder shows how completely. Across three rounds the
package total moved by less than half a percent from the first round to the last, while its
structural half rose about a quarter and its miscellaneous half fell about a fifth and then again by
more than forty percent, because one metal pan stair became a wood stair with no steel risers or
channels and that scope left the package for carpentry. A reader watching the total saw nothing
happen. Everything happened.

Read, in this order: the priced breakout, the scope sentences, the inclusion list, the exclusion list,
the qualifications, and only then the total. Diff all five of them round over round. Totals move last
and least.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file rests on a hundred and eight proposal-level records, thirty-seven estimator leveling
records and one returned scope sheet, read from real bid folders and real bid comparison workbooks
across twenty-one projects. Twenty of those projects carry proposals; the twenty-first appears only
through its leveling sheet. Projects were bid between one and five times each, thirty-four distinct
pricing rounds in all, spanning schematic design, design development, permit progress, fifty and
eighty-five percent construction documents, hundred percent construction documents, final pricing
sets, conformed and re-issued sets, and several public and federal request-for-proposal rounds, over
roughly eight years. Around four hundred and fifty priced lines are captured alongside, in tons, each,
square feet, squares, linear feet, hours, days, months and lump sums.

The bidder pool repeats heavily. One fabricator appears on nine of the projects, another on eight,
several on four to six. That repetition is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal
distinction in section 4 and the bidder-kind finding in section 1 testable, and it is also the main
limit on how far the frequencies here generalize: where this file says a bidder does something, it is
sometimes the same bidder observed across several jobs rather than the market.

Those company counts are themselves upper bounds, for the reason section 2 gives. Two operations are
known to appear under more than one name here, accounting for at least five ranking identities
between them, and they were caught only because someone compared addresses. Nobody has checked the
rest, so the true number of distinct operations behind these records is lower than the number of
company names in them, by an unknown amount.

Building types covered: new-construction multifamily over a steel and concrete podium with wood
framing above, which is the majority and the calibration case; a mixed-use redevelopment containing a
music venue; a theatre restoration; an occupied office building converting to residential; two historic
building conversions; a public housing redevelopment; a center for women and children; a hotel; and a
federal historic renovation of a nineteenth-century industrial building. Steel tonnage across the
corpus spans roughly a hundredfold, from under three tons to more than seven hundred.

What this file cannot yet claim:

- It is one regional market, through one general contractor, with one estimating team's workbook
  templates. Every leveling observation is that team's practice. Nothing here is tested against
  another market or another buyer.
- The filing rule in section 2 is that one buyer's logging convention, confirmed against what the
  documents actually contain and not yet seen anywhere else. Where a different buyer files, confirm
  the convention holds before reading a second trade token as a package span, because a buyer who
  files by folder rather than by judgment will produce the same pattern for a different reason.
- There is almost no buyout evidence. No signed subcontract, no schedule of values and no change
  order history is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions survived negotiation, which
  plugs were right, or whether the beam-penetration counts and the furnish-only installation gaps
  actually cost what the plugs said. The leveling sheets name a selected column and nothing confirms
  the buy. Everything in sections 3 and 7 about plug sizes is a bid-side observation.
- There is no evidence on how a split fabrication-and-erection buy actually performs. The erection-only
  offers were either mis-ranked or never ranked, so the corpus does not show a general contractor
  executing the split.
- Architecturally exposed structural steel is excluded by every bidder who mentions it and priced by
  nobody. There is no evidence on what it is worth, including on a building type where it is almost
  certainly required.
- There is no priced evidence on delegated connection design as a range. It is included, excluded, or
  priced once as an alternate.
- Lead times are thin. One bidder gives a shop drawing and fabrication window; nobody gives a mill
  order to delivery chain; the only hard dated lead time belongs to a purchased proprietary product.
  Steel here is priced as a commodity risk and not as a schedule risk, which may be a property of this
  market rather than of the trade.
- Reinforcing steel is out of scope throughout, so the boundary between this package and reinforcing
  is untested.
- The proprietary framing systems in the corpus appear only as budget documents on conceptual
  drawings and as a material-only offer bounded to part of a building. None has been seen bid against
  a real set as an installed package, so no comparison between a system and a conventional frame is
  supported here.
- Shear studs are included by most bidders and quantified separately by almost nobody, so nothing here
  supports a stud quantity or rate.
- Union structural steel bidding is thinly covered. One project solicited both markets and received
  only open-shop bids. The single quantified labor-basis figure anywhere is one bidder's own union
  erection add on its own proposal.
- No general contractor in this corpus has issued a numbered scope sheet to bidders on a project with
  a real steel frame and had it answered by more than one pool. The one project where a full sheet was
  issued and answered is the evidence for the entire scope-sheet argument in section 3. It is strong
  evidence, because the movement is measured round over round on one project with a stable bidder
  pool, and it is one project.
- Two market priors are carried here as gap-fillers and are untested against this corpus: that the
  erection share holds outside this market, and that the certification gate eliminates bidders in
  practice rather than only on paper. Neither has been observed through to an award.

A note on reading the documents themselves, because it changes what is knowable. A significant
minority of these files have no text layer at all, and a further set carry checkbox matrices whose
column marks vanish under text extraction while every row label survives. A text-only read of those
proposals inverts answers by omission and looks perfectly clean while doing it, and on one group of
projects it would have lost four proposals entirely, including the only priced union delta and the
only priced primer deduct. Render anything whose extracted text looks structurally thin, and render anything whose
grain is a grid.
