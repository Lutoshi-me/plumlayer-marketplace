# Gypsum Underlayment

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the gypsum
underlayment package, the poured floor topping known in the market as gypcrete. Everything here is
learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file speaks in
calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves. Where real
proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real underlayment contractors can bid it and a real
estimator can level it.

One thing to hold from the start, because it governs everything downstream. This trade sells a
poured area, but it prices a number of visits. Two bids on the same building at the same assembly
can differ by half, and the difference will be the mobilization count, not the square footage or
the material. A scope line that names an area without naming a pour sequence is not yet biddable.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

CSI section 03 5400 is the anchor, with 03 5413 used interchangeably and 03 5412 appearing as the
specification section number on at least one project. It sits in Division 3, with the concrete, even
though the work happens deep into the interior finishes sequence and its real neighbors are drywall
and flooring. Expect to find it filed under the concrete division and coordinated against the finish
schedule.

The package as bid is the poured topping plus the acoustic mat under it. That pairing is essentially
never split. Every bidder seen prices the mat and the topping together, and the mat is usually named
by product and thickness in the same sentence as the pour. Treat them as one package with two
specification variables, not as two packages.

Who bids it is unusually varied for a trade this narrow, and the kind of company changes what the
number contains:

- Dedicated gypsum underlayment installers. The core of any bid list. Long condition sets, explicit
  mobilization counts, named products.
- Specialty and general concrete contractors. They bid it as one more pumped-and-placed operation
  and their documents look like concrete documents, sometimes literally titled as purchase orders
  rather than proposals.
- Flooring contractors. The same company that installs the finish floor also pours the underlayment
  on some bid lists, which puts both sides of the trade's largest seam inside one contract.
- Metal framing and drywall subcontractors. They carry it as a work group inside a much larger
  framing and gypsum board package on some projects and stand it alone on others.
- Specialty coatings applicators. Occasionally, and competitively.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits elsewhere:

- Perimeter isolation strip. Furnished and installed by nearly every bidder as part of the pour.
  Its removal is a different matter, covered in section 5.
- The sealer or primer material. Furnished by most bidders and installed by none. This is the
  trade's defining seam and it gets its own treatment in section 5.
- Sound mat, isolation foam and tape as one supplied bundle.
- Pour stops around stair, elevator and chase openings. Usually pushed to the general contractor,
  occasionally carried, and worth a row either way.
- Filling of minor substrate cracks and small penetrations before the pour. Some bidders include a
  foam fill of visible cracks; most make the general contractor do it. One bidder draws the line by
  size, including small penetrations and excluding larger ones.
- Submittals. Rarely named as a scope line, but one bidder lists them, which is a reminder that the
  acoustic and fire assemblies here are submittal-bearing.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like it belongs:

- Installation of the sealer, always.
- Trimming of the excess perimeter isolation strip, essentially always.
- Floor preparation before the finish flooring goes down, always.
- Crack isolation or decoupling membrane under tile, excluded by name where it is mentioned at all.
- Moisture testing and mitigation, excluded wherever it is named.
- Protection of the cured pour during the rest of construction, excluded wherever it is named.
- Underlayment at stairwells, stair landings, elevators, roof levels, cellars and concrete areas.
  Multiple bidders exclude these by name and the ones who do not are usually silent rather than
  inclusive.

## 2. How the market bids

The default output is one number, a short assembly description, and a long conditions block. The
conditions block is where the trade actually lives, and it is typically longer than the scope.

- Single lump sum with no internal breakout is the majority shape, on every project.
- Segmented into two to four numbers is common, and when it happens the segmentation is by scope
  family, by building, or by area type rather than by element.
- A minority price with a real unit rate: square footage times a dollar-per-square-foot rate with
  the extension shown. Where that happens, the same bidder holds the same format across every round
  and every project, which makes its price history readable as a rate rather than a lump. That is
  the single most useful proposal format in this trade and it is a minority habit.
- Unit rates can differ per building inside one proposal. A smaller building carries a higher rate
  per square foot than a larger one on the same job, because mobilization and setup cost does not
  scale with area. Expect the small-building premium and do not read it as a pricing error.

What a proposal typically will state:

- Pour thickness, as an average, and compressive strength in PSI.
- Sound mat product and thickness.
- The levels or areas covered, usually by number.
- A square footage. Nearly always approximate, and nearly always different from the next bidder's.
- A mobilization or pour count, and often a day count.
- Labor basis, union or open shop, stated by most and recorded by an estimator for all.
- A dense general-contractor-responsibility block covering heat, ventilation, water, staging,
  cleanliness and access.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Any quantity below the project or floor level. Room-by-room or unit-by-unit quantities are absent.
- A flatness tolerance to be achieved. What appears instead is a disclaimer that the intent is not
  a level floor, which is not the same thing.
- Anything about the acoustic performance the assembly is meant to deliver, unless a manufacturer
  data sheet happens to be attached.
- What the sealer is for, or when it must go down. Some bidders name a window of roughly twelve to
  twenty-four hours before the finish floor, which is a schedule constraint hidden in a materials
  note.
- Whether the mat can be deleted without failing code. Nobody says this, and the answer is usually no.

Revision habits are the richest part of this trade's documentary behavior, and the mechanisms are
distinct enough to name:

- The negotiation ladder. Same area, same mobilizations, same conditions, monotonically falling
  price across two or three set rounds. Falls of a fifth from first quote to last are ordinary. No
  reason is documented.
- The silent reprice. Scope bullets byte-identical, basis date advances, only the number moves.
- The specification ladder, and this is the dangerous one. The price falls and the assembly quietly
  degrades with it: a thinner pour, a lower PSI, a cheaper mat brand, a different underlayment
  product. None of it is flagged as a change. A drop of a sixth in price has been seen alongside a
  reduction of a quarter in poured material plus two product substitutions, presented as a revised
  proposal with an unchanged letterhead date. Diff the scope bullets word by word before reading any
  price movement as a saving.
- Scope entry as a price rise. The area or the level range changes because the bidder had read the
  wrong floors, and the number rises with it. A third more area and a third more money is not an
  escalation, it is a correction, and it means the earlier round should never have been ranked.
- Commercial basis restatement. The base falls and an add alternate restores it, because an
  owner-controlled insurance program was applied. No scope moved at all.
- Alternate-only revision. Base frozen, deduct alternates added. In this trade the alternates added
  are almost always sound mat downgrades.
- The mobilization concession. The price falls because the bidder has been allowed to assume fewer,
  larger pour deployments. This is the one revision mechanism that reflects a real operational change
  and it is the one most likely to win the job.

Two structural warnings about the documents themselves. First, a company may bid the same project
twice from two regional offices, days apart, at prices within a fraction of a percent, on materially
different assemblies. Ranking them as two bidders double-counts one company and compares two
different scopes. Second, a leveling sheet that lists one row per proposal will show the same company
several times as separate rounds; a bid list that looks twelve deep can be seven companies.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets in this trade have run around twenty to just over thirty rows. Target roughly twenty-five
to thirty-five lines for a full multifamily underlayment package. This is a narrow trade and a sheet
much past that is padding, but a sheet under about fifteen cannot distinguish bidders, because the
distinguishing facts are the assembly attributes and the visit count, and each of those needs its own
row.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Every assembly specification attribute, separately: underlayment product, underlayment thickness,
  compressive strength, sound mat product, sound mat thickness. Five rows, not one assembly row.
  Bidders vary independently on all five and each one carries a price.
- Mobilization or pour count, with the definition of a mobilization attached. A mobilization is
  usually defined as one section of floor prepped and poured in a single shift not exceeding eight
  hours, and bids on the same building have ranged from two to thirty-six of them.
- The pour sequencing assumption: how many pours per floor, whether floors can be poured
  concurrently, and whether both buildings release at once.
- Pre-pours at tubs, showers, MEP closets, chases and dead spaces, as its own line with a stated
  count and a per-occurrence basis. Never fold this into the base.
- Perimeter isolation strip installation and perimeter isolation strip removal, as two separate
  rows. They belong to different parties.
- Sealer furnish and sealer installation, as two separate rows. Same reason.
- Areas by level and by building, with the wood-framed and concrete portions of any mixed level
  split. On a transfer level that is part wood and part slab, only the wood takes underlayment, and
  which is which is the most-missed boundary in this trade.
- Excluded location types as explicit rows answered yes or no: stairwells and landings, elevator
  pits and lobbies, roof levels, cellars and basements, parking, retail, concrete areas, curtain
  wall infills. A sheet that lists a roof level and gets a no from every bidder has done its job.
- Floor flatness required, with the tolerance named, and leveling as a separate priced row. See
  section 7.
- Protection of the cured pour, its own row, naming who furnishes, installs and maintains the
  temporary planking.
- Moisture testing and mitigation, its own row.
- Post-pour floor preparation before the finish flooring, its own row, with the counterparty named.
- Building conditions as a row rather than a footnote: minimum ambient temperature, the hours before
  and after the pour it must hold, and whether the building will be enclosed by then.
- Acoustic and fire ratings required by the drawings, keyed to the sheet and detail that state them.
  One sheet does this and it is the right practice.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Units, corridors and common areas collapse into one line per assembly, not per room type, unless
  the assembly actually changes. It often does change at corridors, which is a different reason to
  split.
- Sound mat, isolation foam and tape collapse into the mat line.
- Individual apartment counts collapse into floor areas. Nothing in this trade is priced per unit.
- Mix ratio and reinforcement fibers are attributes of the underlayment line, not lines. They track
  the thickness and strength already enumerated.
- Levels collapse into a level range wherever the assembly and the substrate are the same.
- Pump, hose, mixing and washout collapse into the mobilization line. They are the mobilization.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be silent
about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. Applied here, the test
fires hardest on the sealer, the isolation strip removal, the pre-pours, the leveling and the
protection, which is exactly the set in the gap list below.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. Where some bidders furnish the sealer and others
exclude it by name, a proposal that says nothing about sealer is a gap to surface, not an inclusion
to assume. The same applies to a bidder who never names a PSI: an unnamed strength is not the
specified strength.

There is a fourth state to watch for beyond included, excluded and silent. A row can be enumerated
on the leveling sheet with a count and a price of zero. Pre-pours have been carried this way, eight
of them at zero, on scope every bidder excludes. It reads as included to anyone scanning totals.

### The structural gap list

These are the plugs. No bidder carries them, so the estimator adds them to every number before
comparing, and a scope line set that names them is doing the plugging in advance.

1. Installation of the sealer the underlayment sub furnished. Plugged as days at a daily rate.
2. Pre-pours at tubs, showers and MEP closets. Plugged as a count of mobilizations or occurrences.
   Bidder-quoted equivalents for the same work on the same sheet have ranged better than three to
   one, so the plug is a rough instrument.
3. Removal of the excess perimeter isolation strip and temporary forming. Plugged as hours at a
   labor rate, on every bidder without exception, because nobody carries it.
4. Post-pour floor preparation to get from what this trade delivers to what the flooring needs.
5. Protection of the cured pour, including temporary planking under wheeled and concentrated loads.
6. Moisture testing and mitigation.
7. Pour stops at stair, elevator and chase openings, where the bidder pushes them to the general
   contractor, which is most of the time.
8. Floor leveling to a finish-grade tolerance, where the specification demands one.

Those eight are the trade's structural gap list. The first three appear as explicit plug rows on real
leveling sheets. The rest live in the exclusion lists and get discovered later.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

### The recurring exclusions, with their counterparty

| Excluded by the underlayment contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Installation of the sealer or primer, material furnished (nearly every bidder) | Finish flooring contractor, usually within a day of the floor going down |
| Trimming or removal of excess perimeter isolation strip (excluded by every bidder seen) | Flooring contractor after the finish floor is installed, per manufacturer instruction, though most bidders name no one |
| Floor preparation before finish floor goods (nearly every bidder) | Flooring contractor, with light sanding and minor skim or patching named |
| Pre-pours at tubs, showers, mechanicals, chases and dead spaces (nearly every bidder) | Back to this trade as an extra, priced per occurrence or per mobilization |
| Moisture testing and mitigation (commonly) | Frequently unassigned, and a recurring real gap |
| Protection of the poured underlayment (commonly) | General contractor, with temporary planking furnished, installed and maintained by others |
| Repair of damage to the pour caused by other trades (commonly) | General contractor, or back-charged |
| Structural preparation, subfloor patching and repair, deflection (commonly) | General contractor and the framing trade, before arrival |
| Sealing of holes, voids and penetrations against leakage (commonly) | General contractor, and this one has physical consequences below |
| Firecaulking of the floor before the pour (some bidders, emphatically) | Firestopping contractor, and it must precede the pour |
| Crack isolation or decoupling membrane under tile (some bidders) | Tiling contractor, installed over the cured gypcrete |
| Underlayment at stairwells and stair landings (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned |
| Underlayment at slab or concrete areas, cellars, roofs, parking (some bidders) | Frequently unassigned, and on a mixed transfer level this is a real gap |
| Curtain wall infills, expansion joint stops, grout into core joints (a minority) | Frequently unassigned |
| Laser leveling and any stated flatness tolerance (a minority state it outright) | Nobody, unless bought as an alternate |
| Permits, street and sidewalk rental, police details, flag persons, traffic control (commonly) | General contractor |
| Union or prevailing wage installation (commonly, as an explicit negative) | Not applicable unless the project requires it, in which case the bid is void |
| Bond costs, and insurance beyond the bidder's existing program (commonly) | General contractor, priced back as an alternate |
| Pallet, bag and excess sand removal (about half; the other half accept pallet removal) | General contractor, via the dumpster the GC also provides |
| Sweeping or vacuuming before installation (a minority) | General contractor, and it is a precondition either way |
| Local hiring requirements (a minority) | General contractor |
| Sound design and acoustic engineering (rarely named) | Architect or acoustic consultant, and its absence from every other bid is not the same as coverage |

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of that table is boilerplate. A repeat bidder reproduces the same list on every project without
a word changed, so its presence carries no project information. Read boilerplate for the trade map,
not for the project.

There is a specific hazard here worth stating plainly. Exclusion lists in this trade contradict their
own priced scope. A bidder has listed sealer in its exclusions on the same page where its scope line
includes furnishing the sealer, and has done it in both directions across two projects. The priced
scope line governs; the boilerplate list is a pasted template. Never level off exclusion lists alone.

What is project signal:

- A named level, area or substrate that is excluded. When a bidder writes out that the concrete area
  of a specific level is excluded, it read the plans and found a mixed-substrate floor. When another
  bidder on the same project says nothing, that is the gap.
- A quantity that departs from the pack. Bidder-stated areas for one building have spread by about a
  fifth routinely, and on one project by more than half, where a single bidder read roughly a third
  less building than everyone else and carried that reading unchallenged into a second round. A
  quantity outlier is a reading dispute, not a takeoff error, and it belongs upstream as a question
  about which floors and which areas actually receive underlayment.
- A stated compressive strength or mat thickness below the specification. Read every assembly
  attribute against the spec on every bid, every round.
- A stated pour sequencing assumption. One bidder wrote that concurrent pours were possible but that
  its breakout assumed one floor at a time because that is what it had been told. That is the trade
  telling you the schedule question is unanswered.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number. Violating them converts the lump sum into a change order
or, in several cases, stops the work entirely. Treat each as a constraint the scope must honor or
price around.

- The building must be drywalled, taped and sanded before this trade arrives. Stated by most. This is
  a hard sequencing gate and it puts the pour late in the interior sequence.
- Minimum ambient temperature, typically fifty degrees, held for around twenty-four hours before and
  seventy-two hours after the pour, with the building enclosed and weather tight. Stated by nearly
  every bidder in some form.
- Material cannot be placed below freezing, and winter placement is renegotiable. Some bidders name
  a floor of thirty-two or thirty-five degrees and rising and say the work will not happen below it
  at any price. That is a gate, not a qualification.
- The floor must be sealed against leakage before the pour, because the material arrives liquid and
  will find every hole. Bidders disclaim all responsibility for leaks. On a wood-framed building this
  is a real damage exposure to the floor below.
- Firecaulking complete before arrival, for the same physical reason.
- Floors broom swept or vacuumed and free of other trades, materials and equipment. Some bidders
  will not work in a unit that is being painted or sheetrocked.
- The subfloor must be structurally sound to a stated deflection limit, and substrate design and
  deflection are the general contractor's responsibility.
- Water supply at a stated flow, typically fifteen to thirty gallons per minute, within a stated
  distance of the pumping area, plus a washout area and a dumpster.
- Unobstructed staging on flat ground, often at a stated minimum footprint, for trucks, pump and
  sand.
- Continuous ventilation after the pour, with fans moved around and windows open for the first days.
- Notice periods of two to three weeks per mobilization, with a cancellation charge inside forty-eight
  hours.
- Where the site is not ready on arrival, an hourly waiting fee and then a remobilization charge.
  Some bidders publish both rates.
- Commercial terms that materially change what a number is worth: a deposit on acceptance, payment
  for material on delivery, payment due the same day the work completes, a tax certificate
  requirement, and a personal payment guarantee from the contractor or owner. These are more
  aggressive than the market norm elsewhere and they belong on the leveling sheet.
- Where a bidder's terms say its quote's terms must be incorporated into any subcontract, check
  whether that subordinates the contract documents.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This trade is defined by what happens at its top surface, and almost every seam is with the finish
flooring contractor.

### The sealer: the seam that matters most

Nearly every bidder furnishes the sealer or primer as material and excludes its installation. The
finish flooring contractor applies it. That is the market convention, it is close to universal, and
it produces three consequences the drawings will never show:

1. The scope sheet needs two rows, furnish and install, and the install row is a plug on every
   bidder.
2. The sealer has a timing window. Where it is named, it goes down roughly twelve to twenty-four
   hours before the finish floor. It is not a post-pour operation, it is a pre-flooring operation,
   and it belongs to the flooring contractor's sequence.
3. Sealing is required specifically where glued-down floor goods will follow, because the adhesive
   needs it. Areas not receiving glued goods can be left unsealed. If nobody works out which areas
   those are, the material quantity is guesswork, and material is furnished by gallon of concentrate
   for dilution rather than by area.

The seam moves. One bidder furnishes sealer in the base on most of its bids and excludes it on
another, offering it as a small add. One excludes it outright while every competitor on the same
project furnishes it. Check every bid, every round.

### Flatness: the seam nobody owns

This is the highest-value finding in the trade and it is stated most plainly by the bidders
themselves. Gypsum underlayment, regardless of installer, achieves about a quarter inch in ten feet.
Finish flooring commonly requires an eighth inch in ten feet, or three sixteenths in two feet. One
bidder names the difference outright as a scope gap between Division 3 and Division 9.

What follows from that:

- Every proposal carries a disclaimer that the intent is not a level floor but a flat smooth surface,
  and that the underlayment follows the existing substrate contours.
- Remedial touch-up and floor preparation before the finish floor is excluded by essentially every
  bidder and pushed to the flooring contractor, sometimes via the general contractor.
- Where a specification does demand a finish-grade tolerance, the bidders who acknowledge it price it
  as an add alternate, not as a base condition. Those alternates have run from roughly a seventh to
  nearly a third of the base price, and the larger ones require a thicker pour to deliver, meaning
  the flatness upgrade is a different assembly rather than a finishing operation.
- The product also does not slope or pitch to drains, and does not reliably hold a height at the
  building perimeter that was set at a door frame, which one bidder flags specifically at balcony
  doors.

If the specification names a flatness tolerance and the scope sheet does not carry a leveling row,
every bid on the sheet is non-compliant and nobody has said so.

### The acoustic assembly: a five-trade outcome

The sound mat is not a comfort upgrade. Manufacturer assembly data attached to real proposals shows a
wood truss floor reaching impact insulation in the high thirties to low forties bare, and reaching the
high thirties to low forties again with the underlayment alone, and only clearing the code minimum of
fifty once the acoustic mat is added. The mat is the layer that makes the assembly comply.

That same data shows the rating depends on the finish floor covering, the resilient channel used by
the drywall contractor, and the cavity insulation. The impact rating on one identical assembly ranges
from the low fifties under thin vinyl to the high seventies under carpet. So the acoustic result is
owned jointly by this trade, flooring, drywall and insulation, and no single package can guarantee it.

And yet the mat is the item every value engineering round proposes to delete or thin. Downgrades from
premium to standard mat run roughly five to fifteen percent of the base price, and stepping from a
quarter inch to an eighth inch has been offered at around fifteen percent. Some bidders simply bid
the thinner mat in the base without flagging it as a deviation, and sell the specified assembly back
as an add alternate. Any mat change is an acoustic compliance change, and it should not be priced
without the rating requirement on the same sheet. One project's leveling sheet does put the impact
insulation class, the sound transmission class and the fire-rated assembly design number on the scope
rows, keyed to the drawing sheet that states them. That is the practice to copy.

### Pre-pours, tubs and the sequencing trap

Tubs and shower pans sit below the finished floor level and the areas under them, along with MEP
closets, chases and other dead spaces, cannot be poured with the main floor unless the fixtures are
already set. Every bidder excludes pre-pours from the base and prices them separately when asked,
usually by email rather than in the proposal.

Two consequences worth carrying:

- A pre-pour lands at a different height than the full floor pour, because of subfloor levelness and
  material limits, and that difference generates additional finish floor preparation which is itself
  excluded. A pre-pour is not a free scope addition.
- Some bidders instead require that tubs be set before they mobilize at all, which pushes the
  sequencing problem to plumbing rather than pricing it.

There is a cross-trade value engineering idea recorded against the drywall and plaster package on one
project: cement board under the tubs, which would allow tubs to be installed before the gypcrete
floors. That eliminates this trade's pre-pour scope entirely by moving substrate work to another
package. It is the kind of idea that only surfaces if both packages are on the table at once.

### Other seams

- Drywall. The pour follows taping and sanding. Drywall also owns the resilient channel that
  co-determines the acoustic rating, and on some projects the same subcontractor carries both
  packages, which hides the seam rather than resolving it.
- Tiling. Crack isolation or decoupling membrane below tile goes over the cured gypcrete and belongs
  to tiling. Where the tiling package assumes self-leveling compound instead of gypcrete, or assumes
  it is pouring its own substrate, two packages are pricing the same floor.
- Firestopping. Firecaulking must be complete before the pour, and bidders are emphatic about it.
- Framing and the general contractor. Substrate soundness, deflection, hole and gap repair, and
  leak sealing are all pushed upstream and all precede the pour.
- Doors and frames. Installed after the pours, per some bidders, which affects the door schedule
  sequence and the undercut.
- Protection. Temporary wood planking over the cured pour where wheeled or concentrated loads will
  cross it, furnished, installed and maintained by others. This is the item most likely to be
  discovered as damage rather than as a line.
- Vertical transport. Some bidders require a lull and driver from the general contractor to receive
  material and get mat to upper floors.
- Waste. Excess sand becomes the general contractor's property to remove or use, on nearly every bid.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What these contractors actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The floor assembly details and the sheet that names the underlayment product, thickness, mat
   product and mat thickness. Where a leveling sheet keys its rows to drawing numbers, these are the
   sheets it keys to.
2. Floor plans, for gross area by level. Quantities are taken as gross square footage with no
   deduction for interior walls or columns, which one bidder states outright. If your quantity nets
   out walls, it will not match any bid on the sheet.
3. The specification section, for strength, thickness and the named acceptable manufacturers.
4. The general contractor's own scope sheet, where one was issued. Two bidders on one project cite
   the scope sheets as their pricing basis rather than the drawings.

They do not price from a room-by-room takeoff. They do not price from the finish schedule, which is
why sound mat extent under carpeted versus hard-surface rooms is a question nobody answers.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- The mobilization count, which is the largest single variable in the trade. Bids on one building
  have carried from fourteen to thirty-six mobilizations, and on a smaller building from two to five.
  The winning bid on one project won by revising its price down on the explicit assumption that the
  whole job could be done in a maximum of two pump deployments. The general contractor can grant
  that concession or not, and it is worth more than any material decision on the sheet.
- The floor release sequence: whether floors come available one at a time or several at once, whether
  both buildings release together, and how many discrete sections each floor breaks into.
- Whether pours can run concurrently across floors.
- Union, open shop, or prevailing wage. The union to residential delta has been quoted at about
  eleven percent of base by a bidder who priced both.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies. Where it does, the credit runs around one
  percent of base and the conventional general liability add is similar.
- Warranty duration required. One year of workmanship is the market default and appears on nearly
  every bid. Where a specification asks for two, a bidder has provided it at no cost, which suggests
  the market default is soft and worth asking about.
- Whether the specification carries a flatness tolerance, and if so which one.
- Which levels and which portions of mixed levels actually receive underlayment. Two bidders on one
  project priced entirely different level ranges of the same building, one of them correcting itself
  two weeks later at thirty percent more money.
- Whether tubs and shower pans will be set before the pour.
- Whether the building will be enclosed, heated and weather tight on the intended pour dates, which
  on a winter schedule determines whether the work can happen at all.
- Site access for the pump: hose diameter through a wall or window opening, a staging footprint for
  trucks and sand, and a washout location.
- Water supply flow and proximity. Bidders name fifteen to thirty gallons per minute, within around
  one hundred and fifty feet.
- Water consumption, which one bidder published at roughly two thousand gallons per ten thousand
  square feet.
- Whether a mockup will be required. One bidder carries a mobilization for one.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders price from approximate areas and generally do not
cite a set at all; a meaningful minority of proposals in this trade name no basis document
whatsoever, on any round, which makes ordering rounds by cited basis impossible for those bidders and
leaves the letterhead date as the only signal. That date is not reliable either: a revised proposal
has carried the original letterhead date unchanged with the revision date appearing only in the
subject line more than two months later, and only the filename recorded it. By permit and construction
documents, the better bidders cite the set and its date and hold their areas stable across rounds,
which is what makes their price ladders readable.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet of poured area, gross, with no deduction for walls or columns. The primary unit and the
  leveling denominator on every estimator sheet seen.
- Mobilizations, pours or pump days. The second unit, and operationally the first. A mobilization is
  typically defined as one prepped-and-poured floor section in a single shift not exceeding eight
  hours. Bidders express the same idea as two-day mobs, three-day mobs, single day pours, pump days
  and trips per building, which makes counts non-comparable until normalized to shifts.
- Each, for pre-pours at tubs, showers and dead spaces.
- Days, for the sealer installation plug and for schedule commitments.
- Hours, for isolation strip removal plugs, for waiting fees and for extra work at a per-crew-member
  rate.
- Gallons of sealer concentrate, furnished for dilution rather than by area.
- Gallons per minute of water supply, and gallons of water consumed per ten thousand square feet.
- Linear feet, only where an unrelated concrete scope has been bundled into the same document.
- Percent of contract value, for bonds, which are usually excluded and requested above a contract
  value threshold.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. The mobilization count and the pour sequencing it implies. Nothing else in this trade moves a
   number this far. The same building has been bid at better than a two to one spread in mobilizations
   between competitors, and the trade's own revisions confirm it: the clearest documented price
   reduction in the corpus was bought with a schedule concession and no scope change at all.
2. Poured thickness. Three quarters of an inch and one inch are the two common depths, and the step
   between them is real material. A reduction from one inch to three quarters accompanied a sixteen
   percent price fall in one revision.
3. The sound mat specification. Premium to standard runs roughly five to fifteen percent of base as a
   deduct; a quarter inch to an eighth inch has been quoted at about fifteen percent. Some bidders bid
   the thinner mat silently and sell the specified one back as an upgrade at around eleven percent
   when the thickness upgrade is bundled with it.
4. Total area, which sounds obvious and is not, because bidder-stated areas for one building spread by
   about a fifth as a matter of course and by more than half in one case.
5. Labor basis. Union to residential rates has been quoted at about eleven percent.
6. Compressive strength. A step from a standard multifamily product at three thousand PSI to a high
   performance product at thirty-five hundred has been priced at about fifteen percent of base.
7. Flatness. Where a finish-grade tolerance is bought as an alternate it has run from roughly a
   seventh to nearly a third of the base, and it usually requires a thicker pour.
8. Pre-pours, which run roughly two to seven percent of base depending on how many locations and at
   which labor basis, and which are quoted at three to one variation between bidders for the same
   work.
9. Building size, in the sense that a smaller pour area carries a higher rate per square foot within
   the same proposal.
10. Insurance program and warranty duration, around one percent each where they appear at all.

Two conventions worth knowing about how the numbers get compared.

On dispersion. Within a single project the spread between the lowest and highest adjusted cost per
square foot has run about 1.3, 1.5 and 1.6 times. That is tight next to most finish trades, and the
tightness is deceptive. It is not evidence that the bidders priced the same scope; on the same sheets
the bidders' own stated quantities differ by a fifth, their mobilization counts differ by better than
two to one, and their assembly specifications differ on thickness, strength and mat. The prices
converge because the material and the crew are commodities. The scopes do not converge at all. A tight
spread in this trade means the sheet did not ask enough questions.

On leveling. An estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus plugs, and the three
plugs applied as standing rows are sealer installation, pre-pours and isolation strip removal. Beyond
those, watch the denominator. On one project the leveling denominator was taken from a single bidder's
stated area while the sheet's own location rows totalled about eight percent more. On another, three
different areas were live inside one sheet at once: the general contractor's budget quantity, the
leveling denominator about a quarter below it, and the bidders' own measurements clustered well below
both. A cost per square foot is only comparable across projects if the square foot means the same
thing, and inside a single sheet it may not even mean one thing.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly forty proposals covering about thirty-five distinct offers from
around a dozen companies, plus four estimator leveling passes across three multifamily residential
projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor. The proposals span design
development, permit and construction document rounds, and several companies appear on all three
projects, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable.
Two of the three projects are large wood-framed buildings over podium levels; the third is a smaller
two-building project.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from wood-framed multifamily residential work in one market through one
  general contractor. Nothing is tested against concrete-frame residential, commercial interiors,
  hospitality, institutional, healthcare or public prevailing-wage work.
- The trade's behavior over concrete substrates is barely represented. Bidders' conditions reference
  a twenty-eight day concrete cure and hydrostatic pressure, but no bid in this corpus is primarily a
  slab job, and the concrete areas of mixed levels are excluded by everyone rather than priced.
- No renovation or existing-building work appears. Repair, patch and partial-pour scope over an
  existing floor is untested here, as is anything involving demolition of an existing topping.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation. In particular, it is unknown who actually ended up installing the sealer and
  trimming the isolation strips on any of these jobs.
- No change order history and no field records. The claim that the flatness gap, the pre-pour height
  difference and the protection exclusion convert into change orders is read out of the proposals'
  own language rather than observed, so treat it as untested.
- No acoustic test report or field verification appears. The impact and sound transmission figures
  quoted here come from manufacturer literature that bidders attached to their own proposals, which
  is a self-interested source for an assembly claim.
- The corpus has not shown radiant heat tubing poured into the underlayment, which is a common
  variant of this trade and would change thickness, sequencing and coordination substantially.
- The corpus has not shown a fire-rated assembly requirement driving the underlayment specification,
  beyond a single design number appearing on one leveling sheet.
- Only one project's leveling sheet carried code rating rows, so the recommendation to put impact and
  fire ratings on the scope sheet rests on a single example, chosen because it is obviously right
  rather than because it is common.
- Every estimator sheet seen left its qualifications and exclusions rows for this trade empty, on all
  three projects, despite every proposal carrying a substantial exclusion list. So there is no record
  here of which exclusions an estimator considered material, and the exclusion analysis in section 4
  is read from the proposals alone.
