# Window Treatments

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the window treatment
package. Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets.
This file speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words
themselves. Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. This is a trade where the bidder authors the quantity, the
specified product is usually unbuildable, and the top of the shade is worth a fifth of the package.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Division 12, most often cited as 122400 or 122413 for shades, occasionally as 122000 for the package
as a whole. Bidders and estimators use these inconsistently; the same company has cited two different
section numbers for the same product on two projects. The trade token is the reliable identifier.

The package is normally shades at apartment windows plus a smaller amenity scope, and the two halves
behave differently enough that they belong in separate rows. Unit shades are a commodity bought in
the hundreds or thousands. Amenity treatments are a design item, drawn in the interior design
package rather than the architectural set, sometimes motorized, sometimes drapery, and priced on a
completely different basis.

Bidder kinds, and this determines packaging more than anything else:

- Window treatment specialists. The core of any bid list. They quote shades only, per each, and
  usually separate material, tax and installation.
- Drapery and soft goods houses. Also core. They quote one number for a count of shades with the tax
  inside it, and they are the ones who will price drapery by the fabric yard.
- Division 10 specialties houses. They carry shades as one section inside a much larger package that
  also holds toilet accessories, fire extinguisher cabinets, lockers, bike racks and partitions. The
  shade block is separately subtotalled and therefore extractable, but the proposal will normally
  condition its pricing on the whole package being awarded together, which conflicts with how a
  specialties tab actually gets bought.
- Blind and shade retailers, who quote from a manufacturer catalogue on a form with furnished-only
  and furnished-and-installed columns.
- Glaziers. Seen once, bidding aluminium blinds with glazing boilerplate carried onto the quote.
- Bundled interiors suppliers who also sell cabinets, countertops and shower enclosures, and whose
  window treatment entry may exist only as a column in a leveling sheet.

Collapse attractors:

- Drapery. Sometimes inside this package, sometimes routed to the owner's furniture package. Both
  routings appear in the corpus on different projects in the same office, which means it is a project
  decision and not a market convention. Ask it explicitly.
- Motorized shades at amenity. Usually inside the package, with the wiring outside it.
- Attic stock. Carried inside the base count by the retail-style bidders and excluded by name by the
  specialty houses. This is a quiet count difference.

Carve-outs, meaning scope that always leaves:

- All electrical, power and control wiring for motorized treatments, line and low voltage.
- Blocking, backing and any rough opening or pocket work.
- Flame certificate application and testing.

## 2. How the market bids

The default document is one page. A count, a product description, one number, and a validity period.
Nearly every proposal states its own shade count, and that count is the bidder's own takeoff.

Proposal shapes:

- One number against one count. The most common shape by a wide margin.
- Segmented by area with material, tax and installation broken out per segment. Common among the
  specialists, and the most useful shape, because the material half and the labor half are separable.
- Segmented by finish schedule mark, one count per mark. Less common and the most checkable, since
  the marks can be diffed against the schedule.
- A section subtotal inside a much larger multi-section package.
- A marked-up plan set with no prose and no price at all, filed as a proposal. Seen once, and that
  bidder still appears in the ranking with a number that exists only in the leveling sheet.

What a proposal will normally state:

- A shade count, usually flagged as approximate.
- Manufacturer, fabric name and openness percentage, and whether a fascia is included.
- Control type: manual clutch, cordless, motorized.
- Sales tax treatment. Included in the number by most, quoted as a conditional add by some, and
  broken out as its own line by others. Three treatments in one bid list is normal and printed totals
  are therefore not comparable as printed.
- Validity, commonly sixty or ninety days, and one instance of a full year.

What a proposal will normally not state:

- How the count was derived. No proposal in the corpus shows a window schedule reference, a room
  count, or a takeoff basis for its number.
- The head detail or top treatment condition, unless the bidder is flagging that it does not exist.
- Warranty duration beyond a stated manufacturer standard plus one year on installation.
- Attic stock, which is excluded by name by the specialists and silently included by the retailers.
- Insurance and bond, produced on request.
- Lead time, which is unusual for a made-to-order product and reflects that these ship fast relative
  to the rest of the interior.

Revision habits:

- A count change with the product held constant.
- A silent material downgrade. One bidder's revision three days later moved a blind slat gauge down
  by two thousandths of an inch, took five percent out of the material line, halved its own validity
  window, and changed nothing else. It was not labelled a deviation, an alternate, or a value
  engineering item. A total diff, a count diff and an exclusion diff would each have seen only a
  saving. Diff the product description sentence.
- A commercial answer appended at zero cost. A revision whose entire substance is a warranty term
  added to the bottom of an otherwise byte-identical page, in answer to a general contractor
  checklist.
- Scope entering. Amenity drapery, motorized amenity shades and an insurance line arriving at
  construction documents on a package whose unit count never moved.
- A reprint of an earlier round refiled at a later set stage. One such file was an image-only scan of
  a proposal already sitting in the earlier folder, nine months older than the folder it was filed
  in. Neither byte hashing nor text hashing merges those two; only rendering finds it.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Scope sheets in this trade have run about nineteen to fifty rows. The larger ones enumerate finish
schedule marks and cite sheets; the smaller ones carry a furnish-and-install row and a handful of
product notes. Target the larger shape.

### What enumerates

- The shade count itself, as a stated quantity on the sheet, decomposed by location. This is the
  most important row set in the trade and it is the one most sheets omit. See section 7 for why.
- Finish schedule mark. Where the schedule carries marks distinguishing living-area shades from
  bedroom shades by openness percentage, each mark is its own row with its own count. Bidders who
  read the schedule answer by mark; those who do not give one blended count.
- Location, separately from mark: apartment units, amenity and common areas, leasing and office
  areas, fitness, and any specialty room. Amenity is a different product, a different manufacturer
  and often a different document, so it never collapses into the unit count.
- Top treatment, its own row. Fascia, exposed roll, pocket, or cassette. This is the trade's largest
  single scope variable after the count. A fascia add on one project was worth about a fifth of the
  base bid, and it was an add rather than a base inclusion only because no head detail existed on
  the set. Never write shades as a single line without saying what happens at the top.
- Fabric openness percentage, its own attribute per mark, because it is code-constrained in some
  markets and drives substitution.
- Control type, its own row: manual clutch, cordless, motorized. Motorized rows carry their own
  wiring seam.
- Drapery, always its own rows, never inside the shade rows. Drapery is priced by fabric yardage plus
  fabrication labor plus install, which is a different measurement basis entirely, and it may not be
  in this package at all.
- Balcony and terrace doors, their own row. Whether the door leaf gets a treatment is answered
  differently by different bidders on the same sheet, and one estimator sheet recorded three answers
  to that single row: yes, no, and a question mark.
- Common area and lobby glass, its own row, for the same reason.
- Attic stock, its own row with a stated quantity or a stated zero.
- Shade training and turnover instruction, its own row. Priced as a small add by at least one
  bidder and carried by nobody else.
- Mobilization count, its own row. Prices are commonly based on one mobilization per section.
- Field measurement basis, its own row. See section 6; this is the trade's defining risk term.

### What collapses

- Individual windows collapse into a count per mark per location. No document in the corpus prices a
  single window, and the mark is the finest grain the market answers.
- Fabric color, hem bar type, bracket type and end caps are attributes of a line.
- Levels collapse into a level range unless the product changes.
- Freight collapses into the line.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

The test fires on the count, on the top treatment, on the balcony doors, on the common area glazing,
on drapery, and on attic stock. Each of those can be carried, excluded, or passed over in silence,
and each produces a different number. Every one of them has been observed being answered three
different ways by three bidders on one project.

Silence is emphatically a finding here. Because these proposals are so short, a scope that is not
named is very unlikely to be priced. A bidder quoting a flat unit-shade count and saying nothing
about amenity is not including amenity.

### The structural gap list

Scope no bidder carries, which the estimator must add before comparing:

1. All electrical work of any kind for motorized treatments: power, control wiring, line and low
   voltage. Excluded by every bidder seen.
2. Blocking, backing and rough openings. Excluded universally.
3. Any ceiling pocket, soffit or recess to conceal a shade. Excluded universally and owned by
   drywall, which is a real cost that appears in no shade number.
4. Flame certificate application and testing. Excluded by the specialists and unmentioned by
   everyone else.
5. Attic stock, excluded by the specialists.
6. Drapery, on the projects where the shade bidders exclude it and the owner's furniture package has
   not been confirmed to carry it.
7. Amenity treatments, where the bidder quoted units only.
8. Escalation and storage where the schedule slips, excluded explicitly by the specialty houses.
9. Overtime, off-hours and weekend rates.
10. Shade training and turnover.

## 4. Typical exclusions and qualifications

Two exclusion vocabularies exist in this trade and they are very different. The one-page drapery and
retail bidders carry almost no exclusions, usually a single boilerplate paragraph about custom orders
and shipping delays. The Division 10 specialty houses carry twenty to thirty lines. A bidder with no
exclusion list is not offering broader scope; it is leaving every boundary open.

| Excluded by the window treatment contractor | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| All electrical work, power and control wiring, line and low voltage (every bidder seen who mentions it) | Electrical |
| Blocking and rough openings (the specialty houses, universally) | Framing or drywall |
| Ceiling pockets, soffits and recesses (universally, by silence or by name) | Drywall |
| Attic stock (the specialty houses; silently included by the retailers) | Owner, or a plug |
| Flame certificate application and testing (the specialty houses) | Frequently unassigned |
| Special warranties beyond manufacturer standard plus one year on installation (the specialty houses) | Priced on request |
| Overtime, off-hours, night and weekend rates (the specialty houses) | General contractor |
| Escalation and storage costs if the schedule slips (the specialty houses) | General contractor |
| Material distribution by elevator, stair carry, street unload (the specialty houses, at a stated hourly rate) | General contractor |
| Additional mobilizations beyond one per section (commonly) | Change order |
| Draperies, where no locations or specifications were found (with a stated reason and sheet references) | Owner furniture package, or an RFI |

Qualifications that are really pricing bases:

- Field measurement off finished walls. The trade's central term. Shades are measured after the wall
  finish is on. If the shop must order ahead of finishes to hold the schedule, the general contractor
  signs off on hold-to dimensions and carries the cost of any variance. This is stated explicitly by
  the specialty houses and left implicit by everyone else, and it is the mechanism by which a shade
  package generates change orders.
- One mobilization per section, with further trips chargeable.
- Package award. The multi-section bidders condition their pricing on the whole package selling
  together and reserve a re-quote if sections are broken out. This conflicts structurally with a
  specialties tab assembled row by row from several bidders.
- Quantity conditionality. At least one bidder states outright that its pricing rests on its own
  quantities and reserves the right to reprice if its counts or model numbers prove to hold
  discrepancies. That is a bidder telling you its number is provisional on a count it authored.
- Validity windows, commonly thirty to ninety days, with market-volatility language attached, and
  one instance of a year.
- Tariff carve-outs, stated as a reservation rather than a priced line.
- Payment terms, including a credit card surcharge and a joint check agreement with the vendor.

The exclusion that carries the most project signal in this trade is the one with a reason and a sheet
reference attached: draperies excluded because no locations or specifications were found on two named
sheets. That is an RFI written by the market, and the corpus shows the same scope being routed to the
owner's furniture package on one project and plugged as a to-value amount inside the shade package on
another, which means nobody knows where it belongs by default.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

- Electrical. Every motorized shade needs power and control wiring, and no shade bidder carries any
  of it. The row must name the electrician and say what is being wired: motor power, a control
  station, a low voltage run, or a building system interface.
- Drywall. Two seams. The first is blocking for wall-mounted brackets. The second, and the expensive
  one, is a ceiling pocket. Where the finish schedule says wall mount with fascia and a ceiling
  detail shows a pocket, the two documents specify different work in two different packages, and the
  shade bidder priced one of them. An estimator sheet has recorded this exact condition with a note
  routing it to the drywall contractor as a question.
- Painting. Priming of walls before installation is excluded by at least one bidder and assigned to
  the painter.
- Interior design and furniture. Amenity treatments are drawn in the interior design package, not the
  architectural set. Drapery may live in the owner's furniture budget entirely. This is the boundary
  most likely to leave a hole.
- Glazing. A glass and mirror company has bid blinds in this corpus. The overlap is real but thin,
  and the glazing file's own record of adjacent seams matches.
- Finish carpentry. Where a shade sits behind a wood valance or inside a millwork recess, the recess
  is carpentry's and the shade bidder assumes it exists. No document in the corpus prices a valance.
- Windows. The window package sets the jamb depth and the head condition an inside-mount shade needs.
  Nothing in a shade proposal verifies it; the field measurement does, late.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What window treatment contractors price from:

1. The finish schedule, for shade marks, openness percentages and mount notes. The primary document
   where marks exist.
2. Unit plans and window elevations, for the count. This is a takeoff and the bidder does it.
3. Head details, for the top treatment. When they exist. When they do not, bidders say so and price
   the cheapest defensible assumption.
4. The interior design package, for amenity products, drapery and specialty rooms.
5. The specification, for the named manufacturer, which is very often not what gets quoted.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each moves the number:

- The count. See section 7. Nothing on the drawings settles it.
- Whether the specified product can actually be furnished. This is the trade's most consistent
  finding and it appears on three of four projects. Specified blinds have been rejected by every
  bidder on a project on the grounds that the drawn windows exceed the product's maximum height,
  compounded by cord restrictions that removed the specified operation from the market. A specified
  fabric openness has been rejected because the state's flammability requirement caps openness below
  the specified value. A specified fabric additive is banned outright in the market. And on one
  project the specified manufacturer had no representation in the bidding market at all, so a bidder's
  answer to the compliance row was simply that it does not represent that manufacturer. Expect the
  specified product to be a substitution target and write the scope line so a substitution is legible
  rather than silent.
- Local code and regulatory constraints on fabric and operation, which are market facts a national
  specification does not carry.
- Whether attic stock is expected and how much.
- Whether drapery is in this package or the owner's.
- The turnover sequence, which drives mobilization count.
- When the walls will be finished, which is what the whole trade's risk term turns on.
- Whether the general contractor will sign hold-to dimensions, and who carries the variance.

Set-stage behavior. Counts do not converge as sets develop. On one project four bidders across two
set stages carried counts spanning about sixty percent, and the highest count came from the last
round. What does change with set development is the arrival of amenity scope: drapery, motorized
treatments and specialty rooms appear at construction documents on packages whose unit counts never
moved.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Each, per shade. The market's unit, universally.
- Linear yards of fabric, plus fabrication labor, for drapery. A different basis on the same page as
  the per-shade lines.
- Square feet. The general contractor's unit, not the market's. A general contractor budget row has
  carried a window treatment quantity in square feet at a rate per square foot while every bidder
  priced per each, and a bidder responding to a square-foot inquiry replied with a shade count that
  did not match the square footage's implied window count.
- Apartment units, as the normalizing denominator on every leveling sheet in the corpus.
- Hourly rate per installer, for stair carry, elevator waiting and street unload.

The count problem is the whole trade and it deserves its own statement. There is no shade schedule on
these sets. The bidder authors the deliverable quantity by taking off the windows, and the counts
diverge badly. On one project the counts were 940, 965, 1086 and 1498. On another, 849, 856, 892 and
1016. On a third, 409, 424 and 448. On a fourth, 237 by the estimator's own row against 257 and 301
from bidders. The divergence has several causes that a scope line can address: whether balcony and
terrace doors are treated, whether common area glazing is included, whether attic stock is in the
count, and whether a wide window is one shade or two.

Every leveling sheet in the corpus normalizes to cost per apartment unit. That denominator never sees
the shade count. A bidder carrying sixty percent more shades and a bidder carrying sixty percent
fewer are compared on the same per-apartment basis with no adjustment, because the sheet has no count
row. This is the trade's biggest leveling weakness and the fix is simple: put a stated count on the
sheet, record each bidder's own count beside it, and normalize before ranking, exactly as the better
smoke curtain sheets do.

Cost drivers, ranked:

1. The shade count.
2. Top treatment. Fascia versus exposed roll has been worth about a fifth of a base bid.
3. Product class: roller shade versus blind versus drapery. On one small project the per-apartment
   rate spanned about 2.8 times, and the span is almost entirely product class, not competitiveness.
4. Fabric grade and openness, and whether a compliant fabric exists at the specified openness.
5. Manual versus motorized.
6. Whether amenity and drapery are in the package.
7. Tax treatment, which differs three ways within one bid list.
8. Labor basis. Both union and open shop appear in every bid list and the corpus does not support a
   claim about direction.
9. Mobilization count and turnover sequence.
10. Attic stock.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. Normalized to cost per apartment unit, spreads within a
single project have run about 1.51 times, about 1.74 times, about 1.83 times and about 2.8 times.
Bid lists run four to six companies.

What the spread does not prove. None of those spreads is a measure of competitiveness, because none
of them is computed on a common quantity. On the project with the 1.74 times spread the underlying
shade counts differed by sixty percent, so the per-apartment column is comparing a bidder pricing
about three shades per apartment against one pricing about four and a half. The 2.8 times spread on
the smallest project spans a roller shade offer and an aluminium blind offer, which are two products,
not two prices for one product. Conversely, the tightest per-apartment agreement in the corpus sits
on a project where one bidder quoted tax as a conditional add, one included it, and one had its tax
added as an adjustment by the estimator, so the closeness of the raw numbers was an artifact of what
each had left out. Scope convergence and price convergence are independent here, and in this trade
the quantity is scope.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on about twenty-three window treatment proposals from ten companies, plus
four estimator leveling sheets, drawn from four mid-rise residential projects in one regional market,
all bid by one general contractor. Proposals span concept budget, design development, permit,
construction document and conformed sets. Several companies appear on three or four of the projects,
which is what makes the revision and boilerplate observations testable.

Three of the twenty-three files needed special handling and each is worth knowing about: one is zero
bytes and errors rather than reading empty, one is an image-only scan that is a reprint of an earlier
round refiled under a later set stage, and one is a thirty-page marked-up plan takeoff with no price
that nonetheless has a ranked number in the leveling sheet. Roughly one file in seven is not what its
name says it is.

What the file cannot yet claim:

- Everything here is apartment shades plus a small amenity scope in one market. Nothing is tested
  against commercial office, hospitality, healthcare or institutional work, where motorized systems,
  building automation integration, blackout and dual-shade assemblies, and solar control engineering
  all become the substance of the trade rather than an amenity footnote.
- Motorized shades appear in the corpus only as a single small amenity line. Nothing here describes
  how a motorized package is bid, integrated or commissioned, and the electrical seam for a real
  motorized project is certainly richer than the one paragraph the corpus supports.
- The regulatory findings in section 6, fabric openness caps and a banned fabric additive, are
  specific to one state. The mechanism generalizes; the values do not.
- No drapery-led project is in the corpus. Drapery appears as one small amenity line on one project
  and as an excluded or routed scope on two others, so the yardage-based pricing convention in
  section 7 rests on a single document.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing shows which counts were
  bought or which exclusions survived.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that field measurement and hold-to dimensions
  generate change orders is read out of proposal language rather than observed.
- Nothing here shows what a correct shade count looks like. Every count in the corpus is a bidder's
  or an estimator's assertion, and none has been reconciled against an as-built quantity, so section
  7 can say that the counts diverge and cannot say which was right.
