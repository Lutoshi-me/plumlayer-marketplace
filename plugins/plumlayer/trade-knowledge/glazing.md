# Glazing

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the glazing package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you,
and what shape the answer has to be in so that real glaziers can bid it and a real estimator can
level it.

One thing to absorb before anything else. In painting, the bidders all bid the same work and differ
on price. In glazing they do not. A glazing bid list routinely contains companies selling different
things under the same trade heading, and the largest number on the sheet is often the one carrying
the most scope rather than the one charging the most. Scope breadth, not price, is the first thing to
establish about any glazing number.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

The anchoring specification section is aluminum-framed entrances and storefronts, in the 0841 range.
Unlike painting, a glazing proposal typically cites several sections at once and prices against each
of them separately: aluminum entrances and storefronts, interior glazing, exterior glazing, and door
hardware. A bidder naming four sections in one proposal is normal and is a good sign, because the
section it omits is usually the scope it is not carrying. The estimator's leveling tab for this trade
sits in the 08 4000 series, with windows, skylights and specialty doors on their own sibling tabs.

The package is not stable across projects, and that instability is the central fact of the trade.
Glazing is a bundle of detachable work families, and which families are in the bundle is decided by
the general contractor project by project, sometimes round by round. The families seen are:

- Exterior aluminum storefront, entrances and curtain wall.
- Interior aluminum storefront and borrowed lites.
- Glazing into hollow metal doors and frames, rated and non-rated.
- Fire-rated glazed openings as complete assemblies.
- Interior glass partitions and office fronts, either stick-built into frames by others or bought as
  a proprietary demountable wall system.
- Glass railings, guardrails and smoke baffles or draft curtains.
- All-glass doors, glass rail doors and the glazing in barn doors.
- Mirrors, split into unit bathroom mirrors, common area and amenity mirrors, fitness mirrors and
  elevator mirrors, each of which is owned differently.
- Applied glazing film.
- Shower enclosures.
- Folding and moving glass walls.
- Aluminum windows, where the same bidder is often the window bidder.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another
division or another section:

- Shower enclosures, by a wide margin the largest. Where a glazier carries them they run from
  roughly 45 to 60 percent of the whole glazing package, so whether they are in or out changes the
  number more than any other single decision. General contractors frequently route them to division
  10 instead and deduct them out of the glazing bids that carried them.
- Unit bathroom mirrors. Sometimes carried in the base, sometimes offered as a priced add naming a
  different supplier, sometimes routed to specialties, sometimes to the electrician when the mirror
  is illuminated.
- Glazing film. Almost always ends up here, and almost never appears on any other trade's sheet.
- Glass railings, guardrails, smoke baffles and draft curtains. Contested with metals, and the wood
  or metal cap on top of the glass is contested again with finish carpentry.
- The glazing in barn doors and in wood doors. The glass is glazing, the door is not.
- Aluminum windows on projects where there is no separate window package.
- Exterior wall panel, seen once, from an envelope contractor that bids siding as well as glazing.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like glazing:

- Shower enclosures to a dedicated enclosure specialist, who templates, fabricates and installs, and
  who is consistently cheaper than a glazier doing the same enclosures. Also to a cast-marble panel
  supplier who quotes the enclosure inside its surround package, and to a unit-fitout supply
  bundler who quotes it alongside cabinets and countertops.
- Interior glass partitions to demountable wall system vendors, who are a different kind of company
  from a glazier and who sell a manufactured product with union installation attached.
- Automatic door operators, sometimes to a door operator specialist named inside the glazier's own
  proposal.
- Unit mirrors to a mirror supplier.
- Louvers, which appear on the same schedule sheet as the storefront and are excluded by essentially
  every glazing bidder that mentions them.
- Skylights, interior storm windows and window treatments, each of which has its own leveling tab.

The practical consequence is that a glazing bid list contains at least these kinds of company, and
they are not comparable to each other: full-scope glaziers, storefront-only glaziers, supply-only
importers who do not install, demountable partition vendors, shower enclosure specialists,
install-only labor houses, unit-fitout supply bundlers, and envelope contractors. Before comparing
any two glazing numbers, establish which kind of company produced each.

## 2. How the market bids

This is a heavily itemized trade. The default output is not one number. It ranges from four bullets
and a total at the low end to eighty pages of manufacturer configurator output at the high end, and
the middle is a set of named work groups each with counted quantities.

Enumeration by mark is the norm. A glazing proposal will commonly list every storefront type mark,
every entrance by door number, every frame type receiving glass with a count, every mirror mark and
every film mark. Grain, not count, is the discriminating fact: two proposals with the same total may
enumerate at completely different depths, and the deeper one is usually the one that read the
schedules.

- Segmented into named work groups, each priced: the most common and most useful shape. Groups
  follow scope family rather than area or level.
- Enumerated by schedule mark with counts but a single price for the whole: also common, especially
  from bidders whose proposal reads as a scope confirmation rather than a takeoff.
- Fully quantified with square feet, linear feet and each, plus glass thickness and hardware set
  numbers per opening: seen from the more sophisticated bidders and worth a great deal at leveling.
- Four bullets and one number: seen, usually from a storefront-only bidder.
- Manufacturer configurator output, one page per unit with metric dimensions, thermal and acoustic
  performance per unit, and a weight and profile-length rollup: seen from imported systems.

Commercial lines get their own rows in this trade in a way they do not in painting. Tax, freight,
design and project management, tariff surcharge, duty and fees, tooling or mold charges, and monthly
escalation all appear as visible priced lines. A proposal total that includes tax and one that
excludes it are routinely sitting next to each other on the same leveling sheet.

Labor market is handled with unusual sophistication. Some bidders price the entire package twice,
union and open shop, as parallel columns. At least one prices a different labor market for each work
group inside a single base bid, union on the aluminum framing and non-union on mirrors, showers and
door lites. A leveling sheet with one labor-type field per bidder cannot represent that bid.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The basis document, named with a date, and often the specification sections with their issue
  dates. Nearly all do this, and this is the reliable way to order rounds.
- Whether addenda were acknowledged, usually as an explicit line, frequently reading that none were.
- The system and product designation for framing, glass makeup, coating, spacer and finish grade.
- Hardware sets by number, tied to specific door numbers.
- Lead times, per work family, stated in weeks and phrased against a trigger such as after approved
  shop drawings or after field verification. This trade states lead times far more consistently than
  most, because they are long enough to matter.
- Deviations from what is drawn, where the product cannot be achieved as shown.
- Deposit and payment terms, because the fabricators behind this trade demand money up front.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Whether it carries the work families it is silent about. Silence in this trade is not an
  inclusion, and given how variable the bundle is, silence is where the money hides.
- Whether the price includes installation. Supply-only offers are common and the disclosure is
  sometimes a single sentence deep in the terms rather than a headline.
- Who owns the perimeter joint. This is decided silently and differently by nearly every bidder.
- Whether hardware is included, at design development, when the hardware schedule does not exist yet.
- Quantities in any form, from the bidders that write in confirmation style rather than takeoff
  style.

Exclusion lists are long, ten to thirty lines, and are close to pure boilerplate. More important:
they routinely contradict the priced body of the same document. A proposal that prices fire-rated
glazing at nine named openings and prices delegated design as a visible breakout will also list
fire-rated systems and delegated design in its general exclusions. The priced breakout is
authoritative and the exclusion list is a pasted template. Never level a glazing package off the
exclusion lists alone.

Revision habits, across same-bidder revision pairs:

- Breakout restructure at an identical total. The most common revision in this trade. The bidder is
  asked to break its number out, it does, the total does not move by a cent, and it adds a line
  saying the breakouts are not standalone prices. The restructure moves no money and is the entire
  information content of the round.
- Mode change. The same bidder, the same openings, days apart, moving from glazing into frames by
  others to furnishing the framing itself, with the number moving by more than a factor of twenty.
  Nothing about the drawings changed.
- Comeback absorption. A first pass that carries no glazing at hollow metal doors, and a second pass
  a week later that carries sixty-odd openings of it. That scope is not volunteered; it has to be
  asked for.
- Scope entry disguised as a price increase. A storefront line going from interior only to exterior
  and interior between rounds, multiplying several times over. A reader comparing round totals
  without reading the work group labels would record a large price rise where the event is scope
  arriving.
- Configuration menus. A document carrying two or more complete priced configurations with no single
  one being the bid. A captured price without its configuration identifier is unusable.
- Deviation disclosure and breakout by reason. A supplier asked to split its number between positions
  drawn as its material and positions it substituted, so the two can be compared against competitors
  who priced what was drawn.

Two cautions on ordering rounds. Letterhead dates do not reliably advance with revisions: a bidder's
second round can carry the first round's date, so ordering by the printed date puts them on the same
day. Order by the cited basis document, and where that is equal, by the added content. And a
revision frequently exists only as an email sentence with two numbers in it, which is a real record
and often the most informative one in the folder.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Glazing scope sheets seen have run 25, 48, 50 and 106 rows. The 106-row sheet is the one where the
leveling actually worked, and it got there by enumerating every entrance door by number and location
across three levels and the roof. Target roughly sixty to a hundred scope lines for a full
multifamily glazing package, and expect the entrance and opening enumeration to be half of them.

The thin sheets fail in a specific, predictable way. On the two projects whose glazing sheets ran
under fifty rows, the scope rows below the top line were left unanswered entirely and the leveling
happened at base-bid level, which is exactly where a trade with this much scope variability cannot be
leveled. A glazing sheet that is never answered row by row is a sheet that produced a ranking of
seven different scopes.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- Work family, first and above everything else: exterior storefront, interior storefront, glazing at
  hollow metal, fire-rated openings, glass partitions, glass railings and guardrails, glass doors,
  mirrors, film, shower enclosures, moving glass walls, windows, louvers. This is the primary
  organizing axis in every estimator sheet and in most proposals, and it is the axis a general
  contractor asks bidders to break out along.
- Every storefront and opening mark that appears on the storefront schedule, split exterior and
  interior. Proposals routinely enumerate these to twenty and thirty marks, and the interior and
  exterior split matters because interior framing is non-thermal and much cheaper.
- Every entrance door by number, with its hardware set number. This is the finest grain the trade
  uses and the one that catches disagreements: a bidder reading the elevations may correct the
  general contractor's own door-type list, and a door deleted between sets will be caught here or
  nowhere.
- Every hollow metal door and frame type receiving glass, with a count and its rating. Bidders that
  carry this scope enumerate it to fifty and sixty openings across six or more frame types. The
  frame type vocabulary is project-specific and carries no meaning transferable to another set.
- Furnish and install mode, as an attribute of each work family rather than of the proposal. Mode
  varies within one proposal, and the same openings priced glaze-only versus framing-furnished
  differ by more than an order of magnitude. This is the single most valuable field on a glazing
  scope line.
- Each mirror mark separately: unit bathroom, common area, amenity restroom, fitness, elevator,
  breezeway. They are owned by different trades and a single mirrors line will always be ambiguous.
- Each film mark separately. Custom and gradient films run well above stock films per square foot,
  and elevator door film prices per leaf rather than per area.
- Glass railing and glass guardrail as distinct lines from glass doors, and the cap on top of the
  railing as its own line because the cap is usually somebody else's.
- Shower enclosures by configuration, not just by count. Panel-door-panel and door-panel are
  different instruments: dropping one fixed panel per enclosure can remove a third of the shower
  block. Also enumerate the enclosure count separately from the surround count, because on a real
  project those differ, and not every shower that gets a surround gets a glass door.
- Labor market per work group, not per bidder.
- Delegated design, engineering and the professional engineer's stamp on shop drawings, as their own
  line. It is specified, it is priced as a visible breakout by bidders that carry it, and it appears
  in the boilerplate exclusions of the same documents.
- Testing, both field and laboratory, as its own line. Carried by some, excluded by most.
- Field measurement versus hold-to dimensions, as a stated basis. Fire-rated framing generally
  requires hold-to dimensions and has the longest lead time of anything in the package; proprietary
  systems advertise not needing them, which is a schedule argument rather than a price argument.
- Perimeter sealant, broken by side: exterior bead, interior bead, and the air and vapor barrier or
  membrane flashing behind them. One bidder includes both beads, another includes exterior only,
  another excludes perimeter caulking outright, and essentially all of them exclude the barrier and
  the flashing.
- The commercial rows, each as a line: sales tax, freight, tariff or duty, tooling or mold charges,
  bond, general liability where an owner-controlled program may or may not apply, and warranty term.
- Warranty duration explicitly. A two-year workmanship warranty where the market default is one year
  is a real adder, quoted at around two percent of project value, and it is invisible on the drawings.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual lites, panels and fields within a single opening or wall mark. Bidders count marks, not
  panes.
- Glass makeup attributes: thickness, tempering, lamination, coating, spacer, low-iron, argon fill.
  These are attributes of a line and they vary by the line's exterior or interior condition, which is
  already an enumeration axis.
- Hardware components within a hardware set. The set number is the unit; a set has a dozen parts and
  the market prices it as one opening.
- Panel-by-panel enumeration within a demountable partition run. The market prices linear feet of
  wall plus a door count.
- Anchors, clips, gaskets, shims and fasteners. These are inside the system price everywhere.
- Shop drawings and submittals as separate lines from the work group they belong to, unless the
  professional engineer's stamp is separately specified.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers. In glazing that
test fires on almost every work family in section 1, which is why the scope sheet has to be long. It
fires hardest on the four items where being silent is the normal behavior: glazing at hollow metal
doors and frames, the perimeter joint, the entrance hardware, and installation itself.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. If a work family is one that some bidders carry
and others route elsewhere, and a given proposal says nothing about it, that silence is a scope gap
to surface, not an inclusion to assume. In this trade that is not a marginal case, it is the usual
case, and it is why glazing numbers on one project can differ by a factor of six without any of them
being wrong.

### The structural gap list

These are the items the market systematically will not carry, that an estimator adds to every number
before comparing. A scope list naming all of them is doing the plugging in advance.

1. Installation labor against supply-only bids.
2. Entrance hardware where the schedule did not exist at bid time.
3. Automatic operators.
4. Glazing at hollow metal doors and frames.
5. Unit bathroom mirrors.
6. Glazing into wood-framed partitions where a bidder excluded it.
7. Shower enclosures being moved in or out of the package.
8. The perimeter joint and the barrier behind it.
9. Blocking.
10. Delegated design and the engineer's stamp.
11. Testing.
12. The two-year warranty uplift.
13. Bond.

Read this section with the self-contradiction rule in front of you. In this trade a printed exclusion
list is a pasted template that travels between projects and sometimes between unrelated competitors,
and it will often exclude something the same document prices in its body. Where the body prices it,
the body governs.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of that table is boilerplate. What is project signal, because it is written for this set and
appears nowhere else in that bidder's other proposals:

- A deviation disclosure. When a bidder writes that a specific mark cannot be achieved as specified,
  that the offered product line cannot deliver the drawn material, that a panel count will differ
  from what is shown, or that applied muntins will look different because the drawings do not
  incorporate the door stile, it read the drawings and found a real problem. These paragraphs are
  the highest-value content in a glazing proposal and they are never boilerplate.
- An exclusion carrying a stated reason. Flashing excluded because no details were provided. A frame
  type priced narrowly because the elevations are ambiguous, with an invitation to correct it. A
  window type excluded because none is shown. These are the project's request-for-information list,
  pre-written by the market, and they should be routed upstream rather than absorbed at leveling.
- A named conflict between documents. A storefront frame depth specified one way and detailed
  another, priced at the specification with the difference quantified. Fire-rated glass absent from
  the glazing specification but shown in the door schedule, with the bidder saying which one it
  priced. Two conflicting detail references for the same smoke baffle. Bidders disclose which side
  they priced, sometimes per line, and different bidders pick different sides.
- A supplier-side market fact. A specified proprietary railing system carrying a linear-foot minimum
  the project does not reach, so a different base shoe was quoted. A specified door series that is
  not compatible with the specified framing series. Neither is visible on any drawing.
- An acknowledgement of an architect's bulletin by number, when the other bidders acknowledge none.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

Treat each as a constraint the scope must either honor or price around.

- Tolerance of the work the glazier receives. Out-of-plumb drywall, floor elevation variance, ceiling
  and soffit levelness, and dimensional variance at the opening. Bidders state numeric thresholds,
  and beyond them the price changes or the gap is not their problem. One disclaims flyby and
  perimeter caulking specifically on the ground that it cannot control drywall condition, while
  offering gasketing instead.
- Climate control. Prefabricated partition systems require a stated temperature and relative humidity
  band maintained from installation through final occupancy. That is a mechanical commitment hidden
  inside a partition proposal.
- Sequencing and readiness. Wet work complete and dry, work above ceilings substantially complete,
  spaces enclosed and weather-tight, areas clear of other trades and tools.
- Access. Exclusive use of a dedicated elevator of adequate size to take full glass lites without
  early unloading, loading dock and staging. Where windows are installed from the interior that is
  stated as the basis, with sidewalk protection by others.
- Deposit and cash flow. Fifty percent of material at order is common and seventy-five percent
  appears on imported systems, sometimes before the manufacturer has even reviewed the order.
  Custom-fabricated goods are non-returnable and non-refundable, and changes after order entry are
  new orders. This is a real commercial exposure that no other finish trade carries.
- Price validity. Fourteen to thirty days is normal, which is very short, and one bidder attaches a
  standing monthly escalation charge after its price protection lapses. A glazing number more than a
  month old should be treated as stale.
- Measurement timing. Enclosure and mirror suppliers disclaim any measurement taken before the
  mounting surfaces are complete, and charge for the wedges and fillers that out-of-plumb conditions
  require.
- Commercial exhibits. One bidder makes its offer contingent on a document being incorporated into
  the subcontract that rewrites indemnity, removes pay-when-paid, bars cross-project setoff, limits
  termination for convenience and adds attorney-fee recovery. Others cap liability at the contract
  value, waive consequential damages and compel arbitration. These change what the number is worth
  and belong on the leveling sheet as their own rows.
- Labor basis. Open shop or union, and whether prevailing wage applies. Stated by nearly every
  bidder in this trade, unlike painting where it often has to be asked for.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the longest section because this is a seam trade. Most of what goes wrong in a glazing
package goes wrong at a boundary rather than at a price.

### The door, frame and hardware handoff

The most consistent boundary in the trade. Aluminum and storefront doors, their frames, and
their hardware are excluded by every hollow metal door and hardware bidder seen, on the assumption
that they belong to glazing. Glazing bidders do carry them, and they carry them properly: entrance
hardware is priced against the specification's hardware section, opening by opening, at part-number
grain, with hinges, exit devices, cylinders, closers, overhead stops, thresholds, sweeps and
gasketing enumerated per door. One glazier labels its own interior aluminum storefront work group
with the door-frame-hardware abbreviation, which is as clear a statement of where this sits as the
market gives.

Three cautions. First, at design development the hardware schedule usually does not exist, and the
glazier asks the general contractor to carry an allowance instead, which on one project ran around a
sixth of the storefront base bid. Do not read a design-development storefront number as including
hardware. Second, a storefront-only or supply-only bidder often does not carry hardware at all, or
carries only what a schedule it never saw would have required. Third, the hardware for an aluminum
entrance is not a rounding error: an access-controlled automatic entrance pair with electrified
concealed vertical rod exits, power transfers, position switches, a controller, a power supply and
touchless actuators is the most expensive single opening in the trade, several times the cost of an
ordinary storefront door.

### Glazing into hollow metal doors and frames

The glass in someone else's door. The hollow metal supplier furnishes the door and frame, the glazier
furnishes and installs the lite. This scope is enumerated to fifty and sixty openings by frame type
and rating on projects that have it, and it is the scope most likely to be missing from a first-pass
proposal. Seen once with unmistakable clarity: a glazier's first round on a set carried none of it,
and its second round a week later carried sixty-three openings of it. Nobody volunteers this scope.
Ask for it explicitly, by frame type and rating, or it will not be there.

Note also that fire-rated glass is frequently specified only in the door schedule and not in the
glazing specification, which bidders notice and say so. That is a documents conflict to route
upstream, not a leveling problem to absorb.

### Interior glass partitions and office fronts

The most commercially confused scope in the trade, because two completely different kinds of company
bid it and the drawings usually favor one of them without saying so.

The specified solution on one project was a wood-framed glass partition: a millwork supplier furnishes
the veneered frames and the glazier supplies glass, doors and door hardware only. Every glazier
answered frames by others. Against that, demountable wall system vendors bid the same rooms with a
proprietary manufactured aluminum-and-glass system, complete with doors, pulls, magnetic locks and
union installation, and the substitution appeared on the leveling sheet as a value engineering row
worth a substantial credit against the specified solution. A third route exists where a glazier
carries a proprietary demountable system as its own work group, and a fourth where a glazier
excludes it entirely on the ground that the system is sold only through certified fabricators and
installers.

So four commercial shapes, and the drawings will not tell you which one applies:

1. Glazier glazes into frames furnished by millwork. Cheapest glazing line by far and the easiest to
   under-scope, because the glazing line looks trivial next to the frames it does not include.
2. Glazier furnishes the aluminum framing, doors and glass as a complete interior storefront.
3. A demountable wall vendor supplies and installs a manufactured system, competing as a substitution.
4. A glazier resells the demountable system as one of its work groups.

The gap between shapes one and two is enormous. The same bidder priced the same nine interior
openings glaze-only in one round and framing-furnished eleven days later, and the number moved by a
factor of twenty. If your scope line does not carry the mode, it is not a scope line.

The demountable vendors also expose dependencies that stick-built glaziers never mention, because
they price them as conditions rather than courtesies: floor and ceiling flatness tolerances they will
not correct, a climate band maintained through occupancy, seismic bracing above nine feet excluded,
non-returnable custom fabrication, and a fifty percent material draw at order.

Card readers and exit buttons are by others in every version of this scope. Magnetic locks are
supplied by the partition; the power and the reader are not.

### Shower enclosures

The largest and most contested block in the trade, and four different kinds of company bid it.

Where a glazier carries enclosures they run from roughly 45 to 60 percent of the whole glazing
package. General contractors frequently route them to division 10 instead, and the leveling sheet
then deducts the enclosure value back out of the glazing bids that carried it. Expect both treatments
on the same project family.

The four routes, priced:

1. A glazier, inside the glazing package. The most expensive route seen, and materially so.
2. A dedicated enclosure specialist that templates, fabricates, installs and warranties. On identical
   enclosure counts this route ran roughly 13 to 26 percent below the glaziers on one project and
   roughly 17 percent below the lowest glazier open-shop price and 41 percent below the union price
   on another. Where an enclosure count is large, this is the route that wins.
3. An offshore supply route inside a unit-fitout bundle, with a certified third-party installer.
   Cheapest on material, but it carries a one-time tooling or mold charge equal to about half the
   material value, plus duty and container freight, none of which scale with quantity and none of
   which appear in a domestic glazier's price.
4. A cast-marble surround supplier quoting the enclosure alongside its panels, which is why a
   glazier's deduct alternate may be phrased as substituting its own hardware brand for the marble
   supplier's enclosure.

A fifth partial route exists and is worth knowing: an install-only labor house that installs
enclosures and surrounds furnished by the general contractor. That bid gives the cleanest read in the
corpus on the furnish and install split, and installation is roughly fifteen percent of the delivered
cost of a glass enclosure. A ranking sorted by total will put that install-only number near the top
and it is not a cheap package bid.

Two quantity traps. First, enclosure counts differ between bidders reading the same set, by single
digits, and neither is obviously wrong. Second, the surround count and the enclosure count are not
the same number: on one project there were nearly ninety more surrounds than glass doors. Enumerate
them separately.

Configuration is the other lever. Panel-door-panel versus door-panel is a third of the block, and
whether the enclosure has a header is a real price difference. Capture the configuration with the
count or the count is not usable.

Finally, the substrate. Enclosure suppliers write pages of qualifications about work other trades
performed: they drill into tile and marble and disclaim what cracks, they will not warrant a leak
where the base is incorrectly pitched, they void leak guarantees where mounting is on anything but
tile or marble, and they charge extra for the wedges and fillers that out-of-plumb walls need. Every
one of those conditions is created by the tile or panel trade and paid for at the enclosure.

### Mirrors

Ownership is genuinely unresolved and the estimator's own notes show it. On one leveling sheet the
mirror rows carry the questions by electrician or specialties, by elevator, and where, written by the
estimator against three different mirror marks. Enumerate every mirror mark separately.

- Unit bathroom mirrors. Sometimes in the glazing base, sometimes an add naming a third-party mirror
  supplier, sometimes excluded to specialties, sometimes to the electrician when illuminated. Framed
  and unframed are different products at different prices, with framed running well above unframed.
- Framed amenity and powder room mirrors. Most glaziers decline these on the ground that they are
  proprietary items the general contractor can buy directly; one carried them.
- Fitness and common area mirrors. Usually in the glazing base, applied with mastic or mechanically
  fastened with channel. Plywood, blocking and wood trim are by others everywhere, and the wood frame
  around a sheet mirror belongs to finish carpentry.
- Elevator mirrors. Often the elevator supplier's, and worth confirming rather than assuming.

### Glass railings, guardrails, smoke baffles and draft curtains

The glazier furnishes the glass, the base shoe, the adhesive, the gaskets and the shims, and sets it
wet-glazed. What leaves the scope every time is the blocking or plywood, the welding at connecting
joints, and the cap. The cap is the specific collapse point: a wood cap belongs to finish carpentry, a
metal cap is contested with metals, and the estimator's own note on the row is likely to be a
question about who has it. Give the cap its own line.

Smoke baffles and draft curtains at open stairs are a real glazing scope that reads like nothing else
on the drawings. They are priced by linear foot of tempered laminated glass and they are easy to
miss entirely.

### Automatic operators and electrified hardware

Three treatments seen, all on the same project. One bidder prices operators as its own numbered scope
with a stated unit rate and an explicit rate for additional operators. Another lists automatic
operators in its general exclusions and prices them in its body anyway at the two doors the hardware
schedule calls for. A third names a separate door operator company as supplying and installing them,
which is the only proposal seen that resolves the question by naming who else has it.

In every version, the wiring, connections, power supplies and integration with the security and fire
alarm systems belong to the electrical and security divisions. The glazier supplies and installs the
operator, the actuators and the mounting boxes and stops there. An operator plus its actuators costs
several times what an ordinary storefront closer and exit device cost, so leaving it ambiguous is
expensive.

### The perimeter joint

The one seam where the market has no convention at all. On a single project one bidder included
interior and exterior beads of perimeter caulking at the storefront framing, one included exterior
caulking only, and one excluded perimeter caulking outright while offering gasketing at flyby
conditions instead. All three excluded the air and vapor barrier and the membrane flashing behind
the joint.

Note how this meets the painting package: painters bound their caulking to painted-to-painted
surfaces, so a storefront perimeter joint between aluminum and drywall is outside the painter's
scope by definition. If the glazier also excludes it, nobody has it. Make the perimeter joint its own
scope line, split by side, and name the barrier and flashing separately.

### The windows boundary

Windows and glazing are the same bidder more often than not, and bids filed under each token
routinely turn out to be the other. On one project the entire glazing package is windows plus door lites
plus mirrors plus draft curtains, with no storefront at all. On another a window supplier's aluminum
proposal is filed under the glazing token. The estimator's own tabs record the routing explicitly,
naming the sibling tab and the value sitting in it, which is where the package boundary is actually
written down.

Where windows and storefront meet physically, the substitution is real: a window type too large to be
achieved as the specified window becomes storefront framing carried as an allowance. A folding or
sliding glass wall at an amenity room is glazing, prices at more than double the exterior storefront
rate per square foot, and carries a long lead time and a deposit.

### Other seams

- Barn doors. The glass is glazing and the door package is not. One bidder priced both ways in the
  same document: glazing only in the base, and the full door, frame and hardware package as an
  alternate at roughly eight times the glazing-only value. Two answers to what looks like one row.
- Supply-only offers. Imported system suppliers deliver to the tailgate and install nothing. The
  disclosure can be a single sentence in the terms. On one project the general contractor carried
  install as another sub's value against a supply-only European entrance package, and the install
  ran to about ninety-five percent of the supply value. Treat any supply-only glazing number as
  roughly half of the delivered cost until proven otherwise.
- Floor closers. The pocket in the slab for a floor closer box belongs to concrete, and at least one
  glazier excludes slab chipping by name. It has to happen before the door arrives.
- Louvers. They appear on the storefront schedule sheet, which pulls them toward this package, and
  essentially every glazing bidder excludes them.
- Exterior wall panel. Seen once, from an envelope contractor bidding glazing, siding and panel under
  one heading. Where a glazing proposal contains a division 7 scope it is over-broad, and the panel
  value has to come out before the glazing numbers are comparable.
- Demolition and existing windows on renovation work. Removal, temporary protection, opening
  preparation and sidewalk protection are excluded by the glazier and belong to demolition and the
  general contractor.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What glaziers actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The storefront schedule and its elevation and detail sheets. This is the primary document. Marks
   come from here and nearly every proposal enumerates against it.
2. The door schedule and the door hardware schedule with its hardware set numbers. The hardware sets
   are how the entrance hardware gets priced at all, and their absence at design development is why
   an allowance appears instead. The door schedule is also where fire-rated glass is often specified
   when the glazing section omits it.
3. Enlarged unit plans and unit schedules, for the shower enclosure and unit mirror counts.
4. The finish and amenity schedules, for mirror marks, film marks and specialty glass marks.
5. The specification sections, cited by number with their issue dates, for framing systems, glass
   makeup, performance criteria, hardware and delegated design.

They price against marks and schedules, not against a room-by-room takeoff. Where quantities appear,
they are square feet of glazed area, linear feet of framing, and counts of openings.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of these moves the number:

- Which work families are actually in this package on this project. The largest single unknown, and
  the reason two honest glazing bids can differ by a factor of six.
- Whether shower enclosures are in glazing or in division 10.
- Whether the bidder installs. Supply-only is a legitimate market shape and installation roughly
  doubles the delivered cost of an imported package.
- Union versus open shop, and whether prevailing wage applies. Open shop runs roughly 12 to 28
  percent below union by work group, widest on the labor-intensive glass railing work and narrowest
  on the material-dominated storefront.
- Tariffs and duty on imported aluminum and glass. Quoted as an explicit surcharge or up-charge, and
  seen at roughly four percent of a demountable partition total and roughly nine percent of an
  imported entrance door supply value. This is a live, changing commercial condition and it belongs
  on the scope line, not in a footnote.
- Tooling and mold charges on offshore-manufactured enclosures, which do not scale with quantity.
- Lead times, which are long and which vary enormously by work family: two to three weeks for glass
  after field verification, four to six for mirrors and railing hardware, eight to ten for aluminum
  storefront production after approved shop drawings, twelve to fourteen for thermally broken poured
  doors, and fourteen to sixteen for fire-rated framing which also requires hold-to dimensions.
  Imported systems add eight to twelve weeks of ocean freight on top of production. Fire-rated
  openings and imported systems drive the buyout date for the whole trade.
- Deposit terms and the cash-flow exposure they create.
- Whether the specified product can actually be achieved as drawn, and whether the specified system
  carries a quantity minimum the project does not reach.
- Whether the specified door series is compatible with the specified framing series.
- Whether the general contractor will accept field measurement or demand hold-to dimensions.
- The warranty term required. One year is the market default for workmanship; a two-year requirement
  costs about two percent of project value and does not appear on a drawing.
- Insurance basis and bond. Where an owner-controlled program applies, some bidders offer no deduct
  at all because insurance sits in their general overhead; others price the add to exclude it at just
  under one percent. Bond runs about one percent of contract value.
- Elevator and hoist availability, dock access, and whether the elevator will take a full glass lite.
- Whether the space will be climate-controlled through occupancy, for prefabricated systems.
- The tolerance of the substrate the glazier receives, and who pays when it is out.

Set-stage behavior is pronounced in this trade. At design development, bidders count storefronts by
location rather than by mark because the schedule marks do not exist yet, ask for a hardware
allowance because the hardware schedule does not exist yet, and price interior openings as a
glaze-only service. By permit and construction documents, the same bidders switch to enumerating
every mark and every door number, attach a priced hardware appendix at part-number grain, and price
the interior openings as furnished framing. Reading a design-development glazing number as if it
were a construction-document number will understate the package badly, and the understatement is
concentrated in hardware and in interior framing.

One more. Do not trust a proposal's filing. Glazing files in this corpus were misfiled across
projects in both directions with byte-identical copies, filed under a sibling trade token, filed
under a token naming a different scope than the document contains, and filed as a single-trade quote
when the document is a multi-division supply bundle. Several proposals are image-only or
near-image-only and return no useful extracted text at all, and one returns mojibake on every
ligature. Open the document, render it if the extracted text looks thin, and read who it is addressed
to and which project it names before believing the folder.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Square feet of glazed area, for storefront, interior glazing, mirrors, film, railings and shower
  glass. The most common quantified unit and the one most bids can be normalized to.
- Linear feet, for storefront and partition framing runs, for glass railing and guardrail, for smoke
  baffles, and for the perimeter of an imported window and door package. Demountable partitions are
  bought in linear feet of wall plus a door count.
- Each, for doors, entrances, shower enclosures, mirrors, automatic operators, film-treated elevator
  doors and openings receiving glass.
- The opening, for hardware. A hardware set is priced per opening and the set number is the unit.
- The leaf, for storefront doors on an estimator's plug sheet.
- The location, for scattered items like back-lit mirrors and applied film at existing glazing.
- The hour, for existing-conditions operations on renovation work such as salvaging and reinstalling
  a historic window.
- Square metres, kilograms and metres of profile, on imported configurator output, which do not
  compare to any other bid on the project without conversion.
- The residential unit, as the normalizing denominator on three of four estimator sheets. The fourth
  levels on a lump sum. The basis is not consistent even within one estimator's own workbooks, so
  read it off each sheet rather than assuming.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, general liability, warranty uplift, tariff surcharge, sales
  tax and monthly escalation.

Cost drivers. In this trade the commercial-shape questions genuinely outrank the price variables, so
they come first:

1. Scope breadth. Which work families are in the number. This dominates everything else and by a
   wide margin.
2. Furnish and install mode, per work family. Glaze-only versus furnish-the-framing on the same
   openings differs by more than an order of magnitude, and supply-only versus delivered-installed
   roughly doubles.
3. Whether shower enclosures are in the package, and by which of the four routes.
4. Union versus open shop, per work group.
5. Total glazed area, and the exterior-to-interior split within it, since interior framing is
   non-thermal and much cheaper.
6. The entrance count and the hardware complexity per entrance. Access control, electrified exit
   devices and automatic operators can multiply an opening several times over a plain storefront door.
7. Glass makeup: low-iron versus clear, coating selection, lamination, triple versus double glazing.
   These are offered as deducts and they are specification compliance changes wearing a price tag.
   A base bid that fell because the coating changed is not a cheaper bid for the same thing.
8. Framing system depth and finish. Where a specification and a detail disagreed on storefront frame
   depth, the bidder quantified the difference at nearly twice what it charged to upgrade the same
   storefront from a manufacturer standard finish to a custom paint finish. A frame depth conflict is
   not a detailing nicety.
9. Tariffs, duty, container freight and tooling charges on imported and offshore product.
10. Fire-rated openings, which are dramatically more expensive per opening than anything else in the
    package. On one small project a single fire-rated door assembly cost more than the entire
    thirty-six-enclosure shower package.
11. Proprietary and specialty systems: folding glass walls at more than double the storefront rate
    per square foot, custom gradient film well above stock film, and vendor-proprietary framing
    systems at a premium over the specified assembly, sold on schedule certainty rather than price.
12. Delegated design, testing and mock-ups.
13. Warranty term, bond and insurance basis.
14. Access, hoisting, phasing and off-hours work.

On dispersion, and no dollar values are given here. Within a single project the spread between the
lowest and highest normalized glazing bid ran at about 3.4 times, 6.7 times and 16 times on the three
projects that normalize per unit. Those spreads are almost entirely scope-breadth artifacts and not
price signals: at the bottom sit interior-partition-only and storefront-only offers, in the middle
sit glaziers without shower enclosures, and at the top sit glaziers carrying three hundred and fifty
enclosures. Ranking those numbers against each other ranks different scopes. Bidder counts per
project ran from five to eleven ranking rows, but the ranking rows overstate the depth of the list,
because several rows are the same company at different rounds and some are product offers rather than
companies.

The most useful single fact about ranking in this trade: in none of the four projects was the
lowest-ranked glazing row the selected sub. On one project the selected bidder was eighth of eleven
rows. A glazing ranking sorted by total, without scope normalization, is close to meaningless, and
the estimator knows it, which is why the adjustment column does more work here than in any other
finish trade.

An estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus adjustments, where the adjustments
are plugs added to every bidder to cover what the market systematically will not carry.

A last note on estimator plug rates, because they are worth knowing as a sanity band even though they
are not market prices. The estimator's own budget rate for exterior storefront ran about a third
below what the market actually bid on the same openings, and its lump-sum plug for an interior
storefront ran several times below what the market charged once the mode turned out to be
furnish-and-install. Budget rates in this trade are systematically light, and they are light in the
two places where mode and breadth are ambiguous.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on about twenty-eight glazing-family proposals and four estimator leveling
sheets, drawn from four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one
general contractor. The proposals span design development, permit, construction document and
conformed sets, and include full-scope glaziers, storefront-only glaziers, imported-system suppliers,
demountable partition vendors, dedicated shower enclosure specialists, an install-only labor house,
two unit-fitout supply bundlers and an envelope contractor. Several bidders appear on more than one
project, which is what makes the boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable,
and several appear at more than one round on the same project, which is what makes the revision
patterns in section 2 testable.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work with amenity and back of house space, in
  one market, through one general contractor. Nothing is tested against commercial office interiors,
  institutional, healthcare, retail or public prevailing-wage work, and the interior office front
  scope in particular would look very different on a commercial fit-out.
- Only one project carried a true exterior curtain wall as a distinct scope, and even there it
  entered a bidder's package mid-round rather than being bid cleanly from the start. Everything this
  file says about curtain wall as distinct from storefront rests on that single case and should be
  treated as untested.
- No unitized curtain wall, structural silicone glazing, point-supported glass or spider fitting
  work appears in the corpus at all.
- Only one project involved renovation of an existing building, so the existing-conditions block,
  the historic window profiles and the salvage-and-reinstall operations rest on that project alone.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is in the corpus, so nothing here shows which exclusions
  survived negotiation, whether the perimeter joint was ever resolved, or who actually ended up with
  the mirrors.
- No change order history is in the corpus. The claim that the tolerance and sequencing
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- No shop drawing, submittal or delegated design package is in the corpus, so the file cannot say
  what the engineering scope actually costs to produce or where it typically fails review.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the general contractor issued a glazing scope sheet and
  every bidder answered it row by row. The one fully answered sheet was answered by two bidders out
  of eleven rows; the rest were answered at base-bid level. What a properly answered glazing scope
  sheet looks like is therefore inferred from what the estimator asked for rather than from what the
  market returned.
- Bid-form breakouts asking for the package split by building and by amenity were left empty by every
  bidder on the project that asked for them. Whether that segmentation is unobtainable or simply was
  not enforced is unknown.
- Tariff exposure appears as a live and explicitly priced line in this corpus, but the corpus covers
  a narrow window of time. The rates and the surcharge conventions should be expected to move, and
  the file deliberately states them as proportions rather than as amounts for that reason.
