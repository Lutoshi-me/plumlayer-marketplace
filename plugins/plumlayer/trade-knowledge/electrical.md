# Electrical

Trade knowledge for an agent about to read a drawing set and produce scope for the electrical package.
Everything here is learned from real subcontractor proposals and estimator leveling sheets. This file
speaks in calibrated plain language: how common a behavior is, is carried by the words themselves.
Where real proposals have not shown something, this file says so.

Read this before reading the drawings. The point is to tell you what the sheets will not tell you, and
what shape the answer has to be in so that real electricians can bid it and a real estimator can level it.

One warning before anything else. Painting is a trade where the proposals say almost nothing.
Electrical is the opposite. The proposals are long, the exclusion lists run past forty items, and the
bidders will tell you their wiring methods, their conductor materials, their lead times and their
assumed labor market. The difficulty here is not scarcity of information. It is that two bidders can
describe what looks like the same scope and mean packages that differ by half the money, because in
this trade the question is rarely what work is included but who furnishes the material.

---

## 1. Trade identity and packaging

Division 26 is the anchor and the trade token is strong. Proposals name section 260000 and its
subsections when they name one at all, which they usually do. Older documents and older bidders still
cite the pre-2004 division number for electrical, sometimes for both the drawings and the
specifications on a current project, so a division number alone does not date a document.

The package as bought in the market is almost never Division 26 alone. The default buy is a turnkey
package covering Division 26 power and lighting, Division 27 communications, and Division 28
electronic safety and security, all from the electrician. A general contractor will say this outright:
the usual practice is to buy a turnkey package with the electrician for all low voltage and security.
Expect the base electrical bidder to be pricing the fire alarm, the tel/data, the access control, the
cameras and the intercom, and expect specialists to be bidding those same scopes directly alongside
them.

Collapse attractors, meaning scope the market pulls into this package even though it sits in another
division or would support its own contractor:

- Fire alarm, in full, including devices, panel, programming, testing and certification. Carried by
  nearly every full-scope bidder.
- Tel/data and structured cabling, meaning the backbone, the risers, the in-unit outlets and the head
  end. Commonly carried, and commonly carved back out as a separately priced alternate.
- Access control, video intercom and CCTV. Usually carried, though the commercial shape varies more
  than any other block in this trade.
- Emergency responder radio systems. Carried by most, and note that the drawings may call for
  separate systems for fire, police and emergency medical, which bidders will split and answer
  differently band by band.
- Area of rescue and elevator two-way communication. Nearly always in this package.
- Lightning protection. Carried more often than not, but a specialist bids it directly on some
  projects, so read it as detachable.
- Temporary power and temporary lighting for the whole site. This is general contractor facing scope
  living inside a trade bid, and it is the single most commonly detached block in the trade.
- Firestopping and coring for the electrician's own penetrations. Usually carried; third party
  firestopping usually not.
- Generator, transfer switches and the generator enclosure. Usually in the base, and increasingly
  pulled out as a direct owner or general contractor buy.
- Conduit and pathway provisions for future solar and future electric vehicle charging. Routinely
  carried as empty raceway even where the systems themselves are excluded.

Specialty carve-outs, meaning scope that leaves this package even when it looks like electrical work:

- Low voltage and structured cabling specialists, who bid telecom, security and audio visual as their
  own three-part proposal and compete head to head with the electrician's turnkey number for the same
  scope.
- Security integrators, who furnish the equipment while somebody else pulls the cable and somebody
  else again provides the pathway.
- Generator and switchgear suppliers, quoting equipment directly to the general contractor so it can
  be released early against a long lead time.
- Electric vehicle charging specialists, offering turnkey installation, and charger vendors offering
  hardware plus a recurring network subscription.
- Fire alarm work on an existing panel, which the incumbent panel manufacturer controls. Bidders
  either carry that manufacturer's programming cost by name or exclude it outright, and both happen on
  the same project.
- Temporary site security and surveillance, which is filed near this trade but is a separate rental
  service and not electrical scope at all.
- Demolition, make safe and temporary power on a renovation, frequently bid as its own enabling
  package by bidders who are not bidding the base.

## 2. How the market bids

The default output is a multi-page letter carrying a scope narrative, a wiring-method block, a long
exclusion list, and one number. Segmentation into a handful of numbers by system family is nearly as
common. Where a general contractor issues a scope sheet, the sheet becomes the proposal.

- Single lump sum with a scope narrative: common, and the shortest examples are two pages with a
  number and a dozen bullets.
- Segmented into a small number of system-family lines, typically power, telecom and security, with a
  total: common, and the usual shape on larger projects.
- A full breakout of ten or more lines by system, each tagged with its own furnish and install
  responsibility: seen, and worth reading closely because that tagging is where the money hides.
- The general contractor's scope sheet answered row by row and returned as the proposal: common
  wherever a sheet is issued, and on some projects the answered sheet arrives with no covering letter
  at all.
- A post-bid comeback list, meaning a formal question list from the general contractor and owner
  answered inline: seen on the larger projects, and it is where the design conflicts surface.

Measured quantities with unit prices are almost never present for the whole package. Counted
quantities without prices are common and are the more useful signal: bidders count card readers,
cameras, intercoms, unit locks, media enclosures, data ports by port count, light poles, bollards,
charging pedestals and fobs, then price them as one number. Read the counts, not the absence of unit
prices.

What a proposal typically will state:

- The wiring method, by location, as a lettered or bulleted block. Concealed common-area branch in
  metal-clad cable with conduit where exposed, non-metallic cable in apartments, conduit with steel
  set screw fittings in the garage, fire alarm rated cable concealed with conduit exposed, plenum
  cable free-air for security and intercom. This block is nearly universal on full-scope bids and it
  is the closest thing this trade has to a specification of its own work.
- The conductor material. Aluminum feeders and aluminum service conductors wherever code permits are
  the market default, with copper branch wiring in units. Bidders state this because it is the largest
  material decision they make.
- The basis document with a date, and usually a drawing range by sheet number. Naming the
  specification section is commonplace.
- Sales tax treatment, often with a taxable and not-taxable checkbox and the state exemption form
  numbers printed on the signature page.
- Lead times for the long lead equipment, in weeks after approved submittals. This is first-class
  content in this trade, not a courtesy.
- The permit basis, either a not-to-exceed allowance or a formula expressed as a fixed amount per
  thousand dollars of electrical cost.
- Labor market. Some bidders state union or open shop outright; an estimator records it for every
  bidder regardless, so treat it as a first-class comparison field even when the proposal omits it.
- A price hold, thirty days being the default. Fourteen and twenty days are seen on enabling and
  specialty scopes, sixty days after a negotiation.

What a proposal typically will not state:

- Measured quantities with unit prices for the package as a whole.
- Labor hours. Asked on every leveling sheet seen and answered by very few.
- Whether it is carrying the fixture package as material or as labor only. This is the most consequential
  omission in the trade and it frequently has to be extracted by asking.
- Crew size and schedule duration, unless the scope sheet asks.
- Bond cost, which is usually offered as a separate add.
- Warranty term beyond the one year almost everyone offers by default, even where two years is
  specified.
- Escalation, unless the bidder has decided to make it a condition, and then it is stated as a rate
  per month until release.

Exclusion behavior does not split this market the way it splits some trades. Nearly every full-scope
proposal carries a long exclusion list, commonly twenty to forty items and sometimes past forty. A
short list is not generosity, it is a short proposal, and the shortest bids seen are two pages with no
exclusions and no drawing numbers. Those bids need the largest plugs.

Revision habits, and this trade revises more than almost any other:

- Four and five rounds from a single bidder on a single project over a month or two is normal, not
  exceptional.
- Most rounds change price only, with the scope text byte-identical to the round before. Movements are
  large. A ladder from an opening budget down by roughly a quarter across four rounds with the scope
  text unchanged after the first revision is a real and unremarkable pattern.
- A revision may move a whole block out of the base and re-present it as an alternate. When enabling
  work leaves the base, the base falls, and that fall is not a price concession. Read the inclusion
  list of both rounds before computing a delta.
- A revision may move the opposite way, absorbing alternate scope back into a rising base. The
  estimator's plug falls at the same time, so the comparable total can rise while the headline base
  looks worse.
- A revision may be triggered by the general contractor issuing an enumerated scope sheet or a
  comeback list, and answering it is the revision.
- A negative line labelled union concession or market recovery may appear, disappear, or be renamed
  between rounds while carrying the same value. It is a negotiated labor credit, not scope.
- Revision labels are unreliable in both directions. A file labelled as one round may contain an
  attachment labelled as another. A document may carry a year typo in its own date. The most reliable
  revision identity seen is a proposal that carries its own dated revision history as bullets, which
  some bidders do and which beats the filename, the folder and any manifest.

Two consequences for a reader. A revision in this trade is usually a negotiation artifact, so do not
assume a later round reflects an addendum. And a proposal is often not one file: the bid, the answered
scope sheet, the comeback answers and the scope of work may sit in three or four files under three
spellings of the same name, and a price movement may exist only as a sentence in an email body.

## 3. Scope grain rules

Electrical scope sheets have run 78, 99, 168 and 421 rows. Target roughly one hundred to two hundred
scope lines for a full multifamily electrical package, and expect to go higher where the project has
an existing building, an occupied tenant, or a jurisdiction with its own compliance requirements. The
78-row sheet is the design development case where there is not yet enough drawn scope to plug against.
The 421-row sheet has grown past scope into a general subcontract questionnaire, and while more than
half its rows are not scope at all, each of those rows is a commercial fact that changes what the
number is worth.

### What enumerates

Give each of these its own line.

- System family, as the primary organizing axis: service and distribution, branch power and devices,
  lighting, lighting controls, fire alarm, emergency responder radio, area of rescue, tel/data,
  access control, video intercom, CCTV, audio visual, generator, temporary work, site work. This is
  the axis every estimator sheet and most proposals use.
- Wiring method, by location. Units, common areas, garage, exposed and concealed each attract a
  different method, and a proposal that names them is telling you its cost basis. A single line saying
  branch wiring will not distinguish a bidder pricing non-metallic cable from one pricing conduit.
- Conductor material, as its own row, asked as a question. Aluminum or copper for feeders, and copper
  or otherwise for unit branch circuits. Bidders on the same set answer this differently, and it is
  the largest single material divergence seen in this trade. Where the feeder schedule shows both
  materials the row is not optional.
- Each responder band of the emergency radio system separately: fire, police and emergency medical.
  Bidders carry one band and exclude the others routinely, and a single line will hide it.
- Each life safety subsystem separately: smoke control, stair pressurization, voice evacuation, area
  of rescue, elevator two-way communication, pre-action and aspirating detection wiring, interlocks
  from mechanical equipment, elevator smoke guard power. Every estimator sheet breaks these out.
- Lighting fixtures separately from lighting controls, and both separately from the installation of
  either. Decorative and architectural fixtures get their own row again, because they are the ones
  most likely to be owner furnished.
- The temporary block, fully broken out: temporary power, temporary lighting, temporary fire alarm,
  power for temporary heaters, power for demolition, abatement and welding, cut cap and make safe,
  the drop for demolition, power for the personnel and material hoist, temporary lighting at
  pedestrian protection, keeping an existing tenant operational, and work required by the site fire
  safety plan. Each of these is answered differently by different bidders on the same project. This
  block alone can run ten rows and is where the largest plugs live.
- Utility company scope, broken by who does what: primary conduits, primary conductors, manholes,
  vaults, transformers, transformer pads, switch pads, transclosures, core holes into the building,
  perimeter ground bussing, and the utility's own back charges. Bidders draw this line in different
  places and the utility does not read your scope sheet.
- Telecommunications and cable provider scope, separately from the electrical utility, for the same
  reason.
- The generator as several rows, not one: the generator, the specified enclosure, the pad or curb,
  vibration isolators, transfer switches and docking station, the fuel or day tank, rigging or crane
  to its final location, and the feeders to it. The enclosure alone can approach the cost of the
  generator, and rigging is excluded by roughly half of bidders.
- Low voltage broken by segment: into the building, main frame to intermediate frame, intermediate
  frame to units, inside units, common areas, wireless access points, and any armored backbone run.
  Bidders carry some segments and not others, and the pattern is not predictable from the drawings.
- Appliance and equipment hookup as its own block, phrased as questions. Whether appliances arrive
  with cords attached, whether any are hardwired, whether hardwire and plug price differently, and who
  installs the microwave and the hood. See section 5.
- Power studies, arc flash, coordination and labeling, separately from third party testing, separately
  again from commissioning.
- Firestopping, coring, sleeves, access panels and putty pads, each on its own line, with furnish and
  install stated separately for access panels.
- Permits, utility back charges, payment portal fees, insurance basis and bond, each as its own row.
- Lead times and manpower as rows that ask for weeks and hours, not dollars.
- Value engineering ideas and requested breakouts as their own block, because both are how this trade
  negotiates.
- Labor market, as an explicit row, and any negotiated labor credit alongside it.
- On a renovation, the existing-conditions block: reconfiguring the existing service while keeping a
  tenant live, cutting over an existing meter, reworking existing wiring passing through the work
  area, troubleshooting an existing fire alarm system, snaking wire in existing walls, and
  disconnecting and removing the old system. Six or more rows, each answered differently.

### What collapses

Do not split these.

- Individual receptacles, switches, boxes, circuits and homeruns collapse into a devices line per
  area. No document seen prices a receptacle.
- Conduit, fittings, supports, hangers and sleeves collapse into the work they serve. Even a bidder
  who states its wiring method does not price the conduit separately.
- Per-fixture enumeration collapses into a fixture package. Bidders count fixture types when they
  count anything, not fixtures one by one, and even a counted proposal prices the package as a block.
- Room-by-room enumeration collapses to area type: units, corridors, amenity, back of house, garage,
  site.
- Levels collapse into a level range unless the scope actually changes at a level, which it does at
  grade, at the mechanical level and at the roof.
- Panel-by-panel and circuit-by-circuit enumeration collapses into the distribution line. Where panel
  schedules are incomplete, that is a document problem to flag, not a reason to enumerate.

### The include-or-exclude click test for this trade

A candidate scope line earns its own row if a reasonable bidder could carry it, exclude it, or be
silent about it, and the three outcomes would produce meaningfully different numbers.

In this trade the test needs a second question that other trades do not need. Ask not only whether a
bidder could exclude the work, but whether a bidder could carry the labor and not the material. If the
answer to the second question is yes, the line must state furnish and install responsibility
explicitly, or it will be answered yes by two bidders who mean entirely different things. This applies
hardest to the fixture package, the service gear, the generator, the security equipment, the network
equipment, the appliances and every piece of mechanical equipment.

The test also tells you when silence is a finding. Where some bidders carry a scope and others exclude
it, a proposal that says nothing about it is a gap to surface, not an inclusion to assume. And watch
for the third state: a scope sheet returned with a line priced at zero and a not-included note beside
it reads as included to anyone skimming the column. Zero is not a price.

### The structural gap list

These are the scope families the market systematically does not carry, which an estimator adds to
every number before comparing. A scope line set that names all of them is doing the plugging in
advance.

1. The lighting fixture and controls package, where the bidder installs but does not furnish.
2. Temporary power, temporary lighting and temporary fire alarm, in every combination.
3. Cut, cap and make safe, and the demolition labor behind it.
4. Utility company back charges, and telephone and cable provider back charges.
5. Concrete of every kind: housekeeping pads, pole bases, transformer and equipment pads, duct bank
   and primary conduit encasement, and the curb at busway penetrations.
6. Excavation, trenching and backfill for the electrician's own conduit.
7. Cutting, patching, painting and roof penetrations.
8. Access panels, or the furnish half of them.
9. Lightning protection.
10. The emergency responder radio system, or the bands of it nobody carried.
11. Third party testing, third party firestopping and independent or infrared testing.
12. Crane and rigging for the generator to its final location.
13. Bond, and the general liability differential where no owner-controlled insurance program applies.
14. The warranty term differential where two years is specified and one year is offered.
15. Payment portal and project management system fees.
16. Wireless access points, network switches and other active network equipment.
17. Motor starters, variable speed drives, control wiring and equipment disconnects.
18. Attic stock, spare parts and extra materials.

no other package carries it, the exclusion is a gap.

### Boilerplate versus project signal

Most of the table above is boilerplate. A repeat bidder reproduces its exclusion list verbatim across
projects and across its own revision rounds, and one bidder's forty-one item list was byte-identical
across four rounds spanning four months. Repeat bidders make up a good share of any electrical bid
list. Read boilerplate for the trade map, not for the project.

Be aware that boilerplate travels between companies as well as within them. Warranty, change order and
consent blocks have been seen word for word identical in the proposals of two unrelated bidders on
different projects, and one bidder's confidentiality clause names a competitor outright. Proposal
language is not a company fingerprint.

What is project signal, because it is written for this set and appears nowhere else:

- An exclusion pointing at a specific sheet and note. When a bidder excludes a conduit run because one
  note on a security sheet requires conduit and the next note contemplates plenum cable without it,
  that bidder read the drawings and found a contradiction. That is an RFI, not a bidder problem.
- An exclusion justified by an unanswered request for information. A bidder excluding all work
  associated with named drawing subscripts because its question was never answered is telling you the
  scope is undefined, and every other bidder either priced it blind or excluded it silently.
- A disclosure that a schedule is incomplete. Panel schedules not complete and breaker fill assumed,
  or load centers not defined with the counts assumed, both appear on the same project from a
  contractor and from a supplier. That is a document gap that will land as a change order.
- A disclosure that two documents disagree. Surge devices included per the panel schedule and not per
  the one-line diagram. Fixture counts taken from the electrical drawings and explicitly not from the
  reflected ceiling plans, on a set where the two disagree. A feeder schedule showing both aluminum
  and copper.
- A disclosure that the drawings do not meet a requirement. A specified sound limit at the generator
  that no manufacturer's standard enclosure achieves. Line-voltage smoke detectors shown where code
  requires networked detectors with low frequency sounder bases. A riser that appears mislabelled,
  with a length allowance carried instead of what is drawn.
- An assumption borrowed from another discipline's drawings, such as pricing all units as having
  suspended gypsum ceilings because the reflected ceiling plans were not clear.
- A capacity warning on existing infrastructure, such as a note that the existing service may not
  support the temporary load and that testing is required before anyone can be sure.

### Qualifications that are really pricing bases

These are conditions attached to the number. Violating one converts the lump sum into a change order.

- The wiring method block. If the project will not permit non-metallic cable in units or free-air
  plenum cable in ceilings, the number is wrong. This is stated by nearly every full-scope bidder and
  it is the most load-bearing qualification in the trade.
- Conductor material. Aluminum feeders and secondaries wherever code permits is the stated basis of
  most competitive numbers, and a copper answer on the same set is a materially different bid.
- Breaker and wire sizing. Some bidders price light gauge cable and small breakers for lighting and
  convenience circuits, which is also a common value engineering offer, so it can be a basis in one
  bid and a savings idea in another.
- Labor market. Union or open shop, and separately whether prevailing wage applies. Bidders state
  prevailing wage almost always as a negative, that the price does not include it.
- Insurance basis. Whether an owner-controlled program applies, with a conventional general liability
  add priced if it does not.
- Warranty term, and whether it starts at final inspection or at substantial completion. One year from
  the day after final inspection is the market default; two years from substantial completion is a
  common specification requirement, and the difference is priced as an alternate when anyone asks.
- Normal working hours, with off hours, overtime and shift work chargeable.
- A mobilization period between award and site start, commonly two weeks.
- Price hold, thirty days by default. Some bidders go further and hold packages only for the calendar
  month quoted, with copper and other commodities on daily rates and a stated right to reprice.
- Escalation as a stated rate. Half a percent per month until material release is one seen convention.
- Tariffs excluded outright, or a stated right to review pricing at award.
- Prepayment conditions. Long lead items required to be prepaid before order placement appears on some
  proposals, which is a cash flow term rather than a scope term but changes what the number is worth.
- Site logistics assumptions: an onsite crane available for the generator, a material and personnel
  hoist available, free onsite parking and space for the bidder's own storage trailers, temporary
  power available at the property line at no cost to the subcontractor.
- Alternates valid only through the rough phase, after which unaccepted alternates are repriced.
- Contract terms attached as a commercial addendum. Interest on late payment, arbitration forum
  selection, attorney fee recovery, disclaimers of consequential damages, a requirement of written
  consent before any back charge, and clauses forbidding removal of any awarded scope without an
  agreed adjustment. These are common enough in this trade to be worth reading, and a bidder who
  attaches them is offering a different deal at the same price.

## 5. Furnish and install seams, and adjacent-trade overlaps

This is the section that matters most in this trade, because the dominant leveling error here is not
missing scope. It is two bidders answering yes to the same row while one furnishes the material and
the other does not.

### The package mode problem

A single proposal will carry different furnish and install responsibilities for different work
families inside it. One bid seen carried install only on the service and distribution, furnish only on
the branch wiring and devices, furnish only on the lighting package, furnish and install on the fire
alarm, install only on the generator, and put the service gear package in its alternates. Its base
came in lowest on cost per unit of any bidder on the project, and the estimator had to add roughly
forty percent of that base back to make it comparable.

So a scope line that says lighting is not a scope line. It has to say who buys the fixtures.

### The lighting fixture package

The most consequential seam in the trade, because the fixture package can run close to a fifth of the
whole electrical number and it moves in and out of the base without changing a word of the scope
description.

Four shapes appear, and the drawings will not tell you which one applies:

1. The electrician furnishes and installs the complete package including controls. Common, and the
   bidder may disclose it as a breakout inside its own base.
2. The package is ordered, paid for and delivered to site by others, and the electrician installs it.
   Common. When this applies, several scope lines appear that exist in no other configuration: receive
   and inventory the delivery, store it in the electrician's own supplied trailers, report damaged
   items and count discrepancies promptly, and accept that the supplier carries warranty and
   replacement. Those belong on the leveling sheet as their own rows.
3. Decorative and architectural fixtures are owner furnished while the rest of the package is not.
   Common, and it is usually the decorative marks that leave.
4. The electrician furnishes the package as material only while somebody else installs it. Seen.

Watch for two further traps. Emergency inverters, emergency drivers and fire rated housings ride with
the fixture package and get excluded with it, so they can fall out even when someone carries the
fixtures. And where owner supplied fixtures are late to site, replacing temporary fixtures and blank
plates is billed hourly by at least one bidder, which is a schedule risk priced as a rate.

### The service gear and the generator

Both are long lead, both are large, and both are increasingly bought direct. A general contractor will
ask bidders to price them as separable breakouts specifically so it can release them early against a
lead time, and once it does, the base electrician is install only on that equipment.

The generator is really six seams, not one. The set, the enclosure, the pad or curb, the transfer
switches, the fuel or day tank, and the rigging to its final position are each carried or excluded
independently. The enclosure is the one to watch: a specified walk-in sound enclosure can approach the
cost of the generator itself and carry a lead time several times longer, while a factory enclosure
costs a fraction and may not meet the specified sound limit. Where a specification names a sound limit
at a stated distance, check it against what standard enclosures actually achieve, because the standard
product may be several decibels over and neither the drawings nor the bidders will volunteer that
until somebody asks.

Buying versus renting a generator is a real decision on a project with a long enabling phase. A year of
rental has evaluated at roughly a seventh of the purchase cost of an equivalent unit, and an estimator
may model it on its own tab with a rental term, a tax toggle and per-vendor inclusions.

### Low voltage: a three-party seam, not a two-party one

Access control and cameras routinely split three ways. An integrator furnishes the equipment, a low
voltage subcontractor pulls and terminates the cable and consumables, and the electrician provides the
pathways and the line voltage. No single proposal describes all three. A bidder saying it installs the
access control system may mean it is doing the middle third only.

The same shape appears on the network side. An owner-side network manager may furnish the wireless
access points and the active network equipment, with the low voltage division installing the access
points, racking the switches, and terminating and testing the cabling, while the network manager
configures. Where this applies, the low voltage bid is labor and cable against somebody else's
hardware.

And the internet service provider is a party too. Unit homeruns, media termination panels, fiber to
the intermediate frames and microduct may all be furnished and installed by the provider by default,
with the electrician holding priced alternates in case the provider does not. Whether the provider is
doing it is not knowable from the drawings and is often still undetermined at bid time.

### Apartment unit locks and door hardware

Contested on every project that has them, and the counts are large because there is one per dwelling
unit. Three positions have been seen on the same scope: an electrician carrying the locks as furnished
by others but programmed by itself, an electrician excluding unit smart entry locks outright, and an
electrician pricing the locks as its own supply and being told by the general contractor to omit them
because the door, frame and hardware vendor provides them. That last correction moved a security
package to roughly two and a half times its original value before the locks came back out.

Electric strikes, mag locks, power transfers, request-to-exit devices and door position contacts split
the same way. The common electrician position is a junction box above the door, flex to the hardware,
and cable back to the head end, with the hardware itself by others. Put the hardware and the wiring on
separate rows or you will get a yes that means half the work.

### Appliances: the wall end of a seam the appliance trade also records

The appliance suppliers stop at the appliance end and warn that quoted ranges may trip protective
breakers on installation. Here is the wall end of the same seam, and it lines up.

- The electrician power wires the disposal, dishwasher, refrigerator, microwave, hood, range, washer,
  dryer and any accessible wall oven and cooktop, with the appliances furnished and installed by
  others.
- The electrician assumes the appliances arrive with cords or pigtails already attached, and excludes
  furnishing cords, pigtails and anti-tip devices.
- The exception that recurs is the accessible wall oven, which is hardwired where the rest are plug
  and cord.
- Installation of the microwave itself is excluded by some, which leaves it between the electrician,
  the appliance installer and the casework installer.
- At least one bidder writes a back charge disclaimer specifically anticipating that an installed
  appliance may trip a breaker, and refuses the charge without prior written consent.

So the pigtail is the gap. The appliance supplier stops before it, the electrician assumes it is
already there, and nobody has priced it. A general contractor scope sheet that asks whether appliances
come with cords attached, whether hardwire and plug price differently, and who installs the microwave
is asking exactly the right three questions, and it is worth asking them every time.

### Mechanical and plumbing equipment

- Power wiring and disconnects to mechanical and plumbing equipment are carried. Motor starters,
  variable speed drives, control relays, thermostats and energy management devices are furnished by
  the trade supplying the equipment.
- Control wiring and temperature control wiring are excluded regardless of voltage, and plumbing
  control wiring with them. This is near universal and it means low voltage inside a mechanical unit
  belongs to the mechanical contractor even though it is wire.
- Equipment disconnects are the exception that bites: where the mechanical equipment schedule says a
  disconnect arrives with the equipment, the electrician excludes both furnishing and installing it,
  so the schedule note has to be read.
- Loose control panels and drives furnished by the mechanical and plumbing trades are installed by the
  electrician, and a scope sheet may ask the electrician to confirm it received their list. The
  electrician is pricing from another trade's list, not from the drawings.
- Heat trace splits cleanly: the plumber furnishes and installs the cable and insulation, the
  electrician provides power. Some projects buy a turnkey heat trace system from the electrician
  instead, so ask.
- Fire pump power is carried; the fire pump controller is furnished by others and wired by the
  electrician.
- Aspirating detection and pre-action piping belong to the fire protection contractor, while the
  electrician provides the modules and the fire alarm connections.
- The elevator seam is smaller but real: cab and machine room power and lighting are carried, the
  transformer for the elevator is usually the elevator contractor's, and elevator company back charges
  are excluded.

### Utility company and site

The utility is a party that does not negotiate. Bidders draw the line differently and all of them
exclude what is beyond it, so read who carries the primary conduits to the property line, who cores
the building, who supplies and terminates the primary conductors, who provides transformers, pads,
switch pads, vaults, transclosures and manholes, and who pays the utility's back charges. A
specification may name an allowance for utility back charges and bidders may carry exactly that
figure, exclude it, or ignore the row entirely.

Everything concrete on the site is somebody else's: pole bases, housekeeping pads, transformer pads,
bollard bases, duct bank encasement and the curb at busway floor penetrations. Whether secondary
service conductors need concrete encasement is a question worth resolving, because the credit for
deleting it comes from the site contractor and not from the electrician.

### Temporary power, demolition and make safe

Treat this as its own package with its own bid list. It is bid standalone by contractors who are not
bidding the base, its boundaries move, and the resulting numbers are not comparable without reading
them.

On one enabling scope, one bidder carried a temporary fire alarm allowance and no demolition labor,
another carried demolition labor and no temporary fire alarm, and a third gave temporary power and
temporary fire alarm as two email sentences with the demolition quoted in labor hours. Their totals
differed by roughly two and a half times and none of them was wrong.

What recurs inside it: make safe is circuit trace, cut, make safe and drop to the floor, with removal
from the building by others. Fire alarm devices are coiled up rather than removed. A temporary fire
alarm system is contingent on an approved impairment plan and is deliberately independent of the live
system, and the cost of daily disabling and enabling the live system is excluded by everyone seen.
Additional power for a specific trade's equipment, welders in particular, is that trade's cost, and
any temporary circuit above a small stated rating gets billed to whoever asked for it. Where an
existing tenant stays live through construction, keeping them operational is its own row and bidders
answer it both ways.

## 6. What the drawings will not tell you

What electricians actually price from, in descending order of what they cite:

1. The electrical drawings themselves, by sheet range: the one-line and riser diagrams, the panel
   schedules, the feeder schedules, the power plans and the site plans. Nearly every proposal names
   the range and the date.
2. The fire alarm, security and telecom drawing sets, cited as their own ranges. Device counts come
   from here, and a bidder that counts anything counts these.
3. The specification, mostly for the power system study, the acceptable manufacturers, the sound
   requirements, the scheduling procedures and the warranty term.
4. The mechanical and plumbing equipment schedules, for connected loads and for which equipment
   arrives with its own disconnect.
5. The architectural drawings, and this is where it goes wrong. Fixture counts appear on both the
   electrical drawings and the reflected ceiling plans, and where the two disagree a bidder may count
   one and say so. Reflected ceiling plans also drive assumptions about ceiling construction that the
   electrical set does not carry.
6. A system responsibility matrix, where the set has one. This is the rare case where the seam is
   drawn, and it is worth looking for before assuming it is not.
7. The dwelling unit count, which is the leveling currency of the trade and often has to be inferred.

Not deducible from any drawing, and each of them moves the number:

- Union versus open shop. The largest single driver. It is tracked on every bidder in every leveling
  seen, and it is a project attribute more than a bidder attribute: one project's bid list can be
  almost entirely union while another in the same regional market is almost entirely open shop.
- Any negotiated labor concession or market recovery credit, which can be worth an eighth of the
  electrical number and appears as a line that is not scope.
- Prevailing wage applicability.
- Whether an owner-controlled insurance program applies.
- Jurisdiction workforce requirements, such as residency, minority and women participation goals
  expressed as percentages of hours, and any business diversity certification requirement.
- Whether the general contractor or owner will buy the service gear and the generator direct.
- Whether the lighting fixture package is owner or general contractor furnished, and whether the
  decorative fixtures are treated differently from the rest.
- What the internet service provider will and will not furnish, which is frequently still undetermined
  at bid time.
- Whether an owner-side network manager exists and what it furnishes.
- Long lead times, which are the schedule risk of this trade. Switchboards and busway have been quoted
  at thirty to thirty-eight weeks, generators from twelve to forty-two weeks depending on the
  enclosure, transfer switches at fourteen to eighteen weeks, group metering at six to ten weeks,
  enclosed breakers at twelve weeks and panels at two to four weeks, all measured after approved
  submittals rather than after award. The submittal period is on top.
- The permit fee basis. Quoted as a not-to-exceed allowance or as a formula per thousand dollars of
  electrical cost, and excluded outright by some.
- Utility company back charges, and whether the specification names an allowance for them.
- Payment portal and project management system fees, quoted as a fraction of a percent of contract
  value plus a flat charge per sub-tier subcontractor.
- Bond cost and the general liability differential.
- The required warranty term, and whether it runs from final inspection or substantial completion.
- Site power availability. What service the site can actually get, at what voltage and amperage, and
  where it comes from. On a renovation, whether the existing service can carry the temporary load at
  all, which may require testing before anyone can answer.
- Whether a crane and a material and personnel hoist will be available, and who pays for the picks.
- Whether an existing tenant or occupant stays live, and what that costs in off hours cutovers.
- Whether an impairment plan will be approved, which gates the temporary fire alarm.
- The permit issue date, where a code change turns on it. Line voltage detectors shown on a set may
  become networked detectors interconnected with the building system if the permit issues after a
  threshold date, and that is invisible on the drawings.
- Tariff and commodity exposure, which most bidders now exclude by name.
- Whether the project is taxable, which bidders will ask with a checkbox and exemption forms.

Set-stage behavior. At design development, bidders price system families as prose bullets with no
counts, carry the fixture package as a stated purchase price rather than a counted schedule, and take
allowances for anything undefined. Treat a design development fixture line as an allowance carry. At
permit and construction documents the same bidders switch to citing drawing ranges and device counts.
The most useful thing a design-stage bid can carry is an explicit line for the cost of scope that will
appear once the drawings are finished, which one bidder priced at roughly a tenth of its own bid, and
which a thorough scope sheet will ask every bidder to provide as a design completion list.

Finally, be sceptical of the set a bid is priced against. A bidder may submit into a conformed-set
round while still pricing the original design documents from months earlier and disclosing it in a
single line. Two documents on the same project may name two different conformed-set dates, and a third
may name a third. A cover page and a body paragraph inside one proposal may cite different sets. A
scope sheet row that simply asks the bidder to confirm the current set is doing real work, and the
answers to it are worth reading before the prices are.

## 7. Quantity and pricing conventions

Units the trade thinks in:

- Cost per dwelling unit, which is the normalizing denominator for the whole package on every
  estimator sheet seen and the basis on which bids are ranked. If the set does not make the unit count
  unambiguous, every bid becomes incomparable.
- Each, for counted devices: card readers, fobs, cameras by interior and exterior, intercom stations,
  unit locks, media enclosures, data ports counted by port, wireless access point locations, light
  poles, bollards, charging pedestals, transfer switches, services by amperage, and temporary panels
  and stands. This is the trade's real enumeration unit and it is present far more often than prices
  are.
- Labor hours, for the package as a whole. Asked on every leveling sheet and answered by few; an
  install-only bid of a few million has been stated at roughly eighteen to twenty thousand hours.
  Where a bidder answers it, it is the best cross-check available on whether its scope matches its
  price.
- Hours, for demolition and make safe on renovation work, sometimes quoted without any dollar value at
  all.
- Linear feet, for feeder allowances, cable runs carried against an uncertain riser, and rental
  generator cabling. Occasional and always specific.
- Kilowatts and kilovolt-amperes for the generator, amperes for services, panels and load centers, and
  volts and phases for temporary services. These are the identifying attributes of the equipment
  lines and belong on them.
- Weeks, for lead time, always measured after approved submittals.
- Decibels at a stated distance, for the generator enclosure.
- Months, for generator rental and for stated escalation rates.
- Percent of contract value, for bond, for general liability where no owner-controlled program
  applies, and for payment portal fees.
- Dollars per thousand dollars of electrical cost, for the electrical permit.
- Crew size, as a headcount range on site.

Cost drivers, ranked by how much movement they cause:

1. Package mode. Whether the bidder furnishes or only installs the fixture package, the service gear
   and the generator. This moves more money than any physical variable in the trade and it is the one
   the drawings cannot show.
2. Labor type, union versus open shop, and any negotiated labor credit alongside it.
3. Scope breadth, meaning whether the low voltage, security, fire alarm and audio visual blocks are
   inside the number at all.
4. Conductor material. Aluminum versus copper on feeders, secondaries and service conductors.
5. Total connected load and device count, driven by unit count and by how much amenity, garage and
   back of house area the project carries.
6. Wiring method. Non-metallic cable versus metal-clad cable versus conduit, by location, and whether
   the code and the specification permit the cheaper method where the bidder assumed it.
7. The generator, its enclosure and its rigging, as a block. On a project that needs one it is a
   material fraction of the package, and the enclosure alone can rival the set.
8. Whether the emergency responder radio system is carried, and how many bands. It routinely needs a
   design that does not exist at bid time, so early numbers are budget numbers.
9. The temporary and enabling package, especially on renovation, where it can exceed the value of some
   whole trades.
10. Existing conditions on renovation: reworking live systems, keeping an occupant operational,
    snaking wire in existing walls, and off hours cutovers.
11. Lead time exposure and whether long lead equipment can be released early.
12. Commercial terms: bond, insurance basis, warranty term, escalation, tariffs and payment portal
    fees.
13. Off hours, overtime, phasing and remobilization.

On dispersion, and no amounts are given here. Within a single project the spread between the lowest
and highest cost per unit among genuine full-scope bidders has run about 2.1 times on a renovation
high-rise, about 1.8 times on a mid-size new build with one much higher outlier, about 1.6 times on a
small design-stage project, and only about 1.2 times on a large new build where the bid list was deep
and the documents were complete. Read that pattern: the spread narrows as the documents firm up and
the market gets thicker, and a wide spread is usually telling you the scope is ambiguous rather than
that some bidders are expensive.

Bidder counts are large. A single project's electrical tab has carried more than thirty real bidder
columns, and fifteen is unremarkable. The general contractor's own budget is carried as a column in
the ranking, with a labor type of its own.

Two things about that ranking will mislead you if you take it at face value. First, sibling
low-voltage-only, security-only, fire-alarm-only, lightning-protection-only and charging-only quotes
sit in the same column as whole-building bids, at a tenth or less of the cost per unit. Ranking on the
column alone puts a cable quote at the top of the list. Second, the ranking carries its own
housekeeping: columns marked superseded, columns marked do-not-use, and columns annotated with the
estimator's own note that a calculation needs fixing. Those are not bids.

The convention that matters most: an estimator does not compare base bids. It compares base bid plus
adjustments, where adjustments are the plugs added to each bidder to cover what it did not carry. The
adjustment column runs in both directions. It has gone negative by a substantial amount where a bidder
over-carried scope nobody else was pricing, and positive by more than forty percent of a bidder's own
base where that bidder priced install-only against furnish-and-install competitors. Where a project's
plugs cluster at nearly the same value across most bidders, that is a gap the whole market shares
rather than any one bidder's omission, and it is the clearest signal available that the scope
documents left something out.

## 8. Coverage and gaps

This file is calibrated on roughly thirty electrical and electrical-adjacent proposals drawn from
four multifamily residential projects in one regional market, all bid by one general contractor, plus
the four matching estimator leveling sheets and a generator buy-versus-rent evaluation. The projects
span a renovation high-rise with an occupied commercial tenant, two ground-up new builds of different
sizes, and one small building still at design development. The proposals span design development,
permit, construction documents and conformed sets, and include several same-bidder revision sequences
of four and five rounds, one of which runs across two set stages under two different company names.
The bidder pool includes companies appearing on more than one project, which is what makes the
boilerplate-versus-project-signal distinction in section 4 testable. Sibling low voltage, security,
telecom, fire alarm, generator, switchgear and charging quotes were opened to establish the seams
without being absorbed into this package.

Open gaps, to be closed by later corpus rounds:

- Everything here comes from multifamily residential work through one general contractor in one
  market. Nothing is tested against commercial interiors, institutional, healthcare, industrial, data
  center or public prevailing-wage work, and healthcare and data center work would change the fire
  alarm, emergency power and grounding sections substantially.
- Only one project involved an existing building, so the entire existing-conditions and enabling-work
  discussion rests on that project alone, and it is the project with the widest bid spread.
- No signed subcontract or executed buyout is present, so nothing here shows which exclusions survived
  negotiation and which were bought out. The revision sequences show prices falling but never show
  what was finally agreed.
- No change order history is present. The claim that the wiring method, sequencing and appliance-cord
  qualifications convert into change orders is read out of the proposals' own language rather than
  observed in the field, so treat it as untested here.
- Only one proposal stated its labor hours, so the hours-per-project convention in section 7 rests on
  a single document.
- Unit-price schedules for the package as a whole are effectively absent, so section 7's quantity
  conventions describe how the trade counts rather than how it prices per unit of work.
- The corpus has not shown solar as a bid package. It appears only as empty conduit provisions and as
  a scope sheet row, so nothing here describes how a real photovoltaic package is bid, priced or
  seamed against the base electrician.
- The corpus has not shown a project where the electrician did not also bid the low voltage, so the
  case where the trades are genuinely separately procured from the outset is inferred from the
  specialists' own proposals rather than observed as a project strategy.
- The corpus has not shown battery storage, microgrid or generator paralleling work.
- The corpus has not shown how packaging changes on a project with no fire alarm scope, or with no
  generator, so the collapse attractors in section 1 are described from projects that all carried
  both.
- Emergency responder radio systems appear in the corpus mostly as budget numbers pending a design
  that did not exist at bid time, so no reliable pricing convention for them can be stated yet.
